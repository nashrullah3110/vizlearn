Projections

A projection keeps the component of a vector that lies in a chosen subspace and discards the rest.

Project onto a subspace

With orthonormal basis columns in Q, projection onto their span is QQᵀv. The residual is perpendicular to every column of Q. Least-squares fitting uses this geometry: it projects observed data onto the column space of a design matrix. QR decomposition makes this calculation stable without explicitly forming the projection matrix; see QR decomposition and orthogonality.

A projection is idempotent: projecting twice gives the same result as once. It is not generally a rotation or a scaling; components outside the selected subspace are removed.

Projection explains least-squares fitting

The projection is the closest point in the chosen line or subspace to v under Euclidean distance. For a line generated by a, the residual r=v−projₐ(v) is perpendicular to a. This perpendicularity characterises the closest point: moving a little along the line cannot reduce the squared distance. For a subspace with orthonormal basis Q, the projected vector is QQᵀv, and Qᵀr=0 expresses perpendicularity to every basis direction.

In least squares, a design matrix X has columns spanning all predictions the model can produce. The fitted vector Xβ is the projection of observed y onto that column space, so the residual y−Xβ is orthogonal to every column of X. This gives the normal equations XᵀXβ=Xᵀy. QR factorisation is generally a more stable way to solve the least-squares problem than explicitly forming XᵀX. Projection matrices are symmetric and idempotent (P²=P); they do not usually preserve the length of vectors because the discarded component shortens the result.

Projection is also a way to decompose a vector into explained and unexplained parts: v=proj(v)+r, with the two parts perpendicular. The squared lengths add, ‖v‖²=‖proj(v)‖²+‖r‖², by the Pythagorean theorem. In regression, the projection is the fitted response and the residual is what the model's column space cannot explain. A small residual does not establish that the model is appropriate; residual patterns can reveal curvature, unequal variance, or missing structure.

For a line generated by a, rescaling a does not change the projected vector: replacing a with ca changes both numerator and denominator by c², which cancels. This is a useful check that projection depends on the subspace, not the arbitrary length of its basis vector. For a non-orthonormal basis matrix, QQᵀ is not generally the projection; use A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ when columns are independent, or a QR/SVD method for better numerical stability.

Overview

Project onto a direction

For nonzero vector a, the projection of v onto a is the closest point to v on the line spanned by a. Its scalar coordinate along a is (a · v)/(a · a); multiplying by a gives the projected vector:

projₐ(v) = ((a · v)/(a · a))a

For a = (1, 2) and v = (3, 1), a · v = 5 and a · a = 5, so the projection is (1, 2). The residual v − projₐ(v) = (2, −1) is perpendicular to a: their dot product is zero. If a = 0, projection onto its direction is undefined.
