title: Understanding Edge Detection
intro: A deep explore how machines "see" outlines and shapes in images.

## Quick Context

Edge detection is a basic technique in <strong>computer vision</strong> and pic handling. Its purpose is to find points in a digital pic where the brightness changes sharply. These points are often organized into a set of curved line segments termed <em>edges</em>. This interactive uses the <strong>Sobel operator</strong>, a classic and fast algorithm for this task.

Edges matter since they carry most of the structural information in an pic. A photograph of a face with all except the edges removed is still recognisable. A photograph with just the smooth regions kept is not. Object edges, texture changes, shadows and depth discontinuities all give edges. And detecting them is often the first step in tasks like object recognition, lane detection, medical imaging and document scanning.

The math-based idea is straightforward: an edge is a region of high gradient. The gradient of an pic at a pixel is a vector pointing in the way of the steepest brightness change. And its magnitude says how steep that change is. A flat region has near-zero gradient. An edge has a large one. The Sobel operator estimates this gradient by convolving the pic with two small 3&times;3 kernels. One for the horizontal way and one for the vertical. Mixing the two gives the gradient magnitude and way at each pixel.

Other edge detectors improve on Sobel in various ways. The Canny detector adds non-max suppression (thinning edges to single-pixel width) and hysteresis thresholding (connecting weak edges to strong ones). It gives cleaner, more continuous contours. The Laplacian of Gaussian detects edges by finding zero-crossings in the second derivative. For deep learning pipelines, learned edge detectors like HED beat all of these. But the Sobel operator stays the standard teaching case since its mechanism is fully transparent.

## The Core Idea: Finding Abrupt Changes

Imagine walking across a flat, grey field that suddenly drops off into a black canyon. Your brain at once registers that change in elevation. Edge detection algorithms do something close with pixel vals. They "walk" across the pic and look for sudden jumps in brightness.

The Sobel operator reaches this by with two small matrices (called <strong>kernels</strong>), one to detect horizontal changes and one for vertical changes. By sliding these kernels over each pixel, it calculates the "gradient" or rate of change in brightness. A high gradient value means a strong edge is likely present.

## Edges are where the counts change fast

An edge is a place where brightness changes sharply. The edge between an object and its background. Or between two surfaces. Since an pic is a grid of counts, "changes sharply" means a large gap between neighbouring pixels. It is a derivative.

The simplest detector is the gap between a pixel's neighbours:

<p class="mono-font">gradient at x = pixel(x+1) &minus; pixel(x&minus;1)</p>

A flat region gives zero. A step from dark (50) to light (200) gives 150. The size of the count is the strength of the edge. The sign is its way.

Doing this in two dimensions gives two gradients. Horizontal and vertical &mdash; which mix into a magnitude and an angle:

<p class="mono-font">magnitude = &radic;(G&#8339;&sup2; + G&#8342;&sup2;)&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;way = arctan(G&#8342; / G&#8339;)</p>

## The classic kernels

<strong>Sobel</strong> is the standard, mixing differencing in one way with smoothing in the other:

```
Gx = [-1  0  +1]        Gy = [-1 -2 -1]
     [-2  0  +2]             [ 0  0  0]
     [-1  0  +1]             [+1 +2 +1]
```

`Gx` responds to vertical edges (where brightness changes horizontally) and `Gy` to horizontal ones. The 2s in the middle row weight the central pixels more. It suppresses noise slightly compared with a plain gap.

<strong>Prewitt</strong> is the same idea with uniform weights &mdash; simpler, marginally noisier. <strong>Scharr</strong> uses weights chosen for better rotational symmetry. And is the better choice when edge <em>angles</em> matter.

<strong>Laplacian</strong> takes the second derivative in both ways at once with a single kernel:

```
[ 0 -1  0]
[-1  4 -1]
[ 0 -1  0]
```

It finds edges as zero-crossings rather than peaks, needs no way mixing. And is much more sensitive to noise. Which is why it is almost always applied after a Gaussian blur (the Laplacian of Gaussian).

## Why blurring first is not optional

Differencing amplifies noise. A single speckled pixel gives a large gap with both its neighbours. So an edge detector run on a raw noisy photograph returns a field of false edges.

Smoothing with a Gaussian blur first removes the high-frequency noise while leaving genuine edges. It are less-frequency, largely intact. The blur radius sets the scale: a small sigma keeps fine texture and some noise, a large sigma keeps just major edges.

The <strong>Canny detector</strong> packages the whole sensible pipeline into one algorithm. And it is still the standard classical way:

1. <strong>Blur</strong> with a Gaussian.
2. <strong>Compute gradients</strong>, often with Sobel.
3. <strong>Non-max suppression</strong> &mdash; thin each ridge to a one-pixel line by keeping just local maxima along the gradient way.
4. <strong>Double thresholding</strong> &mdash; strong edges are kept, weak ones are candidates, the rest are discarded.
5. <strong>Hysteresis</strong> &mdash; keep a weak edge just if it connects to a strong one. It links broken contours without admitting isolated noise.

```python
import cv2
edges = cv2.Canny(gray, threshold1=100, threshold2=200)
```

The two thresholds are the params that matter. A common heuristic is a ratio between 2:1 and 3:1.

## Where this goes wrong

- <strong>Setting the threshold too low:</strong> This leads to a noisy output where each tiny texture is marked as an edge.
- <strong>Setting the threshold too high:</strong> This can cause you to miss key but subtle features in the pic.
- <strong>Forgetting preprocessing:</strong> In real-world applications, pics are often blurred slightly (e.g., with a Gaussian filter) before edge detection to reduce noise and get cleaner results. This interactive skips that step for simplicity.

## Building Canny one stage at a time

An edge is a place where brightness changes fast. So edge detection is differentiation on a grid. This builds the whole chain -- gap, Sobel, gradient way, non-max suppression, hysteresis -- and measures what each stage buys over the one before it.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
H, W = 14, 24
img = np.full((H, W), 60.0)
img[:, 13:] = 190.0                    # a strong vertical edge
img[7:, :13] += 26.0                   # a FAINT horizontal edge, touching it
img[11, 17] += 45.0                    # one isolated bright speck
img += rng.normal(0, 3.5, (H, W))
img = np.clip(img, 0, 255)

FLAT = (slice(9, 14), slice(3, 12))    # a genuinely featureless patch

def show(a, label, hi):
    ramp = " .:-=+*#%@"
    print("   %s" % label)
    for row in a:
        print("      " + "".join(
            ramp[min(9, int(9 * max(0.0, v) / (hi + 1e-9)))] for v in row))

show(img, "the image", 255)
print("      a strong vertical step at column 13 (60 -> 190), a faint")
print("      horizontal step at row 7 (60 -> 86) that runs into it, and one")
print("      isolated bright speck in the right-hand region. the faint step")
print("      is only about twice the noise, deliberately.")
print()
```

Building on the same setup, the next block shows the left cases.

```python-run
import numpy as np

print("STEP 1 -- the crudest derivative: the difference with the neighbour.")
dx = np.abs(np.diff(img, axis=1))
dy = np.abs(np.diff(img, axis=0))
strong_raw = dx[3, 12]
faint_raw = dy[6, 7]
noise_raw = max(dx[FLAT].max(), dy[FLAT].max())
print("      across the strong edge:  %.1f" % strong_raw)
print("      across the faint edge:   %.1f" % faint_raw)
print("      worst noise in the flat: %.1f" % noise_raw)
print("      the strong edge is %.1fx the noise floor and easy. the faint"
      % (strong_raw / noise_raw))
print("      edge is %.2fx it -- indistinguishable. a single-pixel"
      % (faint_raw / noise_raw))
print("      difference amplifies noise as eagerly as it finds edges,")
print("      because differentiation is a high-pass operation and noise is")
print("      entirely high frequency.")
print()

print("STEP 2 -- SOBEL. smooth ALONG the edge while differentiating ACROSS")
print("it. that is the only reason the kernel is 3x3 and not 1x2:")
KX = np.array([[-1., 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]])
KY = KX.T
print("      Kx = [-1 0 1]        Ky = [-1 -2 -1]")
print("           [-2 0 2]             [ 0  0  0]")
print("           [-1 0 1]             [ 1  2  1]")
print("      each is an outer product of [1 2 1] and [-1 0 1]. the [1 2 1]")
print("      half averages 3 pixels along the edge; the [-1 0 1] half")
print("      differentiates across it.")

def conv(a, k):
    p = np.pad(a, 1, mode="edge")
    return np.array([[float((p[i:i + 3, j:j + 3] * k).sum())
                      for j in range(a.shape[1])] for i in range(a.shape[0])])
```

The next block covers the left steps.

```python-run
import numpy as np

gx, gy = conv(img, KX), conv(img, KY)
mag = np.hypot(gx, gy)
s_sob, f_sob = mag[3, 13], mag[7, 6]
n_sob = mag[FLAT].max()
print("      the same three measurements, now with Sobel:")
print("%26s %12s %12s" % ("", "raw diff", "Sobel"))
print("%26s %12.1f %12.1f" % ("strong edge", strong_raw, s_sob))
print("%26s %12.1f %12.1f" % ("faint edge", faint_raw, f_sob))
print("%26s %12.1f %12.1f" % ("worst noise in the flat", noise_raw, n_sob))
print("%26s %11.2fx %11.2fx"
      % ("faint-edge margin", faint_raw / noise_raw, f_sob / n_sob))
print("      Sobel has a gain of 8, so compare the RATIOS, not the values.")
print("      the faint edge went from %.2fx the noise -- lost -- to %.2fx"
      % (faint_raw / noise_raw, f_sob / n_sob))
print("      it. averaging 3 pixels along a line cuts uncorrelated noise")
print("      by about sqrt(3) while leaving a straight edge untouched,")
print("      and a real edge is straight for far more than 3 pixels.")
print()

print("STEP 3 -- DIRECTION. gx and gy together give an angle, not just a")
print("strength:")
ang = np.degrees(np.arctan2(gy, gx))
print("%22s %10s %10s %12s" % ("location", "gx", "gy", "angle"))
for label, (r, c) in (("the strong edge", (3, 13)),
                      ("the faint edge", (7, 6)),
                      ("flat interior", (11, 6))):
    print("%22s %10.1f %10.1f %11.0f deg" % (label, gx[r, c], gy[r, c],
                                             ang[r, c]))
print("   the vertical edge has a big gx and almost no gy; the horizontal")
print("   edge is the reverse. the gradient points ACROSS an edge, never")
print("   along it, so an edge at 0 degrees of gradient is a vertical line.")
print("   over the flat patch the angle is meaningless -- it is the")
print("   direction of the noise, and it changes every pixel.")
print()
```

The next block continues with the left demonstrations.

```python-run
import numpy as np

print("STEP 4 -- NON-MAXIMUM SUPPRESSION. Sobel answers with a ridge two")
print("or three pixels wide; an edge is one pixel wide. keep a pixel only")
print("if it beats its two neighbours ALONG ITS OWN GRADIENT:")
print("      row 7, columns 10-16 before: "
      + " ".join("%4.0f" % v for v in mag[7][10:17]))
nms = np.zeros_like(mag)
for i in range(1, H - 1):
    for j in range(1, W - 1):
        a = ang[i, j] % 180
        if a < 22.5 or a >= 157.5:
            n1, n2 = mag[i, j - 1], mag[i, j + 1]
        elif a < 67.5:
            n1, n2 = mag[i - 1, j + 1], mag[i + 1, j - 1]
        elif a < 112.5:
            n1, n2 = mag[i - 1, j], mag[i + 1, j]
        else:
            n1, n2 = mag[i - 1, j - 1], mag[i + 1, j + 1]
        if mag[i, j] >= n1 and mag[i, j] >= n2:
            nms[i, j] = mag[i, j]
print("      row 7, columns 10-16 after:  "
      + " ".join("%4.0f" % v for v in nms[7][10:17]))
print("      the ridge collapsed onto its crest. this is the first stage")
print("      that needs the ANGLE rather than the strength, which is why")
print("      Sobel bothers to compute two kernels instead of one.")
print("      pixels above 80: %d before, %d after."
      % (int((mag > 80).sum()), int((nms > 80).sum())))
print()
```

Building on the same setup, the next block shows the left cases.

```python-run
import numpy as np

print("STEP 5 -- HYSTERESIS. one threshold cannot work here. try three:")
for t in (80, 200, 400):
    kept = nms > t
    print("      threshold %3d: %3d pixels: %2d on the faint edge, %d on the speck"
          % (t, int(kept.sum()), int(kept[6:9, 0:12].sum()),
             int(kept[10:13, 16:19].sum())))
faint_peak = nms[6:9, 0:12].max()
speck_peak = nms[10:13, 16:19].max()
print("      and here is why no single number works:")
print("         strongest response on the faint edge: %6.1f" % faint_peak)
print("         strongest response on the speck:      %6.1f" % speck_peak)
print("      they are %.0f apart out of %.0f -- the same neighbourhood of"
      % (abs(faint_peak - speck_peak), max(faint_peak, speck_peak)))
print("      values. a threshold that keeps one keeps the other, and a")
print("      threshold that drops one drops the other. BRIGHTNESS ALONE")
print("      CANNOT TELL THEM APART. so use TWO thresholds, and let strong")
print("      edges vouch for weak pixels connected to them:")
LO, HI = 80.0, 400.0
strong = nms >= HI
weak = (nms >= LO) & (nms < HI)
keep = strong.copy()
for _ in range(H * W):
    grown = keep.copy()
    for i in range(1, H - 1):
        for j in range(1, W - 1):
            if weak[i, j] and not keep[i, j] and keep[i - 1:i + 2, j - 1:j + 2].any():
                grown[i, j] = True
    if (grown == keep).all():
        break
    keep = grown
promoted = int(keep.sum() - strong.sum())
print("      strong (>= %.0f): %2d      weak (%.0f to %.0f): %2d"
      % (HI, int(strong.sum()), LO, HI, int(weak.sum())))
print("      after linking: %d kept. %d weak pixels promoted, %d dropped."
      % (int(keep.sum()), promoted, int(weak.sum()) - promoted))
```

The next block covers the final edge map and a summary of each stage.

```python-run
import numpy as np

print("The promoted pixels connect to a strong one. the dropped ones connect to nothing.")
show(keep.astype(float), "the final edge map", 1.0)
print("      the faint edge came back along most of its length, with a")
print("      couple of gaps where the noise happened to flatten its crest.")
print("      and it came back from ONE end: not one of its pixels ever")
print("      cleared the high threshold -- all %d strong pixels are on the"
      % int(strong.sum()))
print("      vertical line (%d of them on the faint edge). the chain starts"
      % int(strong[6:9, 0:12].sum()))
print("      at the junction and walks left. linking is transitive: each")
print("      promoted pixel then vouches for its own neighbour.")
print("      the speck, whose peak response is within %.0f of the faint"
      % abs(faint_peak - speck_peak))
print("      edge's, is gone -- %s in the final map."
      % ("still present" if keep[10:13, 16:19].any() else "not one pixel of it"))
print("      it had no chain leading back to any evidence. that is the")
print("      whole idea: hysteresis judges a pixel by what it CONNECTS to,")
print("      not by how bright it is.")
print()

print("WHAT EACH STAGE REMOVED, counting pixels marked as edge:")
print("%-32s %8s %14s" % ("stage", "pixels", "speck pixels"))
speck_mask = np.zeros((H, W), bool)
speck_mask[10:13, 16:19] = True
for label, m in (("Sobel magnitude > 80", mag > LO),
                 ("+ non-maximum suppression", nms > LO),
                 ("+ hysteresis linking", keep)):
    print("%-32s %8d %14d" % (label, int(m.sum()), int((m & speck_mask).sum())))
print("   thinning cuts the count without discriminating -- it shrinks the")
print("   speck to a crest just as neatly as it shrinks a real edge.")
print("   linking is the only stage that asks whether the evidence is")
print("   connected to anything, and it is the stage that kills the speck.")
print()
print("blur, differentiate, thin, threshold twice: that sequence is Canny,")
print("published in 1986 and still the default in OpenCV. each of the five")
print("stages exists to repair a specific failure of the stage before it,")
print("and none of them does anything a network could not learn -- only")
print("far cheaper, deterministically, with no training data at all.")
```

## Worth remembering

- Edge detection finds edges by looking for rapid changes in pixel intensity.
- The Sobel operator is a fast, classic way with horizontal and vertical filters.
- The <strong>threshold</strong> is a critical param that controls the response of the detection.
- Edge detection is a foundational step for more-level computer vision tasks like object recognition and pic segmentation.

Edge detection is the first step in helping a computer make sense of the visual world, turning a sea of pixels into structured information.

## The connection to convolutional networks

Each one of those kernels is a 3&times;3 convolution filter. The just gap between classical edge detection and the first layer of a CNN is where the counts come from.

Sobel's weights were designed by a person. A CNN's first-layer filters are <em>learned</em> from data by gradient descent. And when you visualise them after training, they look strikingly like Sobel kernels, Gabor filters and colour-opponent detectors. The network rediscovers edge detection since edges are genuinely the most useful low-level feature in natural pics.

That is the honest relationship between the two topics. Classical ways hand-code one layer of filters. Deep networks learn hundreds of layers of them. And the first layer ends up doing roughly what the hand-coded one did.

The real consequences:

- <strong>Do not add Sobel as a preprocessing step</strong> before a CNN. It duplicates what the first layer will learn. And it discards information that later layers might have used.
- <strong>Classical detectors are still useful</strong> when you have no training data, need a deterministic result, must run on a microcontroller. Or want something explainable.
- <strong>Modern learned edge detectors</strong> (HED and its successors) beat Canny much on natural pics, since they use semantic context rather than local contrast alone.

## Where edge detection is still used

- <strong>Industrial inspection</strong> &mdash; measuring parts, checking alignment, finding cracks. There lighting is controlled and rules must be auditable.
- <strong>Document handling</strong> &mdash; locating page edges, table lines and text regions before OCR.
- <strong>Medical imaging</strong> &mdash; delineating structures, often as a part in a more pipeline.
- <strong>Feature extraction for classical vision</strong> &mdash; HOG descriptors. It underpinned pedestrian detection before deep learning, are histograms of just these gradient orientations.
- <strong>Preprocessing for geometry</strong> &mdash; the Hough transform finds lines and circles from edge maps. It is how lane detection and coin counting are done classically.

## Common mistakes

- <strong>Skipping the blur,</strong> giving an edge map dominated by noise.
- <strong>Running edge detection on a colour pic channel by channel</strong> without deciding how to mix the results. Turn to greyscale, or compute the gradient well across channels.
- <strong>With fixed thresholds across varying lighting.</strong> Otsu's way or an adaptive threshold based on pic stats transfers far better.
- <strong>Expecting closed contours.</strong> Edge detectors give fragments. Connecting them into shapes is a split step.
- <strong>Adding it in front of a CNN,</strong> which throws away information the network could have used.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Which detector should I use?</strong> Canny for a general-purpose edge map, Sobel when you need the raw gradients, Scharr when angles matter, Laplacian of Gaussian for blob-like structures.

<strong>How do I choose Canny's thresholds?</strong> Start from the pic's median: low = 0.66&times;median, high = 1.33&times;median is a widely used heuristic that adapts to brightness.

<strong>Why are my edges thick?</strong> Since non-max suppression has not been applied. Sobel gives ridges; Canny thins them.

<strong>Can edge detection work on colour?</strong> Yes &mdash; compute gradients per channel and mix. It catches edges between colours of equal brightness that greyscale change would miss fully.

<strong>Is edge detection obsolete?</strong> For recognition, largely. For measurement, inspection and constrained environments, no. It is fast, deterministic and needs no training data.

<strong>Do CNNs really learn Sobel-like filters?</strong> The first layer of trained pic networks always holds oriented edge and colour-transition detectors. It is one of the most reproducible findings in the field.

## Recap in one screen

- An edge is a large change in brightness. It makes edge detection a derivative computation.
- Sobel and Prewitt give directional gradients. Magnitude and angle come from mixing them.
- Blur first, always &mdash; differencing amplifies noise.
- Canny adds thinning, dual thresholds and hysteresis. And stays the classical standard.
- These kernels are convolutions. And a CNN's first layer learns very close ones from data.
