title: Mean Average Precision
intro: How a list of boxes with trust scores turns one number. What that number isn't telling you.

## Deciding what counts as correct

Classification has an obvious notion of right. Spotting doesn't: a box that
covers most of an object is partly right, and "partly" has to be resolved before
any of it can be counted.

The detail is a threshold on [IoU](iou_and_non_max_suppression.html). A
guess is a **true plus** if its IoU with a ground-truth box of the same
class is at least the threshold, and if that ground-truth box hasn't already
been claimed by a higher-trust guess. Or else it's a false plus.
Ground-truth boxes nothing matched are false negatives.

That second rule matters. Without it, ten shared guesses on one
object would count as ten successes.

## Building the curve

Sort all guesses for a class by trust, highest first, and walk down the
list. After each one, recompute precision and recall over all seen so far,
and plot the point.

The curve in the visualisation is just that walk, one dot per guess. It
starts high &mdash; the most confident guesses are often right &mdash; and
sags as the list goes on. Later guesses are less solid while the
recall denominator stays fixed.

**Mean precision** is the area under that curve. It summarises the whole
ranking rather than speed at one operating point. This Is what makes it
handy: a finder that ranks its correct guesses above its incorrect ones
scores well even so of how its confidences happen to be calibrated.

The exact area math has varied. VOC 2007 sampled precision at eleven
recall levels. VOC 2010 onward and COCO interpolate more finely. The differences
are small and the meanings aren't the same. This Is a reason to
compare numbers only within one rule.

## From AP to mAP

AP is per class. **mAP** is the mean of AP over all classes &mdash; an unweighted
mean, so a class with ten instances counts as much as a class with ten thousand.

That is a deliberate choice and worth knowing. It means mAP is dragged down hard
by rare classes the model handles badly. A model can improve its mAP more by
fixing one rare class than by getting slightly better at a common one.

## Why COCO's numbers look worse

Drag the IoU control. At 0.5 a loosely fitting box counts as a hit. At 0.9 the
box has to be nearly exact.

VOC reported mAP at IoU 0.5 alone. COCO's headline metric means mAP over ten
thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, often written **mAP@[.5:.95]**.
It rewards precise localisation rather than rough spotting, and it's much
harsher: a finder reporting 0.80 under VOC might report 0.45 under COCO on the
same guesses.

So a COCO mAP and a VOC mAP are different readings and must never be checked
right. COCO also reports mAP@0.5 alongside, partly so that check remains
possible.

COCO breaks the number down further. By object size (small, medium,
large) and at fixed spotting counts. Those breakdowns are often more
helpful than the headline: "our finder is bad at small objects" is
actionable in a way that "our mAP is 0.42" isn't.

## What mAP does not tell you

**It has no threshold.** AP integrates over each trust level, so it says
nothing about which threshold to deploy. That is a separate choice.

**It skips the cost of errors.** A missed pedestrian and a spurious traffic
cone count the same.

**It means away the failures you care about.** A model that is great on
19 classes and useless on the 20th can outscore one that is decent on all of
them.

**It says nothing about duplicates before NMS.** mAP is found on
post-processed output, so a finder that fires many shared boxes can look
fine if suppression cleans up.

## AP is the area, worked

Mean precision is the area under the precision-recall curve, and finding one
on a handful of points shows what it rewards. Walk down the trust-sorted
guesses, recomputing precision and recall after each:

|recall|precision|
|---|---|
|0.2|1.00|
|0.4|1.00|
|0.6|0.67|
|0.8|0.50|
|1.0|0.40|

Summing precision across each recall step gives **AP &asymp; 0.71**. Because it
integrates over the whole ranking rather than one operating point, a finder that
puts its correct guesses above its wrong ones scores well even so of how its
confidences are calibrated &mdash; which is just what you want from a summary of
a ranking. The catch is that AP has no threshold. It tells you nothing about
which trust to deploy, and mAP means it *unweighted* across classes, so a
rare class the model handles badly drags the number down as hard as a common one.
And COCO's headline means this again over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95,
which is why a finder reporting 0.80 under VOC's single 0.50 threshold might
report 0.45 under COCO on the same guesses. The numbers are different
readings and can't be checked.

What mAP hides is as key as what it reports. It has no threshold, so it says
nothing about which trust to deploy. It skips the cost of errors, counting a
missed pedestrian and a spurious traffic cone the same. And being an unweighted mean
over classes, a model great on nineteen and useless on the twentieth can outscore
one that is decent on all. COCO's breakdowns. By object size, at fixed
spotting counts &mdash; are often more actionable than the headline, because "bad
at small objects" tells you what to fix where "mAP 0.42" doesn't. And tuning NMS to
lift mAP is a trap: the operating point that raises an integral over all
thresholds isn't the one you ship. So It often makes the deployed finder worse.

## Where it goes wrong

**Checking across datasets or rules.** VOC against COCO, or one AP
interpolation against another, aren't close.

**Reporting mAP alone.** Report the per-class breakdown. It's where the
info is.

**Tuning NMS to raise mAP.** Easy to do and often makes the deployed finder
worse, because the operating point that raises an integral over all
thresholds isn't the one you ship.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What does mAP measure?</strong> How well a detector ranks correct detections above wrong ones, across all classes. It averages the area under each class's precision-recall curve.

<strong>What does mAP@0.5 mean?</strong> A detection counts as correct if its IoU with a ground-truth box is at least 0.5. mAP@[0.5:0.95], used by COCO, averages across IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95.

<strong>How is average precision computed for one class?</strong> Sort detections by confidence, walk down the list marking true and false positives, and trace precision against recall. The area under that curve is AP.

<strong>Why is mAP preferred over accuracy?</strong> Detection has no fixed set of negatives, and accuracy ignores box placement and ranking. mAP rewards both correct localisation and sensible confidence scores.

<strong>What is the difference between VOC and COCO mAP?</strong> VOC uses a single IoU of 0.5, while COCO averages over ten thresholds and so rewards tighter boxes. The same model gets a lower score under COCO.

<strong>Does a high mAP mean the model is ready for production?</strong> Not by itself. It hides the operating threshold, per-size performance and rare-class failures, so check the precision-recall curve at the confidence you will deploy at.

## From one PR curve to a single number

mAP is the standard score for spotting. It's assembled from parts that each discard a thing. This builds it from raw detections upward -- ranking, matching, precision and recall, interpolation, the class mean -- so that when a number comes back low you know which stage to look at.

```python-run
GT = [(0,10,10,50,50),(0,60,60,90,90),(1,20,20,60,60),(1,70,10,95,40),(2,30,30,70,70)]
DETS = [(0,12,12,52,48,0.95),(1,22,18,58,62,0.91),(0,15,15,45,45,0.88),
        (2,33,28,68,72,0.80),(1,10,70,40,95,0.75),(0,58,62,88,92,0.62),
        (2,60,10,80,30,0.55),(1,68,12,92,38,0.40)]
def iou(a, b):
    x1, y1 = max(a[0],b[0]), max(a[1],b[1])
    x2, y2 = min(a[2],b[2]), min(a[3],b[3])
    inter = max(0,x2-x1)*max(0,y2-y1)
    return inter / float((a[2]-a[0])*(a[3]-a[1])+(b[2]-b[0])*(b[3]-b[1])-inter)
print("%d ground-truth boxes, %d detections." % (len(GT), len(DETS)))
print("STEP 1 -- SORT BY CONFIDENCE. ranking matters: a detector that ranks")
print("badly scores badly even if it found everything.")
print()
def evaluate(thresh):
    order = sorted(range(len(DETS)), key=lambda i: -DETS[i][5])
    used, rows = set(), []
    for rank, i in enumerate(order):
        d = DETS[i]; best, best_j = 0.0, -1
        for j, g in enumerate(GT):
            if g[0] != d[0]: continue
            v = iou(d[1:5], g[1:5])
            if v > best: best, best_j = v, j
        if best >= thresh and best_j not in used:
            used.add(best_j); verdict, note = "TP", "gt %d" % best_j
        elif best >= thresh:
            verdict, note = "FP", "gt %d taken" % best_j
        else:
            verdict, note = "FP", "IoU %.2f" % best
        rows.append((rank+1, d[5], best, verdict, note))
    return rows
rows = evaluate(0.5)
print("STEP 2 -- MATCH. TP if IoU >= 0.5 with an unclaimed box:")
print("%-6s %8s %8s %6s  %s" % ("rank", "conf", "IoU", "type", "why"))
for rank, conf, best, verdict, note in rows:
    print("%-6d %8.2f %8.2f %6s  %s" % (rank, conf, best, verdict, note))
print("   rank 3 overlaps gt 0 well but gt 0 was already claimed at rank 1.")
print("   duplicates cost you; that is why NMS runs before evaluation.")
```

The next block walks down the ranked list accumulating precision and recall at each step.

```python-run
GT = [(0,10,10,50,50),(0,60,60,90,90),(1,20,20,60,60),(1,70,10,95,40),(2,30,30,70,70)]
DETS = [(0,12,12,52,48,0.95),(1,22,18,58,62,0.91),(0,15,15,45,45,0.88),
        (2,33,28,68,72,0.80),(1,10,70,40,95,0.75),(0,58,62,88,92,0.62),
        (2,60,10,80,30,0.55),(1,68,12,92,38,0.40)]
def iou(a, b):
    x1, y1 = max(a[0],b[0]), max(a[1],b[1])
    x2, y2 = min(a[2],b[2]), min(a[3],b[3])
    inter = max(0,x2-x1)*max(0,y2-y1)
    return inter / float((a[2]-a[0])*(a[3]-a[1])+(b[2]-b[0])*(b[3]-b[1])-inter)
def evaluate(thresh):
    order = sorted(range(len(DETS)), key=lambda i: -DETS[i][5])
    used, rows = set(), []
    for rank, i in enumerate(order):
        d = DETS[i]; best, best_j = 0.0, -1
        for j, g in enumerate(GT):
            if g[0] != d[0]: continue
            v = iou(d[1:5], g[1:5])
            if v > best: best, best_j = v, j
        if best >= thresh and best_j not in used:
            used.add(best_j); verdict = "TP"
        else: verdict = "FP"
        rows.append((rank+1, d[5], verdict))
    return rows
rows = evaluate(0.5)
print("STEP 3 -- PRECISION AND RECALL after each detection:")
print("   precision = TP/(TP+FP)   recall = TP/all_GT")
tp = fp = 0; P, R = [], []
print("%-6s %6s %6s %6s %10s %10s" % ("rank","type","TP","FP","prec","recall"))
for rank, conf, verdict in rows:
    if verdict == "TP": tp += 1
    else: fp += 1
    p, r = tp/float(tp+fp), tp/float(len(GT))
    P.append(p); R.append(r)
    print("%-6d %6s %6d %6d %10.4f %10.4f" % (rank, verdict, tp, fp, p, r))
print("   recall only rises. precision jitters at every FP -- that is why")
print("   the curve must be interpolated before computing area under it.")
```

The next block interpolates the precision-recall curve and computes AP as the area under it, comparing the all-points and 11-point methods.

```python-run
import numpy as np
P = [1.0, 1.0, 0.6667, 0.75, 0.6, 0.6667, 0.5714, 0.625]
R = [0.2, 0.4, 0.4, 0.6, 0.6, 0.8, 0.8, 1.0]
print("STEP 4 -- INTERPOLATE. replace each precision by the best at that")
print("recall or higher:")
Pi = np.array(P).copy()
for i in range(len(Pi)-2, -1, -1):
    Pi[i] = max(Pi[i], Pi[i+1])
print("%-10s %10s %12s %10s" % ("recall", "prec", "interpolated", "change"))
for i in range(len(P)):
    print("%-10.4f %10.4f %12.4f %10s"
          % (R[i], P[i], Pi[i], "" if abs(Pi[i]-P[i])<1e-4 else "raised"))
print("   this removes dips. you would never deploy at a threshold whose")
print("   precision is beaten by a more permissive one.")
print()
print("STEP 5 -- AP = area under the interpolated curve:")
ap, prev_r = 0.0, 0.0
for i in range(len(R)):
    ap += (R[i]-prev_r)*Pi[i]; prev_r = R[i]
print("   AP at IoU 0.5 = %.4f (all-points method, used by COCO)." % ap)
ap11 = 0.0
for t in np.arange(0, 1.01, 0.1):
    at = [Pi[i] for i in range(len(R)) if R[i] >= t]
    ap11 += (max(at) if at else 0.0) / 11.0
print("   AP, 11-point method = %.4f" % ap11)
print("   AP numbers from different papers are not comparable unless they")
print("   use the same method, threshold and dataset.")
```

The next block sweeps the IoU threshold from 0.50 to 0.95 the way COCO does, then averages the column to get the headline COCO AP.

```python-run
import numpy as np
GT = [(0,10,10,50,50),(0,60,60,90,90),(1,20,20,60,60),(1,70,10,95,40),(2,30,30,70,70)]
DETS = [(0,12,12,52,48,0.95),(1,22,18,58,62,0.91),(0,15,15,45,45,0.88),
        (2,33,28,68,72,0.80),(1,10,70,40,95,0.75),(0,58,62,88,92,0.62),
        (2,60,10,80,30,0.55),(1,68,12,92,38,0.40)]
def iou(a, b):
    x1, y1 = max(a[0],b[0]), max(a[1],b[1])
    x2, y2 = min(a[2],b[2]), min(a[3],b[3])
    inter = max(0,x2-x1)*max(0,y2-y1)
    return inter / float((a[2]-a[0])*(a[3]-a[1])+(b[2]-b[0])*(b[3]-b[1])-inter)
def evaluate(thresh):
    order = sorted(range(len(DETS)), key=lambda i: -DETS[i][5])
    used, rows = set(), []
    for rank, i in enumerate(order):
        d = DETS[i]; best, best_j = 0.0, -1
        for j, g in enumerate(GT):
            if g[0] != d[0]: continue
            v = iou(d[1:5], g[1:5])
            if v > best: best, best_j = v, j
        if best >= thresh and best_j not in used:
            used.add(best_j); rows.append("TP")
        else: rows.append("FP")
    return rows
print("STEP 6 -- SWEEP THE THRESHOLD. COCO averages AP from 0.50 to 0.95:")
print("%-12s %8s %8s %10s" % ("IoU", "TP", "FP", "AP"))
aps = []
for t in np.arange(0.5, 0.96, 0.05):
    rs = evaluate(t); ntp = rs.count("TP")
    tp_, fp_, PP, RR = 0, 0, [], []
    for v in rs:
        if v == "TP": tp_ += 1
        else: fp_ += 1
        PP.append(tp_/float(tp_+fp_)); RR.append(tp_/float(len(GT)))
    Q = np.array(PP)
    for i in range(len(Q)-2,-1,-1): Q[i] = max(Q[i], Q[i+1])
    a, pr = 0.0, 0.0
    for i in range(len(RR)): a += (RR[i]-pr)*Q[i]; pr = RR[i]
    aps.append(a)
    print("%-12.2f %8d %8d %10.4f" % (t, ntp, len(rs)-ntp, a))
print("   COCO AP = %.4f -- gap to AP@0.5 measures localisation." % np.mean(aps))
```

The next block shows the 'm' in mAP: the unweighted average over classes, which gives rare classes equal weight.

```python-run
import numpy as np
print("STEP 7 -- THE 'm'. mAP is the unweighted mean of AP over classes:")
print("%-16s %10s %10s" % ("class", "instances", "AP"))
demo = [("person", 10777, 0.62), ("car", 1918, 0.55), ("toaster", 9, 0.05)]
for name, n, a in demo:
    print("%-16s %10d %10.2f" % (name, n, a))
mAP = np.mean([a for _, _, a in demo])
weighted = sum(n*a for _, n, a in demo) / sum(n for _, n, _ in demo)
print("%-16s %10s %10.4f" % ("mAP", "--", mAP))
print("   mAP is UNWEIGHTED: toaster (%d instances) counts as much as"
      % demo[2][1])
print("   person (%d). weighting would give %.4f instead of %.4f."
      % (demo[0][1], weighted, mAP))
print("   that is deliberate -- it stops a model looking good by acing")
print("   common classes alone -- but a single rare hard class can")
print("   dominate your headline number.")
```
