title: Receptive Field
intro: How much of the first image a single deep unit can really see. Why the answer shapes architecture.

## The question

Take one number in the output of a convolutional network's third layer. Change
a pixel in the input image. Does that number change?

For most pixels, no. A convolution is local. Each output depends only on
a small window of its input. And stacking local ops gives an
output that depends on a larger. But Still bounded, region of the first
image. That region is the unit's **receptive field**, and it's the honest
answer to "what can this feature possibly be detecting".

A unit with a 7&#215;7 receptive field can't detect a face in a 224&#215;224
photo. It has never seen a face. It has seen a 7&#215;7 patch, and
what it reacts to has to be visible in one.

## Counting it, one layer at a time

Drag the layers control above and watch the highlighted band widen as it moves
down the diagram. Each row shows how many input pixels the layer above can be
influenced by.

With stride 1 the rule is simple. A single unit sees 1 pixel of its own input.
One `k`&#215;`k` convolution makes that `k`. Another adds `k - 1` more, because
the window's centre already covers what the previous layer covered and each
side extends by `(k-1)/2`. So:

```
r = 1
for each layer:
    r = r + (k - 1)
```

Three 3&#215;3 layers: 1 → 3 → 5 → 7. The growth is **linear in depth**, and
that is slow. A network of twenty 3&#215;3 layers at stride 1 has a receptive
field of 41 pixels &mdash; less than a fifth of a 224-pixel image.

## Stride changes the arithmetic

Set the stride control to 2 and the widening accelerates sharply.

stride changes the *spacing* between the spots a layer
looks at, measured in first pixels. Call that spacing the jump. At stride 1
the jump stays 1 forever. At stride 2 it doubles each layer. A step of one
unit in layer three matches to a step of four pixels in the input.

```
r = 1; jump = 1
for each layer:
    r = r + (k - 1) * jump
    jump = jump * stride
```

Now the additions themselves grow, and the receptive field expands
geometrically rather than linearly. This is the real reason architectures
downsample. Pooling and strided convolutions are often shown as reducing
work, which they do &mdash; but the more key effect is that they
are the only affordable way to get a deep unit to see the whole image.

## Why 3&#215;3 won everything

Two stacked 3&#215;3 convolutions have the same 5&#215;5 receptive field as
one 5&#215;5 convolution. Three stacked have the same 7&#215;7 as one
7&#215;7. So why not just use the big kernel?

Count the weights, per input/output channel pair:

| Arrangement | Receptive field | Weights |
|---|---|---|
| One 5&#215;5 | 5&#215;5 | 25 |
| Two 3&#215;3 | 5&#215;5 | 18 |
| One 7&#215;7 | 7&#215;7 | 49 |
| Three 3&#215;3 | 7&#215;7 | 27 |

The stack is cheaper, and it has a second edge that matters more: there's
a non-linearity between the layers. One 7&#215;7 convolution is a single
linear function of its 49 inputs. Three 3&#215;3 convolutions with ReLUs
between them is a composition of three linear functions separated by
non-linearities, which can represent things a single linear map can't.

VGG made this arg by name in 2014 and in effect ended large kernels
-purpose vision architectures. All since is 3&#215;3 stacks,
with the occasional [1&#215;1](one_by_one_convolutions.html) for channel work.

## Effective versus theoretical

The number the formula gives is the *theoretical* receptive field: the set of
pixels that could in principle affect the output.

The **effective** receptive field is smaller and softer. parts from the
edge of the theoretical field pass through fewer paths than parts from
the centre, and the number of paths falls off roughly like a Gaussian. In
practice a unit is strongly influenced by the middle of its field and barely
influenced by the rim.

The hands-on consequence is that a network often needs a theoretical
receptive field noticeably larger than the objects it has to recognise, not
merely equal to them. Dilated convolutions exist largely as a way to buy
receptive field without buying downsampling. This Matters when the output has
to stay at full detail &mdash; segmentation, most obviously.

## Why two 3&times;3s beat one 5&times;5

Stacking small kernels reaches as far as one big kernel for fewer weights and more
non-linearity, which is why 3&times;3 stacks won. The receptive field of a stride-1
stack grows by `k-1` per layer, so:

|arrangement|receptive field|weights (per channel pair)|
|---|---|---|
|one 5&times;5|5&times;5|25|
|two 3&times;3|5&times;5|18|
|one 7&times;7|7&times;7|49|
|three 3&times;3|7&times;7|27|

Same reach, fewer weights &mdash; and, more importantly, a ReLU sits between the
stacked layers, so three 3&times;3s are a composition of three non-linear functions
where one 7&times;7 is a single linear map of its 49 inputs. That representational
gap is the arg VGG made in 2014. It ended large kernels. The table also
shows why depth alone isn't enough: at stride 1 the field grows only linearly,
so twenty 3&times;3 layers still see just 41 pixels. Under a fifth of a
224-pixel image. Growing it affordably needs stride. This Makes the per-layer jump
compound geometrically, or dilation. This Is how segmentation networks reach far
while keeping full detail. And the *effective* field is smaller and softer than
this theoretical one, falling off like a Gaussian from the centre, so a network
often needs a theoretical field noticeably larger than the objects it must see.

## Where it goes wrong

**given depth alone is enough.** Twenty stride-1 layers of 3&#215;3 still
only see 41 pixels. Without downsampling or dilation, a deep network can be
blind to any of it large.

**Losing the input detail.** A receptive field of 100 pixels covers
half a 224-pixel image and a twentieth of a 2000-pixel one. Resizing the input
silently changes what the architecture can see.

**Reading spotting failures as a data problem.** If the model always
misses large objects, check the receptive field before collecting more images.

## How much of the image one output pixel can see

A single neuron deep in a network reacts to a patch of the first image. finding that patch's size layer by layer shows most architectural choices -- kernel sizes, depth, dilation and pooling.

```python-run
import numpy as np
print("the recurrence, and it is the whole calculation:")
print("    RF_out = RF_in + (kernel - 1) * jump_in")
print("    jump_out = jump_in * stride")
print()
def trace(layers, label):
    rf, jump = 1, 1
    print(label)
    print("%8s %10s %8s %8s %14s %12s"
          % ("layer", "type", "kernel", "stride", "receptive field", "jump"))
    print("%8s %10s %8s %8s %14d %12d" % ("input", "-", "-", "-", rf, jump))
    for i, (kind, k, s) in enumerate(layers, 1):
        rf = rf + (k - 1) * jump
        jump = jump * s
        print("%8d %10s %8d %8d %14d %12d" % (i, kind, k, s, rf, jump))
    return rf
rf = trace([("conv", 3, 1)] * 8, "EIGHT 3x3 CONVOLUTIONS, stride 1:")
print("   each layer adds 2. after 8 layers a neuron sees %dx%d." % (rf, rf))
print("   growth is LINEAR -- to see 224 pixels you need %d layers."
      % ((224 - 1) // 2))
print()
rf = trace([("conv", 3, 1), ("conv", 3, 1), ("pool", 2, 2),
            ("conv", 3, 1), ("conv", 3, 1), ("pool", 2, 2),
            ("conv", 3, 1), ("conv", 3, 1), ("pool", 2, 2)],
           "VGG PATTERN -- two convs then a pool, three times:")
print("   %dx%d from 9 layers, against 17x17 from 8 without pooling."
      % (rf, rf))
print("   stride-2 layers make growth geometric rather than arithmetic.")
```

Stacking small kernels matches the receptive field of a large one for fewer parameters, and dilation grows the field without any downsampling.

```python-run
import numpy as np
print("TWO 3x3 CONVOLUTIONS vs ONE 5x5 -- same receptive field:")
for label, layers in (("one 5x5", [("conv", 5, 1)]),
                      ("two 3x3", [("conv", 3, 1), ("conv", 3, 1)])):
    rf_, jump_, params = 1, 1, 0
    for _, k, s in layers:
        rf_ += (k - 1) * jump_; jump_ *= s; params += k * k * 64 * 64
    print("   %-10s field %dx%d, %s params (64->64 ch)"
          % (label, rf_, rf_, "{:,}".format(params)))
print("   same field for %.0f%% of the parameters, and two non-linearities."
      % (100 * (2 * 9) / 25))
print()
for label, layers in (("one 7x7", [("conv", 7, 1)]),
                      ("three 3x3", [("conv", 3, 1)] * 3)):
    rf_, jump_, params = 1, 1, 0
    for _, k, s in layers:
        rf_ += (k - 1) * jump_; jump_ *= s; params += k * k * 64 * 64
    print("   %-10s field %dx%d, %s params"
          % (label, rf_, rf_, "{:,}".format(params)))
print()
print("DILATION grows the field without adding parameters or losing")
print("resolution. a dilated kernel has gaps:")
for d in (1, 2, 4):
    eff = 3 + (3 - 1) * (d - 1)
    row = " ".join("x" if i % d == 0 else "." for i in range(eff))
    print("   dilation %d: 3 weights spanning %d pixels   %s" % (d, eff, row))
print()
rf_, jump_ = 1, 1
print("   a stack with doubling dilation, all stride 1:")
print("%8s %10s %16s" % ("layer", "dilation", "receptive field"))
for i, d in enumerate((1, 2, 4, 8, 16), 1):
    eff = 3 + (3 - 1) * (d - 1)
    rf_ += (eff - 1) * jump_
    print("%8d %10d %16d" % (i, d, rf_))
print("   %d pixels from 5 layers and %d weights, no downsampling."
      % (rf_, 5 * 9))
print("   that is why dilation is the standard tool for segmentation.")
```

The effective receptive field is much smaller than the theoretical one because corner pixels contribute far less than centre pixels.

```python-run
import numpy as np
print("THE EFFECTIVE FIELD IS SMALLER THAN THE THEORETICAL ONE.")
print("not every input pixel contributes equally -- the centre is reached")
print("by many paths and the corners by one:")
w = np.array([[1.0]])
for layer in range(4):
    k = np.ones((3, 3))
    new = np.zeros((w.shape[0] + 2, w.shape[1] + 2))
    for i in range(w.shape[0]):
        for j in range(w.shape[1]):
            new[i:i + 3, j:j + 3] += w[i, j] * k
    w = new
w = w / w.max()
print("   contribution weight after 4 layers of 3x3 (field %dx%d):" % w.shape)
for r in w:
    print("      " + "".join("%6.2f" % v for v in r))
centre = w[w.shape[0] // 2, w.shape[1] // 2]
corner = w[0, 0]
print("   centre weight %.2f; corner %.4f -- a factor of %.0f."
      % (centre, corner, centre / corner))
print("   the field is gaussian-ish, not a flat square, so the EFFECTIVE")
print("   field is roughly the square root of the theoretical one.")
print("   that is why very deep networks still benefit from explicit")
print("   long-range mechanisms rather than depth alone.")
```
