title: Harris Corners and Keypoints
intro: What makes a point in an image worth remembering. The determinant that measures it.

## The question behind the detector

To match two photos of the same scene, you need points you can recognise in
both. Which points are those?

The Harris insight is to ask what happens when you shift a small window slightly
in each direction.

**Flat region.** Shift it anywhere and the contents barely change. There's
nothing to lock onto: this window could be almost anywhere.

**Edge.** Shift it along the edge and nothing changes. Shift it across and
all does. It's locatable in one direction and free to slide in the other
&mdash; the aperture problem.

**Corner.** Shift it in any direction at all and the contents change. Its
spot is pinned in both directions, and that is just what a landmark needs
to be.

## Making it arithmetic

The change under a small shift is captured by how the image grad behaves
across the window. Collect the grads into the **structure tensor**:

```
M  =  [ sum(Ix*Ix)   sum(Ix*Iy) ]
      [ sum(Ix*Iy)   sum(Iy*Iy) ]
```

Its two eigenvals say how strongly the intensity varies along the two principal
directions:

| &lambda;1 | &lambda;2 | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| small | small | flat |
| large | small | edge |
| large | large | **corner** |

finding eigenvals at each pixel would be slow. So Harris uses a combination
that behaves the same way without them:

```
R  =  det(M) - k * trace(M)^2
     =  (l1*l2) - k*(l1+l2)^2
```

`R` is large and plus only when both eigenvals are large, strongly minus
at an edge, and near zero on flat ground. The determinant times the
eigenvals, so one small eigenval kills it. The trace term subtracts a fine
that grows when the two are unbalanced.

`k` sets how strictly that balance is enforced &mdash; usually between
0.04 and 0.06. Drag it high in the visualisation and detections thin out to the
sharpest corners. Drag it low and edges start to live.

## Reading the visualisation

Highlighted pixels are those whose response clears the threshold.

Set the threshold low and whole edges light up. This Is the failure mode `R` is
designed to avoid, appearing because the threshold is admitting weakly minus
and near-zero responses. Raise it and only the rectangle's four corners and the
triangle's points remain &mdash; the truly distinctive spots.

The window radius controls how much context each choice uses. A small window is
sensitive to noise and finds many tiny corners. A large one is stable but blurs
nearby corners into a single response and misses fine structure.

## What it is and is not invariant to

**Rotation: yes.** The eigenvals of the structure tensor don't depend on the
coord frame, so a rotated corner is still a corner with the same response.
This is the property that makes Harris handy for matching.

**Illumination: mostly.** A constant brightness offset doesn't change grads
at all. A contrast scaling times them. This Scales `R`, so a scaled
threshold is needed rather than an abs one.

**Scale: no.** This is the major gap. A corner viewed from twice as far is
a smaller corner, and a fixed window either sees only part of it or swamps it
with context.

That gap is what **SIFT** handled, by searching over scales and
spots and recording the scale at which each keypoint is most distinctive,
then showing the local grad pattern so keypoints can be matched rather
than merely found. **ORB** does a thing similar much faster using FAST corners
and binary descriptors, which is why it turns up in real-time systems.

## Where corners are used

Panorama stitching, structure from motion, visual SLAM, camera calibration and
image registration all rest on finding the same physical points in a few images
and solving for the transform between them. Spotting is the first step;
[template matching](template_matching.html) is the option that doesn't
live rotation.

## Flat, edge, corner, by the numbers

The whole finder reduces to the two eigenvals of the structure tensor, and
Harris's shortcut `R = det(M) - k&middot;trace(M)&sup2;` reads them without solving
for them. With `k = 0.04`:

|region|&lambda;&#8321;|&lambda;&#8322;|R|
|---|---|---|---|
|flat|0.1|0.1|+0.008 (near zero)|
|edge|5.0|0.1|**&minus;0.54** (strongly negative)|
|corner|5.0|4.0|**+16.76** (large positive)|

The determinant times the eigenvals. So One small eigenval drags it toward
zero. The trace term subtracts a fine that grows when the two are unbalanced.
The result is large and plus only when *both* eigenvals are large &mdash; the
corner case, where the intensity changes in two free directions and the
window can be pinned down. An edge changes in one direction only, so it comes out
strongly minus and is rejected. A flat region changes in neither and sits near
zero. That is why `R` separates corners from edges without ever finding an
eigenval, and why `k` (0.04-0.06) tunes how strictly the balance is enforced. The
eigenvals are rotation-rule, so a rotated corner scores the same &mdash; but
not scale-rule, which is the gap SIFT was built to close.

What Harris is and isn't rule to decides where it can be used. Rotation: yes
&mdash; the eigenvals don't depend on the coord frame, so a rotated corner
scores the same, which is what makes it usable for matching two views. Illumination:
mostly &mdash; a brightness offset leaves grads untouched, but a contrast scaling
times `R`, which is why the threshold must be scaled to the image max
rather than abs. Scale: no. A corner seen from twice as far is a smaller
corner a fixed window can't capture, and that gap is just what SIFT closed by
searching over scales and recording the one at which each keypoint is most
distinctive. Corners feed panorama stitching, structure-from-motion, visual SLAM and
calibration, all of which rest on finding the same physical point in a few images.

## Where it goes wrong

**An abs threshold.** Response scales with contrast. So A threshold tuned on
one image fails on a darker one. Threshold scaled to the max, as this page
does.

**No non-max suppression.** A corner makes a cluster of high responses,
not one pixel.

**Expecting scale steadiness.** If the camera distance varies, use a
scale-rule finder.

**Corners on a blurred image.** Blur destroys the grads the whole method
depends on, so denoise gently or not at all before detecting.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is a corner, to a computer?</strong> A point where the image changes strongly in every direction when you shift a small window. Along an edge it changes in only one direction, and on flat areas it barely changes.

<strong>What is the Harris response?</strong> R = det(M) - k * trace(M) squared, where M is the matrix of summed gradient products in a window. Large positive R means corner, large negative means edge, small means flat.

<strong>What value should k take?</strong> Typically 0.04 to 0.06. A smaller k admits more corner-like points and a larger k makes the detector stricter.

<strong>Is Harris invariant to rotation and scale?</strong> Rotation, yes, since the eigenvalues of M do not change. Scale, no, so a corner at one size can look like an edge at another.

<strong>Why do I get clusters of detections around one corner?</strong> Neighbouring pixels all have high responses. Apply non-maximum suppression so only the local peak remains.

<strong>How does Shi-Tomasi differ?</strong> It scores a point by the smaller eigenvalue of M instead. It is simple, tends to pick better points to track and is what goodFeaturesToTrack uses.

## Two eigenvalues that tell an edge from a corner

A corner is a place where the image changes in two free directions. That sentence turns right into linear algebra. This builds the structure tensor, reads its eigenvals. Shows why Harris uses a determinant-and-trace shortcut instead of finding them.

```python-run
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
H, W = 14, 20
img = np.full((H, W), 50.0)
img[3:10, 3:9] = 200.0; img[:, 14:16] = 200.0
img += rng.normal(0, 2.0, (H, W))
ramp = " .:-=+*#%@"
def show(a, label, hi=None):
    hi = a.max() if hi is None else hi
    print("   %s" % label)
    for row in a:
        print("      " + "".join(ramp[min(9, int(9*max(0.,v)/(hi+1e-9)))] for v in row))
show(img, "the image", 255)
print("   a solid square (4 corners) and a vertical strip (edges only).")
print()
KX = np.array([[-1., 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]]) / 8.0
p = np.pad(img, 1, mode="edge")
gx = np.array([[float((p[i:i+3, j:j+3]*KX).sum()) for j in range(W)] for i in range(H)])
gy = np.array([[float((p[i:i+3, j:j+3]*KX.T).sum()) for j in range(W)] for i in range(H)])
print("THE STRUCTURE TENSOR at each pixel is a 2x2 matrix:")
print("   M = sum over window of [[gx*gx, gx*gy], [gx*gy, gy*gy]]")
print("   its two eigenvalues tell you: flat, edge, or corner.")
def tensor(r, c, win=3):
    h = win // 2
    r0, r1 = max(0, r-h), min(H, r+h+1)
    c0, c1 = max(0, c-h), min(W, c+h+1)
    a, b = gx[r0:r1, c0:c1].reshape(-1), gy[r0:r1, c0:c1].reshape(-1)
    return np.array([[float(a@a), float(a@b)], [float(a@b), float(b@b)]])
probes = [("flat interior", 6, 5), ("flat background", 12, 10),
          ("vertical edge", 6, 3), ("horizontal edge", 3, 6),
          ("strip's edge", 6, 14), ("top-left corner", 3, 3),
          ("bottom-right corner", 9, 8)]
print("%-26s %8s %8s %10s" % ("location", "l1", "l2", "type"))
for label, r, c in probes:
    ev = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(tensor(r, c)))[::-1]
    if ev[0] < 200: kind = "flat"
    elif ev[1] < ev[0] * 0.15: kind = "edge"
    else: kind = "CORNER"
    print("%-26s %8.0f %8.0f %10s" % (label, ev[0], ev[1], kind))
print("   both small = flat; one large = edge; both large = CORNER.")
```

The next block shows Harris's shortcut: instead of computing eigenvalues, score each pixel with det(M) - k * trace(M)^2, which separates the three cases by sign alone.

```python-run
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
H, W = 14, 20
img = np.full((H, W), 50.0)
img[3:10, 3:9] = 200.0; img[:, 14:16] = 200.0
img += rng.normal(0, 2.0, (H, W))
KX = np.array([[-1., 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]]) / 8.0
p = np.pad(img, 1, mode="edge")
gx = np.array([[float((p[i:i+3, j:j+3]*KX).sum()) for j in range(W)] for i in range(H)])
gy = np.array([[float((p[i:i+3, j:j+3]*KX.T).sum()) for j in range(W)] for i in range(H)])
def tensor(r, c, win=3):
    h = win // 2
    r0, r1 = max(0, r-h), min(H, r+h+1)
    c0, c1 = max(0, c-h), min(W, c+h+1)
    a, b = gx[r0:r1, c0:c1].reshape(-1), gy[r0:r1, c0:c1].reshape(-1)
    return np.array([[float(a@a), float(a@b)], [float(a@b), float(b@b)]])
K = 0.05
print("HARRIS'S SHORTCUT: R = det(M) - k * trace(M)^2")
print("   det(M) = l1*l2, trace(M) = l1+l2. no square roots needed.")
print("%-26s %12s %12s %12s" % ("location", "l1*l2", "det(M)", "R"))
probes = [("vertical edge", 6, 3), ("horizontal edge", 3, 6),
          ("strip's edge", 6, 14), ("top-left corner", 3, 3),
          ("bottom-right corner", 9, 8)]
for label, r, c in probes:
    M = tensor(r, c)
    ev = np.sort(np.linalg.eigvalsh(M))[::-1]
    R = np.linalg.det(M) - K * np.trace(M)**2
    print("%-26s %12.4g %12.4g %12.4g" % (label, ev[0]*ev[1], np.linalg.det(M), R))
print("   det matches the eigenvalue product exactly.")
print("   R's sign: edge -> negative, corner -> positive, flat -> near 0.")
print("   one number, three cases, distinguished by SIGN.")
```

The next block computes the full Harris response map, thresholds it and extracts local maxima to find the four corners of the square.

```python-run
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
H, W = 14, 20
img = np.full((H, W), 50.0)
img[3:10, 3:9] = 200.0; img[:, 14:16] = 200.0
img += rng.normal(0, 2.0, (H, W))
KX = np.array([[-1., 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]]) / 8.0
p = np.pad(img, 1, mode="edge")
gx = np.array([[float((p[i:i+3, j:j+3]*KX).sum()) for j in range(W)] for i in range(H)])
gy = np.array([[float((p[i:i+3, j:j+3]*KX.T).sum()) for j in range(W)] for i in range(H)])
def tensor(r, c, win=3):
    h = win // 2
    r0, r1 = max(0, r-h), min(H, r+h+1)
    c0, c1 = max(0, c-h), min(W, c+h+1)
    a, b = gx[r0:r1, c0:c1].reshape(-1), gy[r0:r1, c0:c1].reshape(-1)
    return np.array([[float(a@a), float(a@b)], [float(a@b), float(b@b)]])
K = 0.05
Rmap = np.zeros((H, W))
for r in range(H):
    for c in range(W):
        M = tensor(r, c)
        Rmap[r, c] = np.linalg.det(M) - K * np.trace(M)**2
ramp = " .:-=+*#%@"
print("THE RESPONSE MAP (positive values only):")
for row in np.maximum(Rmap, 0):
    print("   " + "".join(ramp[min(9, int(9*max(0.,v)/(Rmap.max()+1e-9)))] for v in row))
print("   four corners light up; edges and the strip do not.")
thr = Rmap.max() * 0.25; peaks = []
for r in range(1, H-1):
    for c in range(1, W-1):
        if Rmap[r,c] > thr and Rmap[r,c] >= Rmap[r-1:r+2, c-1:c+2].max():
            peaks.append((r, c, Rmap[r, c]))
print("   %d local maxima above 25%% of max:" % len(peaks))
for r, c, v in peaks:
    print("      (%2d,%2d)  R = %.4g" % (r, c, v))
```

The next block summarises what the Harris response is and is not invariant to, and why SIFT was invented to fix the scale gap.

```python-run
print("WHAT HARRIS IS AND IS NOT INVARIANT TO:")
print("%-26s %s" % ("change", "does R survive?"))
for k, v in (("translation", "yes -- computed locally"),
             ("rotation", "yes -- det and trace are rotation invariant"),
             ("brightness (+b)", "yes -- gradients ignore a constant"),
             ("contrast (*a)", "NO -- R scales as a^4"),
             ("scale (zoom)", "NO -- a corner becomes a curve")):
    print("%-26s %s" % (k, v))
print("   contrast: R is thresholded relative to the image's own maximum.")
print("   scale: SIFT was invented to fix this -- search over scales as")
print("   well as positions. Harris finds WHERE; scale-space finds where")
print("   AND how big.")
```
