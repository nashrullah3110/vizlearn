title: Convolution Kernels by Hand
intro: Nine numbers, one sliding window, and most of classical image processing.

## One operation, many effects

Convolution is a single, simple op that makes a startling range of
results based on nine numbers. Take a small grid of weights &mdash; the
**kernel** &mdash; centre it on a pixel, times each weight by the pixel
underneath it, and add the products together. That sum is the output pixel.
Slide the kernel one step and do it again.

That is the whole method. Blurring, sharpening, edge spotting and
embossing aren't different methods. They're the same method with
different numbers in the grid.

## Reading a kernel

The identity kernel is the easiest to reason about:

```
0  0  0
0  1  0
0  0  0
```

each neighbour is times'd by zero, the centre pixel by one, so the output
is the input unchanged. Now change the centre to 5 and set the four
perp neighbours to &minus;1:

```
 0 -1  0
-1  5 -1
 0 -1  0
```

The centre pixel is amplified and its neighbours are subtracted from it. Where
the zone is flat, the subtraction cancels the amplification just
and nothing happens. Where the centre differs from its surroundings &mdash; at
an edge &mdash; the difference is exaggerated. That is **sharpening**, and it
shows why sharpening amplifies noise: a noisy pixel is, by meaning, a
pixel that differs from its neighbours.

## The sum tells you what kind of filter it is

There are two families, and you can tell them apart by adding the weights up.

**Weights summing to 1** preserve mean brightness. A box blur of nine ones
cut by nine is the arithmetic mean of the zone. A Gaussian blur
weights the centre more heavily than the corners. Both keep the image at the
same total exposure because the total part of each pixel is
unchanged.

**Weights summing to 0** give a difference image. Flat regions become zero,
because a constant times'd by weights that cancel gives nothing. Only where
the image changes does any of it live. Since a result can be minus, these
filters are often displayed with 128 added. This Is why edge images have
that flat grey background.

| Kernel | Sum | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | 1 | everything, unchanged |
| Box blur | 1 (after division) | low-frequency detail |
| Sharpen | 1 | everything, with edges exaggerated |
| Sobel | 0 | edges in one direction |
| Laplacian | 0 | edges in every direction |
| Emboss | 1 | a directional shadow |

## Sobel: why there are two of them

The Sobel operator comes as a pair, and the pair is one kernel rotated by
ninety degrees:

```
Sobel X          Sobel Y
-1  0  1        -1 -2 -1
-2  0  2         0  0  0
-1  0  1         1  2  1
```

Sobel X subtracts the column on the left from the column on the right. A
vertical edge &mdash; where left and right differ &mdash; makes a large
value. A flat edge makes nothing at all, because left and right are
the same there. Sobel Y is the same arg with rows.

Run each on the visualisation above and the skew is obvious: the vertical
sides of the rectangle appear under Sobel X and vanish under Sobel Y. In
practice the two are mixed, often as the square root of the sum of their
squares, to get an edge strength that doesn't care about direction.

The middle row being &minus;2, 0, 2 rather than &minus;1, 0, 1 is a small
piece of smoothing built in: the pixel in line with the centre is weighted
more than the main-lines, so a single noisy pixel has less sway than it
would in a pure difference.

## What happens at the border

A kernel centred on a corner pixel has five of its nine cells hanging over the
edge of the image. a thing has to be decided, and the three common answers
are to leave the border out (making a smaller output), to treat the missing
pixels as zero, or to repeat the nearest real pixel outwards. The
visualisation here repeats the edge pixel. This Is why the frame stays clean;
zero-padding would darken it. The [padding module](padding_in_cnn.html) works
through what that choice costs inside a network.

## Why this matters for CNNs

All above is fixed: someone chose the nine numbers and wrote them down.
A convolutional layer in a neural network performs the the same op and
differs in just one respect. The nine numbers are params, and
gradient descent chooses them.

That is worth sitting with. When people say a CNN "learns to detect edges in
the first layer", they mean that training drives some of the learned kernels
towards a thing that looks very much like Sobel. Edges turn out to
be a handy thing to measure. No one put Sobel there. The architecture only
supplies the sliding window. The content of the window is found.

## One output pixel, worked

A convolution is a weighted sum repeated at each spot, and the character of a
kernel is legible from its numbers. Take the sharpen kernel &mdash; centre 5, the
four perp neighbours &minus;1, corners 0. And apply it where a centre
pixel of 100 sits among neighbours of 90:

`5&times;100 &minus; 90 &minus; 90 &minus; 90 &minus; 90 = 140`

|patch|sharpen output|
|---|---|
|centre 100, neighbours 90 (an edge)|140 &mdash; the difference exaggerated|
|centre 100, all neighbours 100 (flat)|100 &mdash; unchanged|

On flat ground the amplification of the centre and the subtraction of the neighbours
cancel just, so nothing happens. Only where the centre differs from its
surroundings does the output diverge. This Is why sharpening exaggerates edges and,
for the same reason, amplifies noise. A noisy pixel *is* one that differs from
its neighbours. The kernel's weight sum tells you its family: sum to 1 (identity,
blur, sharpen) preserves brightness, sum to 0 (Sobel, Laplacian) makes a
difference image that is flat-grey where nothing changes. A CNN's first layer runs
just this op with the nine numbers as *learned* params &mdash; and
training well drives some of them toward a thing very like Sobel, because
edges turn out to be worth measuring, though no one put Sobel there.

## Where it goes wrong

**Sharpening noise.** Any kernel with minus weights amplifies pixel-to-pixel
variation, and noise is pixel-to-pixel variation. Denoise first, sharpen
second.

**Losing to norm.** A blur kernel of nine ones, applied without
cutting by nine, makes an image nine times too bright &mdash; which, after
clipping at 255, is a white rectangle.

**given bigger is better.** A 3&#215;3 kernel sees a 3&#215;3 region. To
see further you either use a larger kernel. This Costs as n&sup2; more
times, or stack a few small ones. This Is what modern
architectures do and why [receptive field](feature_map_in_cnn.html) is a
concept worth having.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Is convolution the same as correlation?</strong> Strictly, convolution flips the kernel first and correlation does not. Most libraries, and every deep learning framework, actually compute correlation but still call it convolution.

<strong>Why do some kernels sum to 1 and others to 0?</strong> A sum of 1 keeps overall brightness, as in blur and sharpen. A sum of 0 gives a difference image that is zero on flat regions, as in Sobel and Laplacian.

<strong>Why does Sobel come as two kernels?</strong> One measures change along x and the other along y. Combining them gives edge strength from the magnitude and edge direction from the angle.

<strong>What happens at the image border?</strong> The kernel hangs off the edge, so you pad with zeros, replicate or reflect the edge pixels, or just shrink the output. The choice changes the result near the border.

<strong>How does this relate to a CNN layer?</strong> A convolutional layer runs the same sliding weighted sum. The difference is that the kernel values are learned by gradient descent rather than chosen by hand.

<strong>Why do the results sometimes look clipped or all black?</strong> Outputs can be negative or exceed 255, and an 8-bit image cannot hold them. Compute in float, then rescale or take the absolute value before display.

## Slide a 3x3 grid over an image and watch what each one finds

A convolution is a weighted sum repeated at each spot. Six classic kernels are applied to the same small image here, with the math for one output pixel written out in full.

```python-run
import numpy as np
img = np.zeros((9, 9))
img[2:7, 2:7] = 200.0
img[0:3, 6:9] = 120.0
img += np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 4, img.shape)
img = np.clip(img, 0, 255)
SHADE = " .:-=+*#%@"
def show(a, label, lo=None, hi=None):
    lo = a.min() if lo is None else lo
    hi = a.max() if hi is None else hi
    rng_ = max(hi - lo, 1e-9)
    print("   %s" % label)
    for row in a:
        print("      " + "".join(SHADE[int(np.clip((v - lo) / rng_, 0, 1) * 9)]
                                 for v in row))
show(img, "the image (%dx%d, values 0-255):" % img.shape)
print()
def convolve(a, k):
    kh, kw = k.shape
    ph, pw = kh // 2, kw // 2
    padded = np.pad(a, ((ph, ph), (pw, pw)), mode="edge")
    out = np.zeros_like(a, dtype=float)
    for i in range(a.shape[0]):
        for j in range(a.shape[1]):
            out[i, j] = (padded[i:i + kh, j:j + kw] * k).sum()
    return out
print("ONE OUTPUT PIXEL, written out. take the 3x3 patch at (4,4):")
patch = img[3:6, 3:6]
box = np.ones((3, 3)) / 9
print("   patch:")
for r in patch:
    print("      %s" % np.round(r, 1))
print("   kernel (box blur):")
for r in box:
    print("      %s" % np.round(r, 4))
terms = (patch * box).ravel()
print("   multiply elementwise and sum:")
print("      %s" % "  ".join("%.1f" % t for t in terms[:5]))
print("      + ... = %.4f" % terms.sum())
print("   that one number is the output at (4,4). repeat for every pixel.")
```

Six classic kernels are applied to the same image, and the kernel sum reveals the filter family.

```python-run
import numpy as np
img = np.zeros((9, 9))
img[2:7, 2:7] = 200.0
img[0:3, 6:9] = 120.0
img += np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 4, img.shape)
img = np.clip(img, 0, 255)
SHADE = " .:-=+*#%@"
def show(a, label):
    print("   %s" % label)
    for row in a:
        print("      " + "".join(SHADE[int(np.clip((v - a.min()) / max(a.max() - a.min(), 1e-9), 0, 1) * 9)]
                                 for v in row))
def convolve(a, k):
    kh, kw = k.shape
    padded = np.pad(a, ((kh//2, kh//2), (kw//2, kw//2)), mode="edge")
    out = np.zeros_like(a, dtype=float)
    for i in range(a.shape[0]):
        for j in range(a.shape[1]):
            out[i, j] = (padded[i:i+kh, j:j+kw] * k).sum()
    return out
kernels = {
    "identity":        np.array([[0,0,0],[0,1,0],[0,0,0]], float),
    "box blur":        np.ones((3,3)) / 9,
    "sharpen":         np.array([[0,-1,0],[-1,5,-1],[0,-1,0]], float),
    "sobel x (vert.)": np.array([[-1,0,1],[-2,0,2],[-1,0,1]], float),
    "sobel y (horiz.)": np.array([[-1,-2,-1],[0,0,0],[1,2,1]], float),
    "laplacian":       np.array([[0,1,0],[1,-4,1],[0,1,0]], float),
}
print("SIX KERNELS on the same image:")
for name, k in kernels.items():
    out = convolve(img, k)
    s = k.sum()
    kind = ("preserves" if abs(s-1)<1e-9 else "detects change" if abs(s)<1e-9
            else "scales by %.1f" % s)
    print()
    print("   %-18s sum %+.1f  %s  range %+.1f to %+.1f"
          % (name, s, kind, out.min(), out.max()))
    show(np.abs(out) if s == 0 else out, "")
```

Combining the two Sobel directions gives edge strength regardless of orientation, which is what a CNN's early layers learn to compute.

```python-run
import numpy as np
def convolve(a, k):
    kh, kw = k.shape
    padded = np.pad(a, ((kh//2, kh//2), (kw//2, kw//2)), mode="edge")
    out = np.zeros_like(a, dtype=float)
    for i in range(a.shape[0]):
        for j in range(a.shape[1]):
            out[i, j] = (padded[i:i+kh, j:j+kw] * k).sum()
    return out
kernels = {
    "identity":  np.array([[0,0,0],[0,1,0],[0,0,0]], float),
    "box blur":  np.ones((3,3)) / 9,
    "sharpen":   np.array([[0,-1,0],[-1,5,-1],[0,-1,0]], float),
    "sobel x":   np.array([[-1,0,1],[-2,0,2],[-1,0,1]], float),
    "sobel y":   np.array([[-1,-2,-1],[0,0,0],[1,2,1]], float),
    "laplacian": np.array([[0,1,0],[1,-4,1],[0,1,0]], float),
}
flat = np.full((9, 9), 100.0)
print("each kernel on a completely flat image of 100s:")
for name, k in kernels.items():
    print("   %-10s -> centre pixel %+8.2f" % (name, convolve(flat, k)[4, 4]))
print("   edge detectors return 0 because there is no edge.")
```

Combining the two Sobel directions gives gradient magnitude — the strength of an edge regardless of its orientation.

```python-run
import numpy as np
SHADE = " .:-=+*#%@"
def show(a, label):
    print("   %s" % label)
    for row in a:
        print("      " + "".join(SHADE[int(np.clip((v-a.min())/max(a.max()-a.min(),1e-9),0,1)*9)] for v in row))
def convolve(a, k):
    kh, kw = k.shape
    padded = np.pad(a, ((kh//2,kh//2),(kw//2,kw//2)), mode="edge")
    out = np.zeros_like(a, dtype=float)
    for i in range(a.shape[0]):
        for j in range(a.shape[1]):
            out[i, j] = (padded[i:i+kh, j:j+kw] * k).sum()
    return out
img = np.zeros((9,9)); img[2:7,2:7] = 200.0; img[0:3,6:9] = 120.0
img += np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 4, img.shape)
img = np.clip(img, 0, 255)
gx = convolve(img, np.array([[-1,0,1],[-2,0,2],[-1,0,1]], float))
gy = convolve(img, np.array([[-1,-2,-1],[0,0,0],[1,2,1]], float))
mag = np.sqrt(gx**2 + gy**2)
show(mag, "gradient magnitude:")
print("   the bright ring is the boundary of the square.")
ang = np.degrees(np.arctan2(gy, gx))
strong = mag > mag.max() * 0.5
print("strongest edge pixels and their orientations:")
for (i, j) in list(zip(*np.where(strong)))[:6]:
    print("   (%d,%d) mag %7.1f, angle %+7.1f deg" % (i, j, mag[i,j], ang[i,j]))
print("a CNN learns its kernels, but early layers rediscover edge")
print("detectors like these because the data rewards them.")
```
