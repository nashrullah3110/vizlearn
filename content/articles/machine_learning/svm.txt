title: Support Vector Machines: A Visual Guide
intro: A support vector machine chooses a decision boundary with a wide margin. Kernels extend it to nonlinear boundaries, while the regularization setting controls tolerance for training errors.

## The Core Idea of SVM

A Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a powerful supervised learning algorithm used for classification and regression. For classification, its primary goal is to find the best <strong>hyperplane</strong> that best separates data points from the two classes in a high-dimensional space. The "best" hyperplane is the one that has the largest possible <strong>margin</strong>&mdash;the distance between the hyperplane and the nearest data point from either class.

The data points closest to the hyperplane are the support vectors. They are the only points that influence the position and orientation of the edge &mdash; any other training point could be moved or removed without changing the result. This property makes SVMs memory-fast and resistant to outliers far from the edge.

In two dimensions the hyperplane is a line. In three dimensions it is a plane. In more dimensions it is a flat surface that cannot be visualised but is set by the same linear equation. The SVM finds the hyperplane that maximises the gap between the two classes, since a wider margin means the classifier is less sensitive to small perturbations in the data.

When the data is not linearly separable &mdash; no straight edge can split the classes cleanly &mdash; SVMs use the kernel trick. A kernel function computes the dot product of the data points in a higher-dimensional space without explicitly computing the transformed coordinates. The radial basis function (RBF) kernel is the most common choice and can model arbitrarily complex edges. The trade-off is controlled by the regularisation parameter C: a large C penalises misclassifications heavily and gives a tight, possibly overfit edge. A small C lets more margin violations and gives a smoother, more general edge.

## Boosting the Margin

Why is a large margin so key? A larger margin places the decision boundary farther from the nearest training examples. This generally makes it less sensitive to small perturbations, though it does not guarantee calibrated confidence or better performance on every dataset.

The hyperplane is set by the equation <code class="mono-font/50 px-1.5 py-0.5 rounded">w·x - b = 0</code>. There <code>w</code> is a weight vector and <code>b</code> is the bias. The two parallel hyperplanes that set the margin are <code class="mono-font/50 px-1.5 py-0.5 rounded">w·x - b = 1</code> and <code class="mono-font/50 px-1.5 py-0.5 rounded">w·x - b = -1</code>.

## What are Support Vectors?

The data points that lie just on the margin edges are called <strong>Support Vectors</strong>. These are the most critical data points in the dataset since they alone "support" or set the position and orientation of the best hyperplane.

- If you were to move a support vector, the best hyperplane would also move.
- If you were to remove a non-support vector, the hyperplane would not change at all.

In the visualization, these points are highlighted with a green circle. Notice how few points are really needed to set the whole edge!

## The widest street between two classes

Many straight lines can split two groups of points. A support vector machine asks a sharper question: which line leaves the most room?

Picture the edge not as a line but as a road laid between the two classes. Push the kerbs outwards until they touch the nearest points on each side. The width of that road is the <strong>margin</strong>. And the SVM chooses the orientation that makes it as wide as possible.

The reason this is a good idea is generalisation. A edge squeezed up against the training points will misclassify a new point that falls slightly on the wrong side. A edge with a wide buffer on both sides tolerates that wobble.

The points that touch the kerbs are the <strong>support vectors</strong>. And they are the points that determine the boundary. Remove any non-support training point and refit &mdash; you get just the same edge. That is an unusual and useful property: the model is set by a handful of borderline cases rather than by the bulk of the data.

## Soft margins, and the C setting

Real data overlaps, so a road with no cars parked in it often does not exist. Soft-margin SVMs allow margin violations, including points on the wrong side of the boundary, and penalize them.

`C` sets the price:

- <strong>Small C</strong> (0.01&ndash;1) &mdash; violations are cheap. The model prefers a wide margin and tolerates mistakes. Smoother edge, more bias, less variance.
- <strong>Large C</strong> (100&ndash;10,000) &mdash; violations are costly. The model contorts the edge to classify each training point correctly. Narrow margin, low bias, high variance.

C is therefore the regularisation dial in disguise. And it is the first thing to tune. On noisy data, small C almost always wins, since insisting on classifying each training point correctly means fitting the noise.

## The kernel trick, in one picture

Some data cannot be split by any straight line. The classic case is a small circle of one class surrounded by a ring of the other &mdash; no line works, in any orientation.

Now lift each point into a third dimension, with height equal to its distance from the centre. The inner circle rises into a hill, the outer ring stays low. And a flat plane slices cleanly between them. Projected back down to two dimensions, that plane appears as a circle.

That is the whole idea. Map the data into a higher-dimensional space where a flat edge exists. And the flat edge turns a curved one back home.

The <strong>trick</strong> part is that you never really compute the mapping. The SVM needs dot products between pairs of examples. A kernel computes the dot product they would have in the higher-dimensional feature space, using the original coordinates directly. You get the gain of an enormous &mdash; sometimes infinite-dimensional &mdash; feature space at the cost of one extra function call.

|Kernel|Shape it draws|Use when|
|---|---|---|
|Linear|A straight boundary|Many features, text, very large datasets|
|RBF (Gaussian)|Smooth, closed, curved regions|The default for non-linear numeric data|
|Polynomial|Curved with a chosen degree|Interactions of a known order|
|Sigmoid|Similar to a small neural network|Rarely the best choice today|

For the RBF kernel, `gamma` controls how far each training point's influence reaches. Low gamma means broad influence and smooth edges. High gamma means each point just influences its immediate neighbourhood. It gives islands around individual points and overfits enthusiastically. Tuning an RBF SVM means searching over `C` and `gamma` together &mdash; a small grid over powers of ten is the standard way.

## Just a few rows matter

An SVM's edge is decided by the handful of points nearest to it. Delete each other row and refit -- the edge does not move.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification, make_circles
from sklearn.svm import SVC, LinearSVC
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=800, n_features=2, n_informative=2,
                           n_redundant=0, n_clusters_per_class=1,
                           class_sep=1.2, random_state=0)
svm = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0).fit(X, y)
print("trained on %d rows; %d of them are support vectors."
      % (len(X), len(svm.support_)))
print()

# refit using ONLY the support vectors
svm2 = SVC(kernel="linear", C=1.0).fit(X[svm.support_], y[svm.support_])
print("refit on those %d rows alone:" % len(svm.support_))
print("  original coefficients", np.round(svm.coef_[0], 6), "b %.6f" % svm.intercept_[0])
print("  refit coefficients   ", np.round(svm2.coef_[0], 6), "b %.6f" % svm2.intercept_[0])
print("  identical predictions on all %d rows: %s"
      % (len(X), (svm.predict(X) == svm2.predict(X)).all()))
print()
print("the other %d rows contributed nothing. no other common model has this"
      % (len(X) - len(svm.support_)))
print("property -- move a far-away point in logistic regression and the fit shifts.")
```

Next, see how the regularisation parameter C controls the trade-off between margin width and training accuracy.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=800, n_features=2, n_informative=2,
                           n_redundant=0, n_clusters_per_class=1,
                           class_sep=1.2, random_state=0)

print("C is how much you are willing to pay to avoid a mistake:")
print("%8s %10s %14s %12s" % ("C", "support", "margin width", "train acc"))
for C in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 100.0):
    m = SVC(kernel="linear", C=C).fit(X, y)
    width = 2 / np.linalg.norm(m.coef_[0])
    print("%8.2f %10d %14.4f %12.4f"
          % (C, len(m.support_), width, m.score(X, y)))
print("  small C: a wide margin, more points inside it, more tolerance.")
print("  large C: a narrow margin that bends to fit awkward points -- overfitting.")
```

Now check kernels on data that no straight line can split. And see how gamma controls overfitting with the RBF kernel.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_circles
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

Xc, yc = make_circles(n_samples=800, noise=0.22, factor=0.5, random_state=0)
print("the kernel trick, on data no line can split (one ring inside another):")
for name, m in (("linear kernel", SVC(kernel="linear")),
                ("polynomial d=3", SVC(kernel="poly", degree=3)),
                ("rbf kernel", SVC(kernel="rbf"))):
    print("  %-16s cv accuracy %.4f"
          % (name, cross_val_score(m, Xc, yc, cv=5).mean()))
print()
print("the rbf kernel never builds the higher-dimensional space it implies --")
print("it only computes distances in it. that is what makes it affordable.")
print()

print("gamma controls how far one point's influence reaches:")
print("%10s %10s %10s %10s" % ("gamma", "train", "cv", "gap"))
for g in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0, 1000.0):
    tr = SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma=g).fit(Xc, yc).score(Xc, yc)
    cv = cross_val_score(SVC(kernel="rbf", gamma=g), Xc, yc, cv=5).mean()
    print("%10.2f %10.4f %10.4f %10.4f" % (g, tr, cv, tr - cv))
print()
print("at gamma 0.01 the influence of each point is so wide that the two rings")
print("blur into one -- it is barely better than guessing, both ways.")
print("as gamma climbs, each point gets its own small island: training accuracy")
print("keeps rising while the honest score turns and falls. the gap column is")
print("the overfitting, and it is doing exactly what a gap should do.")
```

## The Kernel Trick

What if the data isn't linearly separable? This is where SVMs truly shine. The <strong>kernel trick</strong> is a powerful technique that lets SVMs create non-linear decision edges. It works by mapping the data into a higher-dimensional space where it turns linearly separable.

Imagine data points arranged in a circle, with one class inside and another outside. A straight line can't split them. But if we add a third dimension (e.g., <code>z = x² + y²</code>), we can lift the points into a 3D space where a simple plane can split them. The kernel trick reaches this without the computational cost of really transforming the data, by changing the dot product math.

## Why Use SVM?

- <strong>Effective in High-Dimensional Spaces:</strong> Works well still when the number of dimensions exceeds the number of samples.
- <strong>Memory Fast:</strong> It just uses a subset of training points (the support vectors) in the decision function.
- <strong>Versatile:</strong> Other kernel functions can be named for the decision function, making it adaptable to various data types.

## Real use, and the scaling requirement

```python
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf"))

grid = GridSearchCV(pipe, {
    "svc__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100],
    "svc__gamma": ["scale", 0.01, 0.1, 1],
}, cv=5)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)
```

The `StandardScaler` is not optional. SVMs are built on distances. So an unscaled feature with a large range dominates the kernel and the margin turns a statement about that one column. This is the most common reason an SVM "does not work".

Cost is the other real constraint. Training scales roughly between the square and the cube of the number of rows. It makes kernel SVMs impractical much beyond about 100,000 cases. For more data use `LinearSVC`, which scales far better. Or switch to a linear model trained by stochastic gradient descent.

For probabilities, note that an SVM natively outputs a signed distance from the edge, not a probability. `probability=True` fits a calibration model on top, at the cost of an internal cross-validation. And the resulting probabilities can disagree with the raw decision function near the edge.

## Where SVMs are still the right answer

- <strong>Small to medium datasets with clear margins.</strong> A few thousand rows and well-split classes is where SVMs shine and often beat all else.
- <strong>High-dimensional data.</strong> Text classification with thousands of features and fewer documents than features is a classic linear-SVM win. The margin idea copes with dimensions that make other models nervous.
- <strong>Bioinformatics.</strong> Gene expression datasets &mdash; many features, few samples &mdash; are the textbook use case.
- <strong>Novelty detection.</strong> One-class SVMs learn a edge around "normal" and flag whatever falls outside.

Where they lose: very large datasets (too slow), problems needing calibrated probabilities (not native), heavily imbalanced classes (use `class_weight="balanced"`). And cases demanding interpretability &mdash; an RBF edge is not a story anyone can tell.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Which kernel should I start with?</strong> RBF, with scaled features and a small grid over `C` and `gamma`. If the linear kernel does equally well, keep it &mdash; it is faster and more interpretable.

<strong>What does `gamma="scale"` do?</strong> It sets gamma from the number of features and the data's variance. It is a sensible default and often better than the older `"auto"`.

<strong>Can SVMs do regression?</strong> Yes &mdash; support vector regression fits a tube of width &epsilon; around the data and penalises only points outside it. This makes the method less sensitive to small errors.

<strong>Why is my SVM so slow?</strong> Almost certainly too many rows for a kernel SVM. Subsample, switch to `LinearSVC`. Or use an approximate kernel map such as `Nystroem` followed by a linear model.

<strong>How does it handle more than two classes?</strong> By training many binary classifiers &mdash; one-vs-one in scikit-learn's `SVC`. It is why training time grows quickly with the number of classes.

<strong>Do outliers hurt?</strong> Less than you might expect, since just points near the edge matter. An outlier far inside its own class often has little effect on the boundary. An outlier sitting among the other class is a support vector and does damage &mdash; reduce `C` to tolerate more margin violations.

## Recap in one screen

- The SVM picks the edge with the widest margin between the classes.
- Just the closest points &mdash; the support vectors &mdash; set it.
- `C` prices margin violations: small C for noisy data, large C to insist on perfect training accuracy.
- Kernels give curved edges by computing dot products in a higher-dimensional space without ever visiting it.
- Scale the features, tune `C` and `gamma` together. And switch to a linear model past ~100,000 rows.
