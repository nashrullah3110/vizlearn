title: Understanding Cosine Similarity
intro: Cosine similarity compares the angle between two vectors, so it can measure direction without treating a larger magnitude as greater similarity.

## Measuring similarity by angle, not distance

How do we measure how "close" two things are, especially when they are shown as lists of numeric values (vectors)? While we could measure the distance between their endpoints (Euclidean distance), <strong>Cosine Similarity</strong> offers another perspective: it measures the <strong>angle</strong> between two vectors. This simple but powerful idea is basic to modern AI, from search engines to recommendation systems. This 3D lab lets you build an intuition for what that angle measures. The formula is the dot product of the two vectors divided by the product of their magnitudes: `cos(theta) = (A . B) / (|A| |B|)`. The result ranges from +1 (identical direction), through 0 (orthogonal, no similarity), to &minus;1 (opposite directions). Because cosine divides by both magnitudes, cosine similarity ignores how large the vectors are and cares just about where they point &mdash; which is why it is the standard metric for [embeddings](../natural_language_processing/what_are_embeddings.html) and document similarity. A longer document should not automatically be considered more similar merely because its vector has a larger magnitude. Euclidean distance, by contrast, grows with magnitude. This page lets you drag two 3D vectors, shows the angle and the cosine score updating live. And covers the cases where Euclidean distance is the better choice.

## The core idea: direction matters

Imagine two arrows starting from the same point (the origin). Cosine similarity doesn't care about the length of these arrows (their "magnitude"). It just cares about the way they are pointing.

- If the vectors point in the <strong>exact same way</strong>, the angle between them is 0°. And their cosine similarity is <strong>+1</strong>. They have the same direction.
- If the vectors are <strong>perpendicular</strong> (at a 90° angle), they are considered unrelated or "orthogonal," and their cosine similarity is <strong>0</strong>.
- If the vectors point in <strong>opposite directionss</strong> (180°), they are considered diametrically opposed. And their cosine similarity is <strong>-1</strong>.

This focus on direction is why cosine similarity is so useful. For example, in text analysis, the documents "the cat sat on the mat" and "a cat was sitting on a mat" differ in length and exact wording. But their vector forms will point in similar directions, resulting in a high cosine similarity score.

## Why angle rather than distance

Consider a common use case for cosine similarity. Take two documents about football. One is a 200-word match report. The other is a 4,000-word essay on the same match. Represent each document with a word-count vector: the essay's counts are all roughly twenty times bigger.

Measure the straight-line distance between those two vectors and they look very different, since one is much longer than the other. But they are about the same thing, in the same proportions &mdash; roughly the same share of "goal", "keeper", "cost". Cosine similarity ignores the lengths and compares only direction. So the two document vectors can have a high cosine similarity.

That is the whole idea: <strong>length is magnitude; direction is what cosine compares</strong>. Cosine similarity throws away the loudness.

The output always sits between &minus;1 and 1:

- <strong>1.0</strong> &mdash; the vectors point the same way. Same mix of features, whatever the magnitudes.
- <strong>0.0</strong> &mdash; they are at right angles. The vectors are orthogonal. In text, no words in common.
- <strong>&minus;1.0</strong> &mdash; they point in just opposite directionss.

With word counts or TF-IDF vectors no value can be negative. So scores land between 0 and 1. With learned embeddings, which do have negative parts, the negative half of the range turns reachable &mdash; though in practice most unrelated pairs cluster near 0 rather than at &minus;1.

## Two vectors, worked through

Take three words as the whole vocabulary &mdash; goal, keeper, tax &mdash; and two documents:

- <strong>A</strong> = [3, 2, 0] &mdash; a short match report.
- <strong>B</strong> = [30, 20, 0] &mdash; a long essay on the same match.

The dot product is 3&times;30 + 2&times;20 + 0&times;0 = 90 + 40 = 130.

The lengths are &radic;(9 + 4 + 0) = 3.61 and &radic;(900 + 400 + 0) = 36.06.

So the similarity is 130 / (3.61 &times; 36.06) = 130 / 130.2 = <strong>1.00</strong>. Identical way, just as expected: B is A multiplied by ten.

Now add <strong>C</strong> = [0, 1, 5], a piece about tax policy that mentions a keeper once. Against A: dot product = 0 + 2 + 0 = 2. Lengths 3.61 and 5.10. Similarity = 2 / 18.4 = <strong>0.11</strong>. Almost nothing in common, which is the right answer.

Check that with Euclidean distance: A to B is 33.3, A to C is 5.9. By distance, the tax article is five times "closer" to the match report than the essay about the same match is. That single comparison is the whole argument for with cosine in text.

## Where you meet it in real systems

- <strong>Search and RAG.</strong> A question and a stored paragraph are both turned into embedding vectors. The paragraphs with the highest cosine similarity to the question are the ones fed to the language model. Each vector database you have heard of is, at heart, a machine for finding high cosine similarity quickly.
- <strong>Recommendations.</strong> Represent each user by what they watched and each film by who watched it, then recommend the films whose vectors point in a close way to the user's.
- <strong>Duplicate detection.</strong> Support tickets, product listings and news articles above about 0.9 similarity are often the same thing said twice.
- <strong>Face and voice matching.</strong> A face recogniser turns an pic into a vector. Two photographs of the same person land at similarity around 0.7&ndash;0.9, two other people much less. The system is a threshold on a cosine.
- <strong>Clustering text.</strong> Cosine distance (1 &minus; similarity) is the usual distance for grouping documents, since raw length should not decide which cluster a document joins.

## Cosine, dot product and Euclidean

|Measure|Sensitive to length?|Range|Use it when|
|---|---|---|---|
|Cosine similarity|No|&minus;1 to 1|Comparing meaning or composition, especially text|
|Dot product|Yes|Unbounded|Length carries real information, e.g. confidence or popularity|
|Euclidean distance|Yes|0 upwards|Coordinates in real space, where distance is literal|
|Manhattan distance|Yes|0 upwards|Grid-like movement, or many sparse dimensions|

Two facts worth carrying around. First, on <strong>normalised</strong> vectors &mdash; ones scaled to length 1 &mdash; the dot product and cosine similarity are the same value. It is why embedding libraries normalise on the way in and then use fast dot products. Second, on normalised vectors, ranking by cosine similarity and ranking by Euclidean distance give the same order, since squared distance = 2 &minus; 2&times;cosine. The choice just matters when the vectors are not normalised.

## In code, and the trap in it

```python
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    a, b = np.array(a, float), np.array(b, float)
    denom = np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)
    return float(a @ b / denom) if denom else 0.0   # guard the zero vector

cosine([3, 2, 0], [30, 20, 0])   # 1.0
cosine([3, 2, 0], [0, 1, 5])     # 0.108...
```

The guard on the last line is not pedantry. An empty document, a row of all zeros after filtering. Or a user who has clicked nothing all give a zero vector. And dividing by its length is a division by zero. Left unhandled it turns a `nan` that quietly poisons each average downstream.

For anything bigger than a toy, use `sklearn.metrics.pairwise.cosine_similarity`. It takes two matrices and returns each pairwise score at once. Or normalise your vectors once and use a dot product &mdash; a matrix multiply is orders of magnitude faster than a Python loop over pairs.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Is high cosine similarity the same as "means the same thing"?</strong> Just as far as the vectors are good. On raw word counts, "the match was excellent" and "the match was awful" score highly since they share three words out of four. Modern sentence embeddings handle this far better. But the similarity is always a statement about the form, not about truth.

<strong>What counts as a "high" score?</strong> It depends on the embedding model. So calibrate rather than guess. Score a few hundred pairs you know are tied and a few hundred you know are not, then look at where the two distributions split. For many sentence embedding models unrelated text still scores 0.2&ndash;0.4. So a threshold of 0.5 lets a lot of noise through.

<strong>Why do all my scores look high?</strong> Some embedding models pack all into a narrow cone of the space. So absolute values bunch up. Relative ranking still works: care about which pairs score highest, not whether the score crosses an arbitrary threshold.

<strong>Does it work with more than three dimensions?</strong> The formula is identical for 768 or 3,072 dimensions &mdash; sum the products, divide by the two lengths. Just the picture in your head stops at three.

<strong>Should I remove very common words first?</strong> With plain counts, yes: "the" appearing all over inflates each score. TF-IDF weighting does this automatically by discounting words that appear in most documents. Learned embeddings handle it internally.

## Recap in one screen

- Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their lengths.
- The formula is the dot product divided by both lengths. The result runs from &minus;1 to 1.
- It is the default for text, embeddings, search and recommendations, since a longer document should not be another document.
- On length-1 vectors it equals the dot product. And it ranks identically to Euclidean distance.
- Calibrate your threshold against known pairs, and always guard against zero-length vectors.

## How It's Calculated

The similarity score is literally the cosine of the angle ($\theta$) between the two vectors, $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$. The formula looks like this:

<code class="text-lg mono-font" style="color: var(--text-main);"> Similarity = cos($\theta$) = (A · B) / (||A|| * ||B||) </code>

Let's break that down:

<h4 class="font-bold" style="color: var(--text-main);">A · B (The Dot Product)</h4>

This measures how much the vectors "agree." You calculate it by multiplying the corresponding parts of each vector and summing the results. (A.x * B.x) + (A.y * B.y) + ... <h4 class="font-bold" style="color: var(--text-main);">||A|| and ||B|| (The Magnitudes)</h4>

This is the length of each vector, calculated with the Pythagorean theorem. By dividing by the magnitudes, we "normalize" the vectors, effectively ignoring their lengths and focusing just on their way.

## The metric that ignores how long a vector is

Cosine similarity measures angle, not distance. That single property is why it is the default for text and embeddings. And where it goes wrong.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity, euclidean_distances

a = np.array([[1.0, 2.0, 3.0]])
b = np.array([[2.0, 4.0, 6.0]])       # exactly 2x a
c = np.array([[3.0, 2.0, 1.0]])       # same length as a, different direction

print("a =", a[0], " b = 2a =", b[0], " c =", c[0])
print()
print("%-28s %12s %12s" % ("", "cosine sim", "euclidean"))
for name, v in (("a vs b (same direction)", b), ("a vs c (same length)", c)):
    print("%-28s %12.6f %12.6f"
          % (name, cosine_similarity(a, v)[0, 0], euclidean_distances(a, v)[0, 0]))
print()
print("cosine calls a and b identical. they point the same way; one is just")
print("longer. euclidean distance calls them 3.74 apart, because it measures")
print("exactly that length difference.")
print()
print("computed by hand, so there is nothing hidden:")
print("  dot(a, b)     = %.4f" % a[0].dot(b[0]))
print("  |a| = %.6f, |b| = %.6f" % (np.linalg.norm(a), np.linalg.norm(b)))
print("  dot / (|a||b|) = %.6f"
      % (a[0].dot(b[0]) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))))
```

The score ranges from +1 (same way) through 0 (perpendicular) to -1 (opposite). And text vectors that cannot go negative stay in the 0-to-1 band.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity, euclidean_distances

print("the range, and what each end means:")
for name, u, v in (("identical direction", [1, 1], [3, 3]),
                   ("perpendicular",       [1, 0], [0, 5]),
                   ("opposite",            [1, 2], [-2, -4]),
                   ("partly aligned",      [1, 0], [1, 1])):
    print("  %-22s %+.4f" % (name, cosine_similarity([u], [v])[0, 0]))
print("  -1 to +1 for arbitrary vectors. for counts and TF-IDF, which are")
print("  never negative, it is 0 to 1 -- there is no way to be 'opposite'.")
print()

print("why text uses it. two documents on the same subject, different lengths:")
short = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 2.0, 1.0, 0.0])
long_ = short * 20
other = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 9.0])
for name, v in (("the same doc, 20x longer", long_), ("a doc about cats", other)):
    print("  %-26s cosine %.6f   euclidean %8.4f"
          % (name, cosine_similarity([short], [v])[0, 0],
             euclidean_distances([short], [v])[0, 0]))
print("  euclidean would rank the cat document as the closer match, purely")
print("  because it is a similar length. cosine is not fooled.")
```

On unit-length vectors, cosine similarity and Euclidean distance give the same ranking. So a vector database can use whichever is faster.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity, euclidean_distances

print("the connection worth knowing: on unit-length vectors the two are the")
print("same ranking. euclidean^2 = 2 - 2*cosine, exactly.")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
v = rng.normal(size=(5, 8))
v = v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=1, keepdims=True)
d2 = euclidean_distances(v[:1], v[1:])[0] ** 2
cs = cosine_similarity(v[:1], v[1:])[0]
print("  euclidean^2 :", np.round(d2, 6))
print("  2 - 2*cosine:", np.round(2 - 2 * cs, 6))
print()
print("so if your vectors are normalised -- and most embedding models return")
print("them normalised -- cosine and euclidean give identical neighbours, and")
print("a vector database can use whichever is faster.")
print()
print("where it fails: when magnitude is the signal. two users who rated the")
print("same films the same way but one rated 4 films and one rated 400 look")
print("identical to cosine, and they are not the same kind of user.")
```

This is what happens in real-world applications like document similarity.

## Summing up

- <strong>It's a Measure of Way, Not Size:</strong> Cosine similarity ignores the magnitude (or length) of the vectors and just considers their orientation in space.
- <strong>The Range is [-1, 1]:</strong> A score of +1 means identical direction, 0 means they are unrelated (orthogonal). And -1 means they are exact opposites.
- <strong>Foundation for NLP:</strong> This is the primary way we measure the similarity between words, sentences. And whole documents after they have been turned into vector embeddings (like Word2Vec or BERT embeddings).
- <strong>Powers Recommendation Engines:</strong> It's used to find close users or items. If your vector of movie ratings is close to someone else's, the engine might recommend movies you haven't seen that they liked.
