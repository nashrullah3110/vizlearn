title: Gaussian Mixture Models
intro: A Gaussian mixture model represents data as a weighted combination of Gaussian components. It gives each point a probability of membership, which is useful when cluster boundaries are uncertain or groups have different shapes.

## Model data as a mixture

K-means assigns each point to one cluster. A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) instead estimates how likely each component is to have generated each point. This soft assignment preserves uncertainty, but assumes the data can be represented by a mixture of Gaussian distributions.

## The mixture model

A GMM with K components has a mean μₖ, covariance Σₖ and mixture weight πₖ for each component. The weights are non-negative and sum to one. Its density is:

<p><strong>p(x) = Σₖ πₖ 𝒩(x | μₖ, Σₖ)</strong></p>

The covariance controls each component’s shape and orientation. A full covariance can describe an ellipse; a spherical covariance restricts the component to a circle or sphere. The point’s **responsibility** rₖ is the posterior probability that component k generated it.

## How EM fits the model

Expectation–Maximisation alternates two steps. In the **E-step**, compute each point’s responsibility under the current component parameters. In the **M-step**, update the means, covariances and weights using those responsibilities. Repeat until the log-likelihood stops improving meaningfully.

For two one-dimensional components, N(0, 1) with weight 0.6 and N(3, 1) with weight 0.4, the responsibility for component one is about 0.97 at x = 0.5 and 0.60 at x = 1.5. The midpoint remains uncertain rather than being forced into a hard cluster. The visualiser shows the same pattern as you change **Overlap of the two groups** and step through **EM iterations**.

EM does not promise the global best fit. It can converge to a local optimum that depends on its initial parameters, so fit from multiple starts and compare the resulting likelihood or information criterion.

## Where GMMs differ from K-means

A GMM can represent components with different sizes and elliptical shapes, and it returns soft memberships. K-means uses hard assignments and prefers roughly spherical groups of similar scale. A GMM is useful when probabilistic membership or an elliptical model matches the data; K-means is simpler when compact, roughly spherical clusters are adequate.

Fit several component counts and compare criteria such as BIC or AIC, along with held-out fit and whether the resulting groups make sense for the task. These criteria balance fit against model complexity; they do not guarantee that the selected groups are meaningful.

## Limits and common mistakes

- **Wrong shape assumption.** Gaussian components may fit ellipses, but not arbitrary structures such as crescents without using many components.
- **Local optima.** Use several initialisations and check stability across runs.
- **Too many components.** A complex mixture can fit noise as well as structure. Compare criteria and validate the use of the clusters.
- **Near-singular covariance.** A component can collapse around a few points. Covariance regularisation can help, but does not remove the need to inspect the fit.

For implementation details and covariance options, see scikit-learn’s [Gaussian mixture documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/mixture.html). Compare with [K-means](k_means.html) and [hierarchical clustering](hierarchical_clustering.html) when choosing a clustering method.
