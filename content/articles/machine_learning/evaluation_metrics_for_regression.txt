## Overview

In regression, our goal is to predict a continuous value, like a price or a temperature. But how do we know if our model's predictions are any good? <strong>Evaluation metrics</strong> are the tools we use to measure a model's performance and quantify its error. This lab visualizes four of the most common regression metrics, allowing you to see how they respond to changes in data and model fit in real-time.

## The Four Key Metrics

<p>Each metric tells a slightly different story about your model's errors. The interactive plot above shows the true data points (green dots), the model's prediction line (orange), and the errors, or <strong>residuals</strong> (dashed red lines), which are the distances between each true point and the line.</p><h4 class="font-bold" style="color: var(--text-main);">1. Mean Absolute Error (MAE)</h4><strong>What it is:</strong> The average of the absolute differences between the true values and the predicted values. <br><strong>Formula:</strong> $ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i| $ <br><strong>Interpretation:</strong> It tells you, on average, how far off your predictions are. It's easy to understand because it's in the same units as the target variable. It treats all errors equally, big or small. <h4 class="font-bold" style="color: var(--text-main);">2. Mean Squared Error (MSE)</h4><strong>What it is:</strong> The average of the squared differences between the true and predicted values. <br><strong>Formula:</strong> $ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 $ <br><strong>Interpretation:</strong> By squaring the errors, MSE penalizes larger errors much more heavily than smaller ones. This makes it sensitive to outliers. Its units are squared, which can make it hard to interpret directly. <h4 class="font-bold" style="color: var(--text-main);">3. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)</h4><strong>What it is:</strong> The square root of the MSE. <br><strong>Formula:</strong> $ \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2} $ <br><strong>Interpretation:</strong> RMSE is the most popular metric. Like MSE, it punishes large errors, but by taking the square root, it returns the error metric to the original units of the target variable, making it more interpretable than MSE. <h4 class="font-bold" style="color: var(--text-main);">4. R-Squared ($R^2$)</h4><strong>What it is:</strong> The proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variable(s). <br><strong>Formula:</strong> $ 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2} $ <br><strong>Interpretation:</strong> $R^2$ ranges from 0 to 1. A score of 1.0 means the model perfectly explains the data's variance. A score of 0.0 means the model is no better than just predicting the mean of the target variable. A negative score means the model is even worse than that!

## Five houses, four metrics

Definitions blur together until you put numbers through them. Here are five predicted and actual house prices, in thousands of pounds:

|House|Actual|Predicted|Error|Absolute error|Squared error|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|1|200|190|&minus;10|10|100|
|2|250|265|+15|15|225|
|3|180|175|&minus;5|5|25|
|4|300|290|&minus;10|10|100|
|5|400|340|&minus;60|60|3600|

- <strong>MAE</strong> = (10 + 15 + 5 + 10 + 60) / 5 = <strong>20</strong>. On average the model is &pound;20k out.
- <strong>MSE</strong> = (100 + 225 + 25 + 100 + 3600) / 5 = <strong>810</strong>. In squared thousands of pounds, which is not a unit anyone can picture.
- <strong>RMSE</strong> = &radic;810 = <strong>28.5</strong>. Back in thousands of pounds, and noticeably larger than the MAE.
- <strong>MAPE</strong> = mean of (10/200, 15/250, 5/180, 10/300, 60/400) = mean of 5%, 6%, 2.8%, 3.3%, 15% = <strong>6.4%</strong>.

The gap between MAE 20 and RMSE 28.5 is the whole lesson. Four houses are predicted within &pound;15k; one is out by &pound;60k. Squaring turns that single house into 3600 of the 4050 total &mdash; 89% of the MSE comes from one row out of five. RMSE is loudly telling you there is a bad miss somewhere; MAE quietly averages it in.

So a rule you can use immediately: <strong>if RMSE is much larger than MAE, you have outliers.</strong> Go and look at them before you touch the model.

## R&sup2;, and what "explained variance" means

R&sup2; answers a different question from the others: not "how wrong am I?" but "how much better am I than the laziest possible model?"

That lazy model is predicting the mean of the target for every row. Compute its total squared error, compute your model's, and:

<p class="mono-font">R&sup2; = 1 &minus; (your squared error / the mean-predictor's squared error)</p>

- <strong>R&sup2; = 1</strong> &mdash; perfect predictions.
- <strong>R&sup2; = 0</strong> &mdash; exactly as good as always guessing the average. Your model has added nothing.
- <strong>R&sup2; &lt; 0</strong> &mdash; worse than guessing the average. Entirely possible on test data, and always worth investigating rather than reporting.

The trap is that R&sup2; never falls when you add a feature to a linear model, even a column of random numbers, because the extra freedom can only reduce training error. <strong>Adjusted R&sup2;</strong> corrects for this by penalising the number of predictors, and is the one to quote when comparing models with different numbers of features.

The other trap is comparing R&sup2; across datasets. An R&sup2; of 0.4 might be superb in a noisy domain like human behaviour and embarrassing in a controlled physical measurement. It is a comparison against the mean of <em>this</em> dataset, so it means nothing across two.

## Choosing by the shape of the mistake you can tolerate

Each metric encodes an opinion about which errors matter.

|Metric|Units|Outliers|Best when|
|---|---|---|---|
|MAE|Same as target|Treated like any other error|Every pound of error costs the same|
|MSE|Squared|Dominates the score|You are optimising and want smooth gradients|
|RMSE|Same as target|Heavily weighted|Large misses are disproportionately expensive|
|MAPE|Percent|Explodes near zero|Relative error matters across different scales|
|R&sup2;|None|Follows MSE|Explaining to stakeholders how much you beat the average|
|Huber loss|Same as target|Damped past a threshold|You want RMSE's behaviour without outlier tyranny|

Two practical notes. MAPE is undefined when an actual value is zero and blows up when one is near zero &mdash; for demand forecasting where zeros are common, use MAE or a symmetric variant instead. And MSE is the loss most regressors minimise internally, so evaluating with MSE tells you how well the optimiser did its stated job, not necessarily how useful the model is.

## Reporting numbers people can act on

A metric on its own is a number without a verdict. Three habits make it a decision.

<strong>Quote a baseline beside it.</strong> "RMSE 28.5" means nothing alone. "RMSE 28.5, against 61.0 for predicting the average and 34.2 for last year's model" is a result. Build the dumb baseline first, every time.

<strong>Say what the error means in the target's units.</strong> "Typically wrong by &pound;20k on a &pound;250k house" is a sentence a business owner can price. Percentages help here, which is one of the few genuine arguments for MAPE.

<strong>Plot the residuals.</strong> The single most informative thing you can do with a regression model costs two lines of code: plot predicted values against errors. A shapeless cloud around zero is healthy. A curve means the model is missing a non-linear relationship. A funnel widening to the right means the error grows with the size of the target, and predicting the logarithm often fixes it. A handful of points far from the rest are the outliers your RMSE has been complaining about.

```python
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error, mean_squared_error, r2_score
import numpy as np

y_true = [200, 250, 180, 300, 400]
y_pred = [190, 265, 175, 290, 340]

mae  = mean_absolute_error(y_true, y_pred)             # 20.0
rmse = np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_true, y_pred))     # 28.46
r2   = r2_score(y_true, y_pred)                        # 0.87

baseline = np.full_like(y_true, np.mean(y_true), dtype=float)
print(mean_absolute_error(y_true, baseline))           # the number to beat
```

## Which Metric Should You Use?

- Use <strong>MAE</strong> when you want a simple, interpretable metric that is not sensitive to outliers. For example, forecasting sales where a few large errors are acceptable.
- Use <strong>RMSE</strong> when large errors are particularly undesirable and you want to penalize them more. It's the most common metric for regression tasks. For example, predicting house prices where a large error could be very costly.
- Use <strong>$R^2$</strong> when you want to understand the proportion of variance your model explains. It's great for communicating the overall "goodness of fit" of your model to a non-technical audience.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Which metric should I optimise during training?</strong> Usually MSE or Huber, because they are smooth and differentiable. You can train on MSE and still report MAE &mdash; the training loss and the reporting metric do not have to match, and often should not.

<strong>My R&sup2; is negative on the test set. What happened?</strong> The model is doing worse than predicting the average. Common causes: severe overfitting, a distribution shift between train and test, or a preprocessing step fitted on the wrong data.

<strong>Is RMSE always bigger than MAE?</strong> Yes, or equal &mdash; they are only equal when every error has the same magnitude. The ratio between them is a quick outlier detector.

<strong>How do I compare models predicting different targets?</strong> Not with RMSE, which lives in the target's units. Use MAPE or R&sup2;, or normalise RMSE by the target's mean or range.

<strong>Should I remove outliers to improve my metrics?</strong> Only if they are genuine errors &mdash; a price of &pound;1 for a house, a sensor reading of 999. Removing real but inconvenient values makes your metric look better and your model worse in exactly the cases that matter.

<strong>Can I use accuracy for regression?</strong> Not directly, but "within &pound;10k" style tolerance accuracy is a legitimate business metric and is often the one stakeholders actually care about. Report it alongside, not instead of, a proper error measure.

## Recap in one screen

- MAE is the average miss in the target's own units; it treats all errors equally.
- MSE and RMSE square the errors, so a few big misses dominate; RMSE brings the units back.
- RMSE much larger than MAE is a reliable signal that outliers are present.
- R&sup2; compares your model to predicting the mean; below zero it has lost to the mean.
- MAPE is intuitive as a percentage but breaks near zero.
- Always report a baseline, always plot the residuals.
