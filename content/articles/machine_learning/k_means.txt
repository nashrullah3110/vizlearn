title: K-Means Clustering
intro: K-means partitions numeric points into a chosen number of groups by alternating assignment to nearby centroids and centroid updates. It works best for compact groups under the chosen distance scale.

## The two steps

Choose (k) initial centroids. **Assignment:** assign each point to its nearest centroid. **Update:** replace each centroid with the mean of its assigned points. Repeat until assignments stop changing or another stopping rule is reached.

The objective, called **inertia**, is the sum of squared distances from each point to its assigned centroid. Assignment lowers or preserves it; updating to the mean lowers or preserves it. Because the objective can have local minima, different initial centroids can produce different results. Practical implementations run multiple starts and keep the lowest-inertia solution.

## Follow a clustering

Set **Clusters (K)** and choose an **Initial Layout**. Press the step control to watch points change assignments and centroids move. Increase **Points Count** to see how the display changes as the dataset grows. Compare **Gaussian Blobs** with **Nested Moons** or **Concentric Circles**: k-means can split curved groups because each cluster is represented by a centroid and nearest-centroid regions.

## Choosing k

The elbow method plots inertia for several values of (k). Inertia always decreases as (k) increases, reaching zero if every point gets its own cluster. A bend where additional centroids yield smaller improvements can suggest a useful value, but the bend may be unclear and is not a statistical proof.

Silhouette score compares each point’s distance to its own cluster with its distance to the nearest other cluster. Higher values indicate better separation under that distance measure, but it too favors some shapes and does not establish that clusters are meaningful. Use these diagnostics with domain knowledge and, when relevant, downstream validation.

## When k-means fits

It is a reasonable baseline when features are numeric, clusters are roughly compact and similarly scaled, and assigning every point to a cluster is appropriate. Standardize features when measurement units differ. K-means is sensitive to outliers because both squared distances and means can be pulled by extreme points.

It is a poor match when clusters have very different densities or sizes, are strongly non-convex, or contain substantial noise. Consider DBSCAN for density-connected shapes and noise, or hierarchical clustering when examining multiple merge levels is useful.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating (k) as discovered:** it is supplied by the user; choose and justify it.
- **Reading lower inertia as proof of better clusters:** it almost always improves with larger (k).
- **Skipping feature scaling:** large-unit features can dominate distance.
- **Trusting one initialization:** run multiple starts and inspect stability.
