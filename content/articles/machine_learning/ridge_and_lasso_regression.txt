title: Ridge and Lasso: A Practical Guide
intro: Ridge and Lasso add coefficient penalties to least squares. Ridge shrinks coefficients; Lasso can set some to zero and perform feature selection.

## Quick Context

Give a degree-12 polynomial eighteen noisy points and it will pass through nearly all of them. The fit looks superb on the data it has seen. And it is worthless: between the points the curve swings violently, since the just way to hit each point is to use enormous coefficients that cancel each other out.

Regularisation attacks this right. Instead of asking the model just to fit the data, you ask it to fit the data <em>and</em> keep its coefficients small. And you decide how much the second part matters. Ridge (L2) adds the sum of squared weights to the loss. It shrinks each coefficient toward zero but never all the way. Lasso (L1) adds the sum of absolute weights. It can drive coefficients just to zero &mdash; doing feature choice automatically. The hyperparameter lambda controls the cost's strength: too small and the model overfits as before, too large and it underfits by flattening all coefficients. Elastic Net mixes both costs with a mixing ratio, giving you a knob between ridge's stability and lasso's sparsity. The geometric intuition is that L2's constraint region is a circle (all ways shrink equally) while L1's is a diamond (coefficients hit the axes and snap to zero). Cross-validation picks lambda &mdash; scikit-learn's `RidgeCV` and `LassoCV` do this in one call. This page fits both models on noisy polynomial data, prints the coefficients and shows which ones lasso zeroed out. And covers the Elastic Net compromise.

## One extra term

Ordinary least squares minimises the squared error alone. Ridge and Lasso add a cost on the size of the weights:

Ridge: &nbsp; minimise &nbsp; SSE + &lambda; &middot; &Sigma; w<sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup>

Lasso: &nbsp; minimise &nbsp; SSE + &lambda; &middot; &Sigma; |w<sub>i</sub>|

&lambda; (lambda) is the dial. At &lambda; = 0 both reduce just to OLS. As &lambda; grows the cost dominates and each coefficient is pushed toward zero, until at very large &lambda; the model gives up and predicts a flat line through the mean.

The just gap between the two is whether you square the weights or take their absolute value. That one change has a consequence out of all proportion to its size.

## Why Lasso zeroes and Ridge does not

Look at how each cost behaves as a coefficient ways zero. The derivative of <span class="mono-font">w<sup>2</sup></span> is <span class="mono-font">2w</span>. It it goes to zero &mdash; so the closer a Ridge coefficient gets to zero, the weaker the force pushing it more. It ways zero and never arrives.

The derivative of <span class="mono-font">|w|</span> is a constant &plusmn;1, no matter how small w is. Lasso pushes with the same strength all the way down. So a coefficient whose contribution to the fit is worth less than that constant push gets driven to <em>just</em> zero and stays there.

That is why Lasso does feature choice and Ridge does not. Watch the coefficient bars above: under Ridge they all shrink together and stay non-zero. Under Lasso they vanish one by one.

## Why a cost helps at all

Plain least squares has one instruction: make the training errors as small as possible. With many features, or with features that are nearly duplicates of each other, it will do just that by giving some enormous coefficients that happen to cancel each other out &mdash; +4,300 on one column, &minus;4,290 on a near-copy.

Those coefficients fit the training data beautifully and mean nothing. Move one input slightly and the prediction swings wildly, since two large coefficients are no longer cancelling.

Regularisation adds a second instruction: <em>and keep the coefficients small</em>. The model now minimises

<p class="mono-font">error on the training data &nbsp;+&nbsp; &lambda; &times; (size of the coefficients)</p>

&lambda; (called `alpha` in scikit-learn) sets the exchange rate between the two. At &lambda; = 0 you are back to ordinary least squares. As &lambda; grows, the model turns progressively less willing to buy a small error reduction with a large coefficient. And at very large &lambda; each coefficient is pushed to nearly zero and the model predicts the mean.

Deliberately accepting some bias to remove a lot of variance is the whole trade. And on data with many correlated features it is nearly always worth making.

## The one gap that changes all

The two ways differ just in how "size of the coefficients" is measured.

- <strong>Ridge (L2)</strong> penalises the sum of <em>squared</em> coefficients.
- <strong>Lasso (L1)</strong> penalises the sum of <em>absolute</em> coefficients.

That small change gives a large behavioural gap. Squaring means the cost on a coefficient of 0.1 is 0.01 &mdash; almost nothing &mdash; so ridge has very little incentive to push a small coefficient all the way to zero. It shrinks all smoothly and keeps each feature.

With absolute values, the cost on 0.1 is 0.1. And the penalty exerts constant pressure toward zero as the pressure on any other coefficient. So lasso pushes weak coefficients to just zero. And the features attached to them drop out of the model entirely.

That makes lasso a feature selector as well as a regulariser. Fit it with a moderate alpha on 200 columns and you may be left with 30 non-zero coefficients and a model you can really read.

|&nbsp;|Ridge (L2)|Lasso (L1)|
|---|---|---|
|Penalty|Sum of squares|Sum of absolute values|
|Coefficients|Shrunk, never exactly zero|Some become exactly zero|
|Feature selection|No|Yes|
|Correlated features|Shares weight between them|Picks one, drops the rest|
|Solution|Closed form exists|Iterative|
|Use when|All features plausibly matter|You suspect many are useless|

The correlated-features row is the one that decides most real cases. Ridge splits the weight across a group of correlated columns. It is stable and sensible. Lasso arbitrarily keeps one and zeroes the others. And which one it keeps can change with a small change in the data &mdash; unstable, though the predictions stay close.

<strong>Elastic Net</strong> uses both costs with a mixing ratio. And it is the standard answer when you want lasso's sparsity without its instability on correlated groups.

## Two penalties, two different coefficient patterns

Ridge shrinks each coefficient toward zero. Lasso sends most of them just to zero. Watching both on the same correlated data shows why that gap matters.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, p = 120, 60
X = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
X[:, 1] = X[:, 0] + rng.normal(0, 0.05, n)     # a near-duplicate column
true = np.zeros(p); true[[0, 5, 12, 30]] = [4.0, -3.0, 2.5, 1.5]
y = X @ true + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n)
print("%d rows, %d columns, and only 4 of those columns matter." % (n, p))
print()

def report(name, model):
    m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), model)
    cv = cross_val_score(m, X, y, cv=5).mean()
    m.fit(X, y)
    coef = m[-1].coef_
    nz = (np.abs(coef) > 1e-6).sum()
    print("%-22s cv R2 %7.4f   nonzero %3d/%d   largest |coef| %7.3f"
          % (name, cv, nz, p, np.abs(coef).max()))
    return coef
```

## Guided tour

1. <strong>See the problem first.</strong> Set the <strong>Cost Type</strong> to None (plain OLS) and set the <strong>Polynomial Degree</strong> slider to 12. The curve whips between the training points, train MSE is almost zero. And test MSE is enormous. That gap is overfitting, made visible.
2. <strong>Apply a little Ridge.</strong> Switch <strong>Cost Type</strong> to Ridge (L2) and set the <strong>Lambda</strong> slider to -3. The curve calms down at once and test MSE drops, while train MSE rises slightly. That trade is the whole point of regularisation.
3. <strong>Overdo it.</strong> Set the <strong>Lambda</strong> slider to its max, 2. The model is now so heavily penalised that it flattens toward a straight line and underfits &mdash; both errors climb together. Regularisation is a dial, not a switch.
4. <strong>Watch Lasso delete.</strong> Switch <strong>Cost Type</strong> to Lasso (L1) and set the <strong>Lambda</strong> slider to -1. Count the grey bars: coefficients have gone to just zero. And the non-zero count falls. Ridge at the same lambda leaves each bar standing.
5. <strong>Starve it of data.</strong> Set the <strong>Training Points</strong> slider to 8 with degree 12 and no cost. With fewer points than parameters the fit is unstable. Add the cost back and it turns usable again &mdash; this is why regularisation matters most when data is scarce.
6. <strong>Remove the noise.</strong> Set the <strong>Noise</strong> slider to 0. Now the high-degree fit is fine without any cost, since there is no noise left to memorise. Overfitting is a response to noise, not to flexibility on its own.

## Choosing between them

- <strong>Ridge</strong> when you believe most features carry some signal. And especially when features are correlated. Ridge spreads weight across a correlated group rather than picking one arbitrarily.
- <strong>Lasso</strong> when you suspect most features are irrelevant and you want the model to tell you which. The zeros are the output you care about, as much as the predictions are.
- <strong>Elastic Net</strong>, which adds both costs, when you want Lasso's choice but have correlated groups that Lasso would otherwise split arbitrarily.

In practice &lambda; is not chosen by eye. It is selected by <a href="cross_validation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">cross-validation</a>: fit at many values, keep the one with the best held-out error.

## Failure modes

- <strong>Forgetting to scale first.</strong> The cost acts on raw coefficient magnitudes. And a coefficient's size depends on its feature's units. A feature measured in metres gets a coefficient a thousand times more than the same feature in millimetres. So it absorbs a thousand times more cost. Standardise before regularising; this is not optional.
- <strong>Penalising the intercept.</strong> The bias term should not be shrunk &mdash; doing so drags predictions toward zero rather than toward the mean. Each good build excludes it. If you write your own, remember to.
- <strong>Reading Lasso's zeros as truth.</strong> With correlated features Lasso picks one from a group more or less arbitrarily. And a small change in the data can flip which one survives. Its choice is useful, not stable.
- <strong>Tuning lambda on the test set.</strong> That makes the test score optimistic and no longer an estimate of anything. Use a validation split or cross-validation. And keep the test set for the end.

## Where that leaves you

Both ways add a cost on coefficient size to the loss, trading a little training accuracy for a lot of stability, with lambda controlling how hard that trade is pushed. Ridge squares the weights. So its pressure fades as a coefficient nears zero and all coefficients shrink toward zero. Lasso uses absolute values. So its pressure stays constant and weak coefficients are driven to just zero. It makes it a feature selector as well as a regulariser. Scale your features first, never penalise the intercept. And pick lambda by cross-validation rather than by eye.

## Choosing alpha, and the scaling requirement

Alpha is not a setting you reason your way to &mdash; it is chosen by cross-validation. And scikit-learn gives estimators that do just that.

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV, LassoCV
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
import numpy as np

alphas = np.logspace(-3, 3, 25)      # 0.001 to 1000, log-spaced

ridge = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV(alphas=alphas, cv=5))
lasso = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(alphas=alphas, cv=5))

ridge.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(ridge[-1].alpha_)               # the value cross-validation chose
```

Search alpha on a logarithmic scale &mdash; the interesting range spans orders of magnitude. And a linear grid wastes almost all its points.

<strong>Scaling is mandatory.</strong> The cost is applied to raw coefficient values. So a feature measured in thousands naturally has a tiny coefficient and is barely penalised, while a feature measured in units has a large one and is penalised heavily. Standardise first and the cost falls evenly. Skipping this is the most common way to get nonsense out of a regularised model.

Note also that the intercept is not penalised. And should not be &mdash; shrinking it towards zero would mean insisting the target is centred at zero.

## Reading the coefficient path

The most informative plot in this topic is the coefficient path: alpha on a log x-axis, coefficient values on the y-axis, one line per feature.

For ridge, the lines converge smoothly towards zero as alpha grows. But never touch it. For lasso, lines hit zero one after another and stay there &mdash; and the order in which they drop out is a ranking of how much each feature was adding. The last few survivors are the features carrying the signal.

Two things to look for. A coefficient that changes sign as alpha varies is a warning about collinearity, not a finding. And a large gap between the alpha with the lowest cross-validation error and the "one standard error" alpha &mdash; the largest alpha within one standard error of the best &mdash; is a chance to buy a much simpler model for almost no accuracy. That heuristic is standard practice in statistics and underused elsewhere.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Which should I use by default?</strong> Ridge, if you have no strong reason to prefer otherwise. It is stable, has a closed-form solution. And does not throw away information. Move to lasso when you namely want a smaller feature set. And to Elastic Net when you want both.

<strong>Does regularisation work with logistic regression?</strong> Yes, identically. And scikit-learn applies L2 by default with `C = 1/alpha`. All here transfers.

<strong>Can lasso select more features than there are rows?</strong> No &mdash; lasso can select at most n features from a dataset with n rows. Elastic Net does not have this limitation. It is another reason it is preferred in wide, short datasets such as genomics.

<strong>Should I trust lasso's feature choice?</strong> As a ranking, broadly. As a definitive statement about which features matter, no &mdash; refit with other random splits and see how stable the selected set is. Stability choice formalises this.

<strong>Is regularisation the same as feature choice?</strong> Lasso happens to do both. Ridge does not select anything. It keeps each feature and reduces their influence.

<strong>What if alpha comes out at in effect zero?</strong> Then your data does not need regularisation &mdash; plenty of rows, few features, low collinearity. That is a legitimate outcome and worth noticing.

## Recap in one screen

- Both ways add a cost on coefficient size to the usual squared-error objective.
- Ridge squares the coefficients: smooth shrinkage, nothing reaches zero, correlated features share the weight.
- Lasso uses absolute values: weak coefficients hit just zero. So the model selects features.
- Elastic Net mixes the two and is the safe default on correlated, wide data.
- Standardise your features, search alpha on a log scale by cross-validation. And read the coefficient path.
