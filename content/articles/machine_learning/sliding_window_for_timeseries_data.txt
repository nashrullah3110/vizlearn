title: Sliding Window for Time Series: A Practical Guide
intro: Supervised learning needs (input, target) pairs and a time series is just one long sequence. A sliding window manufactures the pairs - and the way you split them afterwards is where most time-series projects go wrong.

## Turning a sequence into a supervised problem

A net needs cases with features and a label. A time series is a single ordered run of vals. So you build cases by sliding a fixed-length window along it: the vals inside the window are the input. And the value at once after it is the target.

With the series <span class="mono-font">[10, 12, 15, 13, 18, 20, 19]</span> and a window of 3:

[10, 12, 15] &rarr; 13<br>[12, 15, 13] &rarr; 18<br>[15, 13, 18] &rarr; 20<br>[13, 18, 20] &rarr; 19

Seven observations turn four training cases. In general a series of length n with window W and stride S yields <span class="mono-font">&lfloor;(n &minus; W) / S&rfloor; + 1</span> cases &mdash; so the window costs you W observations’ worth of data before you get anything at all.

This page covers how to choose the window size and stride, how to split the resulting cases without leaking future data into training. And the multi-step forecasting variant where the target is a sequence rather than a single value. The visualisation above lets you drag the window across a real time series and watch the (input, target) pairs appear. The most common mistake &mdash; shuffling windowed rows as if they were independent &mdash; is showed with code that measures how badly it inflates the score.

## Window size and stride

<strong>Window size (W)</strong> is how much history the net sees per prediction. And it is a real modelling assumption: it asserts that nothing older than W steps matters. Too small and the net cannot see the pattern &mdash; a weekly cycle needs at least seven daily steps. Too large and each case carries mostly irrelevant history, the input dimension grows. And the count of cases shrinks.<strong>Stride (S)</strong> is how far the window jumps between cases. A stride of 1 gives the max count of cases, heavily overlapping and therefore highly correlated. A more stride gives fewer, more independent cases. Stride 1 is the usual choice for training. More strides are used to cut redundancy on very long series.

## Turning a timeline into a table

Machine learning nets expect a table: one row per case, one column per feature. And rows that do not depend on each other. A time series is none of those things &mdash; it is one long sequence where each value depends on the ones before it.

The sliding window is the change. Pick a window length, slide it along the series. And each position turns one row of an ordinary supervised learning problem.

With daily sales [10, 12, 15, 13, 18, 20, 22] and a window of 3:

|Row|Features (past 3 days)|Target (next day)|
|---|---|---|
|1|10, 12, 15|13|
|2|12, 15, 13|18|
|3|15, 13, 18|20|
|4|13, 18, 20|22|

Seven counts have turn four training cases with three features each. From here, any tabular net will do &mdash; linear regression, random forest, gradient boosting &mdash; and none of them need to know anything about time.

Two immediate consequences. You lose the first `window` observations, since there is no complete history for them. And consecutive rows overlap heavily. So they are not independent &mdash; which is just why the usual random train/test split is invalid here.

## Choosing the window and the horizon

Two counts set the setup, and they answer other questions.

<strong>Window size (lookback)</strong> &mdash; how much history each prediction sees. Too short and the net cannot see the pattern: a window of 3 on daily data cannot represent a weekly cycle. Too long and you have many features, fewer rows. And a lot of ancient history diluting recent signal.

Start from the domain. Daily data with weekly seasonality wants at least 7, often 14. Hourly data with a daily cycle wants at least 24. Then tune around that starting point. And let the data confirm it &mdash; an autocorrelation plot shows right how far back the useful dependence reaches.

<strong>Horizon</strong> &mdash; how far ahead you predict. One step is easiest and most exact. For multiple steps there are three plans:

- <strong>Recursive.</strong> Predict one step, feed it back in as an input, predict the next. Simple, and errors compound quickly.
- <strong>Direct.</strong> Train a split net for each horizon &mdash; one for t+1, one for t+2. And so on. More nets, no error compounding.
- <strong>Multi-output.</strong> One net predicting the whole horizon vector at once. Fast, and it can learn the relationships between the steps.

<strong>Stride</strong> is the third, quieter setting: how far the window moves each time. A stride of 1 extracts each possible window and maximises the count of training rows. More strides reduce overlap and training time, at the cost of fewer cases.

## Splitting time series without cheating

Shuffling a windowed dataset is the single most common mistake in this topic. And it gives beautiful, worthless results.

The reason is direct: window 1 holds days 1&ndash;3 and window 2 holds days 2&ndash;4. Put one in training and the other in test. And the test row shares two-thirds of its inputs with a training row. Worse, a shuffled split lets the net train on next month while being tested on this month. It is a capability it will never have in production.

The correct way is a chronological split &mdash; train on the earliest portion, validate on the middle, test on the most recent &mdash; and for cross-validation, an expanding or rolling window:

|Fold|Train on|Test on|
|---|---|---|
|1|Months 1&ndash;6|Month 7|
|2|Months 1&ndash;7|Month 8|
|3|Months 1&ndash;8|Month 9|

Scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` builds just this. Add a small <strong>gap</strong> between the end of training and the start of testing when your windows overlap the edge. So no test window shares observations with a training window.

The same discipline applies to scaling: fit the scaler on the training period just. Fitting it on the whole series leaks the future's mean and standard deviation into the past.

## Interactive Exploration Guide

1. <strong>Watch the window slide.</strong> Set <strong>Window Size (W)</strong> to 3 and <strong>Stride (S)</strong> to 1, then scroll along the series. Each position gives one training case. And consecutive cases share almost all their vals.
2. <strong>Widen the window.</strong> Raise <strong>Window Size (W)</strong> to 10. Each case carries more history. And there are fewer of them &mdash; the trade is visible right in the count.
3. <strong>Increase the stride.</strong> Set <strong>Stride (S)</strong> to 5. The windows stop overlapping heavily and the case count drops sharply. But each one is far more independent of the last.
4. <strong>Change the data.</strong> Press <strong>Random Series</strong> and try a window shorter than the visible cycle, then longer. A window that cannot span one period of the pattern cannot represent it.

## Splitting without leaking

This is the part that matters most and is most often got wrong. Random train-test splitting is <em>invalid</em> for time series: it puts future windows in the training set and past windows in the test set. So the net is trained on the future to predict the past.

Scores from that setup look excellent and mean nothing. Split <strong>chronologically</strong> &mdash; train on the earliest portion, validate on the middle, test on the most recent &mdash; so the test mirrors how the net will really be used.

Overlapping windows add a second leak at the edge: the last training window and the first test window share observations. Leave a gap of at least W steps between the splits.

The same applies to scaling. Fit the scaler on the training period just. Computing a mean over the whole series leaks future information into each training case.

## Traps worth knowing

- <strong>Random shuffling before the split.</strong> Guarantees leakage and a score that will not survive deployment.
- <strong>No gap between train and test.</strong> Overlapping windows straddle the edge and share data.
- <strong>Scaling on the full series.</strong> The training data learns statistics that include the test period.
- <strong>Ignoring non-stationarity.</strong> If the mean or variance drifts over time, a net fitted on early data may not transfer. Gap the series or net the trend clearly.
- <strong>Predicting one step and reporting it as multi-step.</strong> Errors compound when a net’s own predictions are fed back as inputs. Test the horizon you really need.

## Key takeaway

A sliding window turns a time series into supervised (input, target) pairs, with the window size encoding how much history you claim is relevant and the stride controlling how much consecutive cases overlap. The modelling choice is straightforward. The discipline is in the split &mdash; chronological, with a gap of at least one window between segments. And each scaler fitted on the training period alone.

## Features worth adding to the window

The raw lagged vals are the starting point, not the finished feature set. Three additions often earn their place:

<strong>Rolling statistics.</strong> The mean, standard deviation, min and max over the window summarise its level and volatility in a few stable counts. A rolling mean over 7 days is often a stronger feature than seven individual lags.

<strong>Calendar features.</strong> Day of week, month, hour, and a holiday flag. Most human-made series have strong calendar structure that lags alone represent inefficiently. Encode cyclical vals as sine and cosine pairs so that hour 23 and hour 0 are next rather than 23 units apart.

<strong>Gaps and ratios.</strong> The change from the last value. Or the ratio to the value a week ago, makes trends explicit. For series with a trend this often matters more than anything else, since tree nets cannot extrapolate: a forest trained on vals between 100 and 200 will never predict 250. But it can predict "a 5% increase" from a differenced target.

```python
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({"sales": series})
for lag in range(1, 8):
    df[f"lag_{lag}"] = df["sales"].shift(lag)

df["roll_mean_7"] = df["sales"].shift(1).rolling(7).mean()
df["roll_std_7"]  = df["sales"].shift(1).rolling(7).std()
df["dow"]         = df.index.dayofweek
df["target"]      = df["sales"].shift(-1)      # next day
df = df.dropna()
```

The `.shift(1)` before `.rolling()` is the key detail. Without it, the rolling mean at time t includes the value at time t &mdash; the very thing you are trying to predict. That is a leak, it is easy to write by accident. And it gives suspiciously excellent results.

## Where this way fits

Windowing plus a gradient-boosted net is a strong, unglamorous baseline for most forecasting problems. And it often beats deep sequence nets on business data with limited history.

It is the right tool for demand and sales forecasting, sensor and IoT prediction, predictive maintenance (windows of vibration or temperature readings), anomaly detection over rolling statistics. And energy load forecasting.

It is the wrong tool when the sequence is very long and the dependencies are distant &mdash; a fixed window simply cannot see beyond its own length. It is where recurrent networks and transformers earn their keep. It also handles irregular sampling poorly: windows assume evenly spaced observations. So resample first if your timestamps are ragged.

## Turning a series into rows, without cheating

A series is cut into overlapping windows so a supervised net can eat it. Those windows overlap, and that overlap is how a shuffled split quietly hands the net its own answers.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit, KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 900
# a random walk with a cycle on top -- non-stationary, like most real series
series = 50 + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)) + 6 * np.sin(np.arange(n) / 11.0)
print("a series of %d points: a random walk with a cycle on top." % n)
print("first eight values:", np.round(series[:8], 2))
print()

def windows(s, lookback, horizon=1):
    X = np.array([s[i:i + lookback] for i in range(len(s) - lookback - horizon + 1)])
    y = np.array([s[i + lookback + horizon - 1] for i in range(len(X))])
    return X, y

X, y = windows(series, lookback=10)
print("with lookback=10, horizon=1:")
print("  row 0 features:", np.round(X[0], 2))
print("  row 0 target  :", round(y[0], 2), " (that is series[10])")
print("  row 1 features:", np.round(X[1], 2))
print("  %d points became %d rows of %d columns." % (n, len(X), X.shape[1]))
print("  consecutive rows share 9 of their 10 values. they are near-duplicates,")
print("  and that is where every mistake below comes from.")
print()

split = int(0.8 * len(X))
print("%-34s %12s %12s" % ("", "ridge MAE", "forest MAE"))
```

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit, KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

def evaluate(label, Xa, ya):
    out = []
    for mk in (lambda: Ridge(),
               lambda: RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=80, random_state=0)):
        m = mk().fit(Xa[:split], ya[:split])
        out.append(mean_absolute_error(ya[split:], m.predict(Xa[split:])))
    print("%-34s %12.4f %12.4f" % (label, out[0], out[1]))
    return out

honest = evaluate("chronological split (correct)", X, y)
idx = rng.permutation(len(X))
cheat = evaluate("shuffled split (cheating)", X[idx], y[idx])
print()
print("the forest went from %.4f to %.4f -- it looks %.1fx better purely from"
      % (honest[1], cheat[1], honest[1] / cheat[1]))
print("the shuffle. a shuffled test row shares 9 of its 10 values with training")
print("rows either side of it in time, and a forest is flexible enough to look")
print("that neighbour up rather than learn anything.")
print()
print("note that ridge barely moved (%.4f to %.4f). the leak is real either"
      % (honest[0], cheat[0]))
print("way; a linear model just is not able to exploit it. so 'my score did")
print("not change when I shuffled' is not evidence that the split was safe.")
print()
```

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit, KFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.metrics import mean_absolute_error

print("the same trap in cross-validation:")
for label, cv in (("KFold(5, shuffled)", KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)),
                  ("TimeSeriesSplit(5)", TimeSeriesSplit(5))):
    s = -cross_val_score(RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=80, random_state=0),
                         X, y, cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error").mean()
    print("  %-22s forest MAE %.4f" % (label, s))
print("  TimeSeriesSplit only trains on rows that come BEFORE the fold it")
print("  tests on, and the training window grows:")
for i, (a, b) in enumerate(TimeSeriesSplit(5).split(X)):
    print("     fold %d: train rows 0-%d, test rows %d-%d" % (i, a[-1], b[0], b[-1]))
print()

print("the baseline you have to beat -- just predict the last value you saw:")
print("  naive           MAE %.4f" % mean_absolute_error(y[split:], X[split:, -1]))
print("  ridge, honest   MAE %.4f" % honest[0])
print("  forest, honest  MAE %.4f" % honest[1])
print("  on a random walk the naive forecast is genuinely hard to beat, which")

```python-run
print()

print("lookback is a real hyperparameter. tune it on time-ordered folds:")
print("%10s %14s" % ("lookback", "ridge MAE"))
for lb in (1, 3, 10, 30, 60):
    Xl, yl = windows(series, lookback=lb)
    print("%10d %14.4f"
          % (lb, -cross_val_score(Ridge(), Xl, yl, cv=TimeSeriesSplit(4),
                                  scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error").mean()))
print()

print("and the horizon changes the problem completely:")
for h in (1, 5, 20):
    Xh, yh = windows(series, lookback=20, horizon=h)
    sp = int(0.8 * len(Xh))
    m = Ridge().fit(Xh[:sp], yh[:sp])
    print("  %2d step(s) ahead: ridge MAE %.4f   naive MAE %.4f"
          % (h, mean_absolute_error(yh[sp:], m.predict(Xh[sp:])),
             mean_absolute_error(yh[sp:], Xh[sp:, -1])))
print("  a model trained for horizon 1 is not a model for horizon 20.")
print()
print("one more leak with no code to show for it: scaling. fitting a scaler on")
print("the whole series puts the future's mean and standard deviation into")
print("your training rows. fit it on the training rows only, inside a pipeline.")
```

## Questions people ask

<strong>How do I choose the window size?</strong> Start from the known seasonality, check an autocorrelation plot, then tune it as a hyperparameter with time-series cross-validation.

<strong>Can I use a random forest for forecasting?</strong> Yes, on windowed features, with one caveat: tree nets cannot predict outside the range of their training targets. For a trending series, net the gap rather than the level.

<strong>What about missing timestamps?</strong> Resample to a regular frequency first and decide clearly how to fill gaps &mdash; forward fill, interpolate. Or flag them as missing. Silently skipping them corrupts each window that spans the gap.

<strong>Should the target be scaled?</strong> Often not needed for tree nets. For neural networks, yes &mdash; and remember to invert the scaling before reporting errors. Or your RMSE will be in the wrong units.

<strong>How many rows do I need?</strong> Enough to cover a few full seasonal cycles. Two years of daily data to learn annual seasonality. A few weeks is not enough to learn anything about December.

<strong>Do I need a split net per series?</strong> Not necessarily. Training one net across many tied series, with an identifier feature, often beats hundreds of individual nets &mdash; the shared net borrows strength from series with more history.

## Recap in one screen

- A sliding window turns a sequence into a normal feature/target table.
- Window size is how far back the net sees. Horizon is how far ahead it predicts. Stride is how far the window moves.
- Never shuffle: split chronologically, use `TimeSeriesSplit`. And leave a gap when windows overlap.
- Add rolling statistics, calendar features and gaps &mdash; and shift before rolling. Or you leak the target.
- Fixed windows cannot see beyond their own length. That is where sequence nets take over.
