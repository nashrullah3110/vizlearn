title: Understanding Linear Regression
intro: Ordinary least squares fits a linear relationship by minimizing the sum of squared residuals. The residual pattern and assumptions matter as much as the fitted coefficients.

## Finding the best straight line through data

Linear regression models the relationship between a predictor *x* and a response *y* as a straight line: <code class="font-bold mono-font" style="color: var(--text-main);">y = mx + c</code>. Here *m* is the slope and *c* is the y-intercept. **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)** finds the values of *m* and *c* that minimise the sum of squared residuals &mdash; the vertical distances between each data point and the line. Since the loss function (mean squared error) is a convex quadratic in the two parameters, OLS has a closed-form solution rather than needing iterative search: compute two partial derivatives, set them to zero, and solve a pair of linear equations. The solution generalises to multiple predictors as the normal-equation solution <code>(X&#7488;X)&#8315;&sup1;X&#7488;y</code>. It is how scikit-learn's `LinearRegression` works under the hood. Gradient descent reaches the same answer by iterating. And it scales better when the feature count is very large or when the data does not fit in memory. The residuals &mdash; the vertical gaps the loss measures &mdash; carry diagnostic information after fitting: curves, widening fans, or isolated clusters can signal that a straight line is the wrong model, that variance changes with the predictor, or that outliers are distorting the fit. This page shows the regression line updating as you drag data points, plots the residuals, displays the loss surface as a function of *m* and *c*, and walks through the OLS derivation step by step.

## What Does "Best Fit" Mean? The Loss Function

How do we know which line is the "best"? We measure its error. For each data point, the vertical distance between the point and our line is called the <strong>residual</strong> or <strong>error</strong>. These are the red lines in the visualization. A good line should have small residuals.

To measure the total error for the whole dataset, we use a <strong>loss function</strong>. A common and effective one is the <strong>Mean Squared Error (MSE)</strong>. Here's how it works:

1. For each data point, calculate the residual (error).
2. Square each residual. We do this so that negative and positive errors don't cancel each other out. And it also penalizes more errors more heavily.
3. Calculate the average of all these squared residuals.

The "best" line is the one with the lowest possible MSE. Your goal is to find the values of 'm' and 'c' that cut this loss.

## Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)

While you can manually drag the line to try and find the best fit, there's a direct mathematical way to find the perfect 'm' and 'c'. This way is called <strong>Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)</strong>. It's a formula that takes all the data points and instantly calculates the slope and intercept that guarantee the min possible Mean Squared Error. When you click the "Snap to Best Fit" button, you are seeing the OLS solution.

## What the line is really claiming

A fitted line is a claim in a sentence: "for each extra unit of x, y goes up by m &mdash; and when x is zero, y is c."

Fit house price against floor area and you might get price = 2.8 &times; area + 45. Read it back: each extra square metre is worth &pound;2,800. And the model's starting point for a hypothetical zero-area house is &pound;45,000. The first half is a genuinely useful business fact. The second half is a mathematical convenience &mdash; there are no zero-area houses. And the intercept is just where the line happens to cross the axis when extended beyond the data.

That distinction matters constantly. A slope is often interpretable. An intercept often is not, unless zero is a real, observed value of x.

With a few features the equation grows terms but not ideas:

<p class="mono-font">price = 2.8&times;area + 12.5&times;bedrooms &minus; 0.9&times;age + 30</p>

Each coefficient now means "the effect of this feature <em>holding the others constant</em>". Adding a bedroom is worth &pound;12,500 for a house of the same floor area &mdash; which is another and more careful statement than "houses with more bedrooms cost more".

## Why squares, and not just distances

"Best fit" needs a definition before it can be computed. The obvious one &mdash; add up how far each point is from the line &mdash; turns out to be the wrong choice for three reasons.

Errors above and below the line cancel out if you add them raw. So a wildly wrong line through the middle scores zero. Absolute values fix that but have a corner at zero. It makes the calculus awkward and the solution not unique. Squares fix both: always positive, smooth all over, and differentiable.

Squaring also does something with real consequences: <strong>it punishes big misses disproportionately</strong>. Two points off by 5 add 50. One point off by 10 adds 100. The fitted line will contort it to reduce a single large error. It is why one outlier can visibly tilt a regression line and why you should always look at a scatter plot before trusting the coefficients.

There is a deeper justification too. If errors around the true line are normally distributed, then the least-squares line is just the max-likelihood estimate &mdash; the line that makes the observed data most probable. That is why this particular definition of "best" has stuck for two centuries.

## OLS has a formula, and gradient descent does not need it

Ordinary least squares is unusual among fitting procedures: the answer can be written down right rather than searched for.

For a single feature, the slope and intercept come out as:

<p class="mono-font">m = &Sigma;(x &minus; x&#772;)(y &minus; y&#772;) / &Sigma;(x &minus; x&#772;)&sup2; &nbsp;&nbsp; c = y&#772; &minus; m&thinsp;x&#772;</p>

Two facts fall out of the second equation. The line always passes through the point (x&#772;, y&#772;) &mdash; the mean of both vars. And once you know the slope, the intercept costs nothing to compute.

In matrix form for any number of features it turns the normal equation, <span class="mono-font">&beta; = (X&#7488;X)&#8315;&sup1;X&#7488;y</span> &mdash; one math, no iteration, no learning rate. And the answer is exact.

So why does anyone use gradient descent? Since inverting X&#7488;X costs roughly the cube of the number of features. And it fails outright when features are perfectly correlated, which makes the matrix non-invertible. Below a few thousand features, use the closed form. For very large feature sets, iterative methods may be more practical, especially when the data does not fit in memory.

```python
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)
print(model.coef_, model.intercept_)     # the slopes and the c
print(model.score(X, y))                 # R-squared on the training data
```

## The assumptions, and how to tell they are broken

Least squares always returns a line. Whether that line means anything rests on four assumptions, each with a visible symptom when it fails.

|Assumption|What it means|How it fails visibly|
|---|---|---|
|Linearity|The real relationship is a straight line|Residuals form a curve, not a cloud|
|Independence|Errors are unrelated to each other|Residuals follow a pattern over time|
|Equal variance|Spread of errors is constant across x|Residual plot fans out like a funnel|
|Normal errors|Errors are roughly bell-shaped|Heavy tails on a Q-Q plot|

The single most valuable diagnostic is the residual plot: predicted values on the x-axis, errors on the y-axis. A shapeless band around zero means the assumptions are holding well enough. A curve means you need a transformed or extra feature. A funnel means the error grows with the prediction. And modelling log(y) instead of y often straightens it out.

Two more problems worth naming. <strong>Multicollinearity</strong> &mdash; features that are nearly duplicates &mdash; leaves the total predictions fine but makes individual coefficients wild and unstable, flipping sign when you add a row. And extrapolation: a line fitted on houses of 50&ndash;200 square metres will confidently quote a price for a 900-square-metre house. That extrapolated prediction is unreliable.

## Solve it three ways and get the same line

The normal equation, scikit-learn, and gradient descent all land on the same coefficients. Seeing that makes the closed form stop being magic.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 300
X = np.column_stack([rng.normal(0, 1, n), rng.normal(0, 2, n)])
true_w, true_b = np.array([2.5, -1.2]), 4.0
y = X @ true_w + true_b + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n)

Xb = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X])          # bias column

# 1. the normal equation: w = (X'X)^-1 X'y
w_normal = np.linalg.solve(Xb.T @ Xb, Xb.T @ y)
print("normal equation   b=%7.4f  w=%s" % (w_normal[0], np.round(w_normal[1:], 4)))

# 2. scikit-learn
lr = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)
print("scikit-learn      b=%7.4f  w=%s" % (lr.intercept_, np.round(lr.coef_, 4)))

# 3. gradient descent, from scratch
w = np.zeros(3)
for step in range(4000):
    grad = 2 * Xb.T @ (Xb @ w - y) / n
    w -= 0.05 * grad
print("gradient descent  b=%7.4f  w=%s" % (w[0], np.round(w[1:], 4)))
print()
print("true values       b=%7.4f  w=%s" % (true_b, true_w))
```

The same fitted line reveals diagnostic properties in its residuals.

```python-run
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import r2_score, mean_squared_error

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 300
X = np.column_stack([rng.normal(0, 1, n), rng.normal(0, 2, n)])
true_w, true_b = np.array([2.5, -1.2]), 4.0
y = X @ true_w + true_b + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n)
Xb = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), X])
w_normal = np.linalg.solve(Xb.T @ Xb, Xb.T @ y)
lr = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)

print("OLS minimises the sum of SQUARED residuals. nothing else has a lower one:")
best = ((y - Xb @ w_normal) ** 2).sum()
print("  the fitted line       : %.4f" % best)
for nudge in (0.05, -0.05, 0.20):
    alt = w_normal.copy(); alt[1] += nudge
    print("  move w[0] by %+.2f    : %.4f" % (nudge, ((y - Xb @ alt) ** 2).sum()))
print()
resid = y - lr.predict(X)
print("the residuals are what is left over:")
print("  they sum to almost exactly zero : %.10f" % resid.sum())
print("  and are uncorrelated with each x: %s"
      % np.round([np.corrcoef(X[:, i], resid)[0, 1] for i in range(2)], 10))
print("  that is not luck -- it is what setting the derivative to zero means.")
print()
print("R^2 %.4f, RMSE %.4f (the noise we added had sd 1.50)"
      % (r2_score(y, lr.predict(X)), np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y, lr.predict(X)))))
print()
print("squaring is why one bad outlier moves the whole line:")
y2 = y.copy(); y2[0] += 60
print("  refit with one point moved by +60: w = %s"
      % np.round(LinearRegression().fit(X, y2).coef_, 4))
print("  the other 299 points did not change.")
```

## Experiments to try

Use the interactive visualization to build your intuition.

- <strong>Manual Fitting:</strong> Click "Generate New Data". Now, without with the "Snap" button, try to find the best fit yourself. Drag the purple (slope) and green (intercept) handles on the line. Watch the "Current Loss (MSE)" value in the left panel. Try to get it as low as possible. Notice how even small changes can dramatically affect the MSE.
- <strong>See the Best Solution:</strong> Once you think you have a good fit, click the "Snap to Best Fit" button. The line will jump to the OLS solution. And you can see the "Best Loss" in the panel. How close were you?
- <strong>The Impact of Outliers:</strong> Generate a new dataset. Now, click and drag one of the blue data points far away from the others. This point is now an "outlier". Click "Snap to Best Fit" again. Notice how the best-fit line is pulled towards the outlier. This shows that OLS is sensitive to outliers since the squaring process in MSE gives large errors (like the one from the outlier) a very heavy weight.
- <strong>Visualize the Loss:</strong> Check the "Show Best Line" box. Now your manual line (hypothesis) and the best-fit line are both visible. The "Live Loss" panel on the left shows you how far your current MSE is from the best possible MSE. This gives you a clear target: make your line match the amber-colored best line to reach zero "Distance to Best".

## Questions people ask

<strong>Do I need to scale the features?</strong> Not for the closed-form solution. It is unaffected by units. You do need it for gradient descent to converge sensibly. And for checking coefficient sizes against each other.

<strong>Can linear regression fit curves?</strong> Yes, if you give it curved features. Add x&sup2; as a column and it fits a parabola &mdash; the model is linear in its <em>parameters</em>, not necessarily in the inputs. That is the whole idea behind polynomial regression.

<strong>What does a negative R&sup2; on the test set mean?</strong> That the model does worse than predicting the average. Often overfitting, a data leak. Or a distribution shift between train and test.

<strong>Is a p-value on a coefficient enough to claim an effect is real?</strong> No. It says the coefficient is unlikely to be zero given the model's assumptions. It says nothing about causation. And it is unreliable when the assumptions above are violated.

<strong>What if two features are almost identical?</strong> Drop one, mix them. Or move to ridge regression. It handles correlated features gracefully by shrinking their coefficients together.

<strong>Is least squares reliable to outliers?</strong> The opposite. Squaring makes it unusually sensitive. Huber regression or RANSAC are the standard reliable options when outliers are genuine and unavoidable.

## Recap in one screen

- The model is a straight line: a slope per feature, plus one intercept.
- "Best" means the smallest sum of squared vertical distances &mdash; smooth, always positive. And heavily influenced by outliers.
- OLS has an exact closed-form solution. Gradient descent is for when that turns too costly.
- The line always passes through the mean of x and the mean of y.
- Read the residual plot before you read the coefficients. And never extrapolate past the data you fitted.
