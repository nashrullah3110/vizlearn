title: Optimizers in 3D: A Practical Guide
intro: A loss surface in three dimensions is the only version you can actually look at. The features that decide which optimiser wins &mdash; ravines, plateaus, saddle points &mdash; are all visible here.

## What the terrain is made of

- <strong>Ravines</strong> &mdash; steep in one direction, nearly flat in another. Plain SGD ricochets between the walls.
- <strong>Plateaus</strong> &mdash; large regions where the gradient is tiny. Progress crawls because there is almost nothing to follow.
- <strong>Saddle points</strong> &mdash; the gradient is close to zero but you are not at a minimum: the surface curves up in some directions and down in others.

At a saddle point, plain SGD stalls because the gradient is near zero in every direction &mdash; momentum, or a per-parameter scaling like Adam, is what pushes through. A ravine (a long narrow valley) is the other classic failure mode: the gradient points across the valley rather than along it, so plain SGD oscillates side to side instead of progressing toward the minimum. [Momentum](optimizers_in_neural_networks.html) damps the oscillation by averaging past gradients, and RMSprop scales each parameter's learning rate by the recent magnitude of its gradient, which shrinks the step across the valley and lets the step along it dominate. Adam combines both: it carries a momentum term and a per-parameter scaling term, with bias correction for the first few steps. The 3D loss surface makes these differences visible in a way that 2D curves cannot &mdash; you can see the trajectory spiralling into a bowl, overshooting a ridge, or threading through a saddle. This page plots three loss surfaces (a bowl, a ravine, and a saddle), runs SGD, momentum, RMSprop, and Adam on each, and shows the trajectory and convergence speed side by side.

## Saddles matter more than local minima

The folk explanation of training failure is "it got stuck in a local minimum". In high dimensions that is mostly wrong. For a point to be a local minimum, the surface must curve upward in <em>every</em> direction at once &mdash; and with millions of parameters that is vanishingly unlikely. Saddle points, where it curves up in some directions and down in others, are enormously more common.

This is a practical difference. A local minimum is a trap. A saddle is not &mdash; there is a way down, and an optimiser with momentum carries enough velocity from earlier steps to keep moving across the flat region until it finds one.

## The loss surface as a landscape

Picture two weights on the horizontal axes and the loss on the vertical one. Training is a ball rolling downhill on that surface, and the optimiser decides how the ball moves.

Real networks have millions of weights, so the surface is millions of dimensions rather than two. The picture is still useful, because the features that make optimisation hard are visible in three dimensions:

- <strong>Ravines</strong> &mdash; steep in one direction, nearly flat in another. Plain gradient descent zigzags across the steep direction and crawls along the flat one.
- <strong>Saddle points</strong> &mdash; downhill in some directions, uphill in others, with a gradient of zero at the centre. In high dimensions these vastly outnumber local minima, because it is improbable for millions of curvature signs to agree.
- <strong>Plateaus</strong> &mdash; large flat regions where gradients are tiny and progress stalls.
- <strong>Sharp versus flat minima</strong> &mdash; both fit the training data; flat ones tend to generalise better, because a small shift in the weights (or in the data) changes the loss less.

## How each optimiser behaves on that landscape

<strong>Plain SGD</strong> takes a step directly downhill, scaled by the learning rate. In a ravine that means oscillating across the narrow direction while barely advancing along the valley floor.

<strong>Momentum</strong> accumulates velocity, so the oscillations partly cancel and the consistent downhill direction builds speed. It is the difference between a ball on a rough surface and one with mass.

<strong>Adam</strong> gives every weight its own effective step size, dividing by the square root of its recent squared gradients. Weights on flat directions get larger steps and weights on steep ones get smaller, which reshapes the ravine into something closer to a bowl.

|Optimiser|In a ravine|On a plateau|Near a saddle|
|---|---|---|---|
|SGD|Zigzags slowly|Stalls|Can stall|
|SGD + momentum|Advances along the valley|Coasts through|Coasts through|
|Adam|Advances directly|Amplifies small gradients|Escapes readily|

That plateau row explains a real advantage of adaptive methods: dividing by a small accumulated gradient magnitude <em>increases</em> the step, so Adam keeps moving where SGD would sit still.

## Saddle points, not local minima

The old fear was that gradient descent would get stuck in bad local minima. High-dimensional geometry says otherwise.

For a stationary point to be a local minimum, the curvature must be positive in <em>every</em> direction. With ten million parameters, that requires ten million signs to agree, which is vanishingly unlikely. Almost every point where the gradient vanishes is a saddle &mdash; downhill in at least one direction.

That is good news, because saddles are escapable. The stochastic noise from mini-batches is usually enough to push the model off the ridge and onto a descending path, which is one reason mini-batch training generalises better than full-batch.

It also reframes what training is doing: not searching for a unique global minimum, but descending through a field of many near-equivalent good solutions, most of which are fine.

## What usually goes wrong

<strong>Over-reading the picture.</strong> This surface has two parameters. A real network has millions, and its loss landscape has properties that genuinely do not exist in 3D. Use this to build intuition about momentum and adaptive step sizes; do not use it to conclude anything about how many minima a real network has.<strong>Judging an optimiser from one starting point.</strong> Change where the run begins and the ranking often changes with it. A single trajectory is an anecdote.

## In one line

The shape of the surface, not the cleverness of the algorithm, decides which optimiser looks good.

## Sharp and flat minima

Two solutions can fit the training data equally well and behave very differently on new data.

A <strong>sharp</strong> minimum is a narrow crevice: move the weights slightly and the loss rises steeply. Since the test distribution differs slightly from the training one, that sensitivity translates into worse test performance.

A <strong>flat</strong> minimum is a broad basin: nearby weights are almost as good, so a small mismatch between train and test costs little.

Several observed behaviours follow from this:

- <strong>Small batches generalise slightly better</strong> because their gradient noise makes it hard to settle into a narrow crevice.
- <strong>Very large batches sometimes generalise worse</strong>, and techniques like LARS and longer warm-up exist to counter it.
- <strong>Weight averaging</strong> (SWA) averages weights from several late-training points, landing nearer the centre of a basin than any individual point.
- <strong>Sharpness-aware minimisation</strong> (SAM) explicitly penalises sharpness by taking a step towards the worst nearby point before updating.

## Visualising a real loss surface

You can plot the field of an actual network, and the pictures are informative.

Take a trained model's weights, pick two random directions in weight space, and evaluate the loss on a grid of small perturbations along them. Plotting that grid gives a two-dimensional slice through a million-dimensional surface.

What such plots reliably show: networks <em>without</em> skip connections have chaotic, spiky surfaces; networks <em>with</em> them are dramatically smoother. That is a fairly direct visual explanation of why residual connections make deep networks trainable, and it is one of the more satisfying results in the area.

The caveat is that a two-dimensional slice of a million-dimensional surface can be misleading. Treat these plots as intuition, not proof.

## Four surfaces that break four different optimisers

A bowl, a ravine, a saddle and a plateau. Each one defeats a different optimiser, and running all four on all four shows why there is no single right answer.

```python-run
import numpy as np
def bowl(w): return 0.5*(w**2).sum(), w
def ravine(w):
    c = np.array([400.,1.]); return 0.5*(c*w**2).sum(), c*w
def saddle(w): return w[0]**2-w[1]**2, np.array([2*w[0],-2*w[1]])
def plateau(w):
    s = np.tanh(w); return (s**2).sum(), 2*s*(1-s**2)
def sgd(fn,w,n,lr):
    for _ in range(n): w = w - lr*fn(w)[1]
    return w
def momentum(fn,w,n,lr,beta=0.9):
    v = np.zeros_like(w)
    for _ in range(n): v = beta*v+fn(w)[1]; w = w-lr*v
    return w
def rmsprop(fn,w,n,lr,beta=0.9):
    s = np.zeros_like(w)
    for _ in range(n):
        g = fn(w)[1]; s = beta*s+(1-beta)*g**2; w = w-lr*g/(np.sqrt(s)+1e-8)
    return w
def adam(fn,w,n,lr,b1=0.9,b2=0.999):
    m = np.zeros_like(w); v = np.zeros_like(w)
    for t in range(1,n+1):
        g = fn(w)[1]; m = b1*m+(1-b1)*g; v = b2*v+(1-b2)*g**2
        w = w-lr*(m/(1-b1**t))/(np.sqrt(v/(1-b2**t))+1e-8)
    return w
SURFS = [("bowl",bowl,[3.,3.]), ("ravine 400:1",ravine,[-2.,-2.]),
         ("saddle",saddle,[.001,.001]), ("plateau",plateau,[3.,3.])]
OPTS = [("SGD",sgd,.004),("momentum",momentum,.002),
        ("RMSProp",rmsprop,.02),("Adam",adam,.02)]
print("Four surfaces, four optimisers, 500 steps each (loss reached):")
print("%-16s %12s %12s %12s %12s" % ("surface",*[n for n,_,_ in OPTS]))
for sn,fn,s0 in SURFS:
    vals = []
    for _,opt,lr in OPTS:
        loss = fn(opt(fn, np.array(s0), 500, lr))[0]
        vals.append("%.3e" % loss if np.isfinite(loss) else "diverged")
    print("%-16s %12s %12s %12s %12s" % (sn, *vals))
```

import numpy as np

def ravine(w):
    c = np.array([400.0, 1.0])
    return 0.5 * (c * w**2).sum(), c * w

def sgd(fn, w, n, lr):

print()
print("RAVINE -- 400x steeper across than along. SGD's step is capped by")
print("the steep direction (above 2/400 = %.4f it diverges), so the" % (2/400))
print("shallow direction crawls at that same tiny rate:")
for step in (0, 100, 300, 500):
    ww = sgd(ravine, np.array([-2.0, -2.0]), step, 0.004)
    print("   SGD after %3d steps: %s" % (step, np.round(ww, 5)))
print("   the first coordinate is solved fast; the second is still")

The saddle and plateau are the surfaces that separate adaptive methods from plain SGD.

```python-run
import numpy as np
def saddle(w): return w[0]**2-w[1]**2, np.array([2*w[0],-2*w[1]])
def plateau(w):
    s = np.tanh(w); return (s**2).sum(), 2*s*(1-s**2)
def sgd(fn,w,n,lr):
    for _ in range(n): w = w-lr*fn(w)[1]
    return w
def momentum(fn,w,n,lr,beta=0.9):
    v = np.zeros_like(w)
    for _ in range(n): v = beta*v+fn(w)[1]; w = w-lr*v
    return w
def rmsprop(fn,w,n,lr,beta=0.9):
    s = np.zeros_like(w)
    for _ in range(n):
        g = fn(w)[1]; s = beta*s+(1-beta)*g**2; w = w-lr*g/(np.sqrt(s)+1e-8)
    return w
def adam(fn,w,n,lr,b1=0.9,b2=0.999):
    m = np.zeros_like(w); v = np.zeros_like(w)
    for t in range(1,n+1):
        g = fn(w)[1]; m = b1*m+(1-b1)*g; v = b2*v+(1-b2)*g**2
        w = w-lr*(m/(1-b1**t))/(np.sqrt(v/(1-b2**t))+1e-8)
    return w
OPTS = [("SGD",sgd,.004),("momentum",momentum,.002),
        ("RMSProp",rmsprop,.02),("Adam",adam,.02)]

print("SADDLE -- gradient is zero at the origin, yet it is not a minimum:")
for on, opt, lr in OPTS:
    w = opt(saddle, np.array([.001,.001]), 500, lr)
    print("   %-10s -> %s  %s" % (on, np.round(w,4), "escaped" if abs(w[1])>.5 else "stuck"))
print("   normalising step size lets a tiny gradient produce a full step.")
print()
print("PLATEAU -- tanh saturates, gradient vanishes far from origin:")
for r in (1., 2., 3., 4.):
    g = plateau(np.array([r,r]))[1]
    print("   dist %.0f: loss %.4f, |grad| %.3e" % (r, plateau(np.array([r,r]))[0], np.linalg.norm(g)))
print("   SGD barely moves. same problem as vanishing gradients.")
print()
print("SUMMARY: a real surface is all four at once. adaptive methods rescale")
print("by gradient magnitude; momentum accumulates the consistent direction.")
print("plain SGD handles none, yet generalises better on many vision tasks.")
```

## Questions people ask

<strong>Does the optimiser find the global minimum?</strong> No, and it does not need to. High-dimensional landscapes have vast numbers of near-equivalent good solutions.

<strong>Why does momentum help?</strong> It averages recent gradients, so consistent directions accumulate and oscillations cancel &mdash; exactly what a ravine needs.

<strong>Are local minima a problem?</strong> Rarely. Saddle points and plateaus are the real obstacles in high dimensions.

<strong>Should I use a large batch to get a cleaner gradient?</strong> A cleaner gradient is not automatically better &mdash; the noise helps escape saddles and avoid sharp minima. Scale the learning rate if you do increase the batch.

<strong>What is the difference between the learning rate and momentum?</strong> The learning rate is how far to step; momentum is how much of the previous direction to keep.

<strong>Can I see whether my minimum is flat?</strong> Approximately &mdash; perturb the weights slightly and measure how much the loss rises. Large increases mean a sharp minimum.

## Recap in one screen

- Loss landscapes have ravines, plateaus and saddles; the optimiser's job is to navigate them.
- Momentum damps oscillation across a ravine; adaptive methods rescale each direction.
- In high dimensions almost every zero-gradient point is a saddle, not a local minimum &mdash; and noise escapes them.
- Flat minima generalise better than sharp ones, which is why small batches and weight averaging help.
- Skip connections visibly smooth the surface, which is a direct explanation of why they work.
