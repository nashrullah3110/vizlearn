## Quick Context

Batch Normalization (BatchNorm) was introduced by Ioffe &amp; Szegedy in 2015 and is now a standard building block in virtually every deep network. It normalizes the inputs to each layer so that training is faster and more stable. This page lets you <em>see</em> what happens with and without it.

## 1) The Problem BatchNorm Solves

When you feed raw features with different scales (e.g., Age 18–80 vs. Salary 20k–200k) into a network, the first-layer weights that receive the large feature grow disproportionately. During backpropagation those large weights amplify gradients in early layers (<span style="color:var(--accent-danger)">exploding</span>), while deeper layers receive progressively smaller updates (<span style="color:var(--accent-blue)">vanishing</span>). The result: unstable training or very slow convergence.

Normalizing inputs at the front door helps the first layer, but <strong>internal covariate shift</strong> — the distribution of each hidden layer's inputs keeps changing as weights update — still destabilizes deeper layers. BatchNorm fixes this by re-normalizing activations <em>inside</em> the network at every layer.

- <strong>ε</strong> is a tiny constant (e.g., 1e-5) to avoid division by zero.
- <strong>γ</strong> and <strong>β</strong> are learnable parameters that let the network recover any scale/shift it needs — BatchNorm doesn't permanently force zero-mean unit-variance; it just gives the optimizer a much friendlier starting point.
- At inference time, the running mean and variance computed during training are used instead of batch statistics.

## 3) Why This Is Worth Learning

- <strong>Faster convergence</strong> — you can often use much higher learning rates.
- <strong>Regularization effect</strong> — the noise from batch statistics acts as mild regularization, sometimes reducing the need for Dropout.
- <strong>Enables deeper networks</strong> — gradients stay in a healthy range across 50, 100, or even 1000+ layers.
- <strong>Interview staple</strong> — expect questions on BatchNorm in almost every ML/DL interview.

## 5) Where BatchNorm Is Placed

There are two common conventions:

- <strong>Before activation</strong>: Linear → BatchNorm → ReLU (the original paper's approach).
- <strong>After activation</strong>: Linear → ReLU → BatchNorm (some practitioners prefer this).

In practice both work well; consistency within your architecture matters more than the placement choice.

## 6) Common Mistakes

- <strong>Forgetting model.eval()</strong> — at inference, if you don't switch to eval mode, PyTorch/TensorFlow will use live batch stats instead of running averages, producing inconsistent predictions.
- <strong>Batch size too small</strong> — with batch size 1 or 2, the batch mean/variance estimate is too noisy. Use Group Normalization or Layer Normalization instead.
- <strong>Using BatchNorm with Dropout carelessly</strong> — both introduce stochasticity; their interaction can hurt. Test whether you need both.
- <strong>Applying BatchNorm to the output layer</strong> — normalizing the final prediction layer often harms performance.

## 8) Key Takeaways

- BatchNorm normalizes hidden-layer activations using batch statistics during training and running statistics during inference.
- It fights internal covariate shift, enabling higher learning rates and deeper architectures.
- The learnable parameters γ and β let the network undo the normalization if needed.
- Always switch to eval mode at inference and be mindful of batch size.
