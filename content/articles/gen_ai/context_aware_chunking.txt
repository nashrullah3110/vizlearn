title: Context-aware chunking: A Practical Guide
intro: What is context-aware chunking, and what problem does it solve that the other strategies do not?

## The problem is coreference, not boundaries

Prose is written to be read in order, so it leans on everything before it: "it", "this policy", "as described above", "the latter". Cut one paragraph out and those references dangle.

Two consequences. The generator cannot answer from the chunk because it does not know what "it" is. And retrieval never surfaces the chunk in the first place, because the embedding is of text that does not contain the words a user would search for.

## What gets added

<p><strong>Cheap and free:</strong> document title and heading path, prepended. Deterministic, no model call, and usually the largest single improvement.</p>

<p><strong>Neighbour context:</strong> the previous and next chunk, or a window around the chunk, stored for generation but not embedded &mdash; the <a href="parent_document_retriever.html">parent document retriever</a> is exactly this idea, retrieving small and returning large.</p>

<p><strong>Generated context:</strong> an LLM writes a sentence situating the chunk in the document, prepended before embedding. Anthropic's "contextual retrieval" is this, and it works well. It costs one model call per chunk at index time, which prompt caching over the shared document makes affordable.</p>

## What it costs, and what to watch

Index time and money, both once. The subtler cost is dilution: prepending 200 tokens of context to a 300-token chunk means the embedding is largely <em>about the context</em>, so chunks from the same section start looking alike and the retriever loses its ability to distinguish them.

Keep the enrichment short relative to the chunk, and measure on a retrieval evaluation set rather than assuming. This is the strategy with the best evidence behind it and it is not free of trade-offs.

## Contextual retrieval, and what it costs

The strongest published form of this is to have an LLM write a short sentence situating each chunk inside its document &mdash; what it is about, what it follows &mdash; and prepend that before embedding. Reported reductions in retrieval failure are large, and the technique is simple enough to implement in an afternoon.

The cost is one model call per chunk at index time, which sounds prohibitive on a large corpus and mostly is not: the document is the same for every chunk in it, so <a href="caching_in_rag_pipelines.html">prompt caching</a> over that shared prefix makes the marginal call cheap. It is the clearest practical example of two of these techniques composing.

It is still an index-time cost paid on every re-index, so it belongs in your pipeline design rather than being bolted on: changing the chunker means regenerating every context sentence.

## Embedded text and returned text can differ

The enrichment does not have to be what the model reads. You can embed the enriched text &mdash; so the chunk is findable &mdash; while storing and returning the original, so the generator is not fed repetitive boilerplate.

Separating the two is the general form of a pattern that appears everywhere in retrieval: embed something optimised for matching, return something optimised for reading. Parent-document retrieval embeds a small chunk and returns its parent. Summary indexing embeds a summary and returns the document. Contextual retrieval embeds chunk-plus-context and can return either.

Once you see the pattern, the design question stops being "how should I chunk?" and becomes two questions with different answers: what should be <em>matched</em> against, and what should be <em>read</em>?

## Measuring whether the enrichment helped

Enrichment is easy to add and easy to overdo, and the only way to tell which you have done is to measure both halves.

<p><strong>Did findability improve?</strong> <a href="recall_at_k.html">Recall@k</a> on a fixed query set, before and after. This is the number the technique is supposed to move, and it usually does.</p>

<p><strong>Did distinguishability degrade?</strong> The failure mode is chunks from one section becoming interchangeable. A cheap proxy is the mean pairwise similarity between chunks that share a parent: if it climbs sharply after enrichment, the shared context is dominating and the retriever is losing its ability to pick between them.</p>

Watching only the first will lead you to enrich more and more, because recall keeps improving right up until the point where the top-k fills with near-identical chunks from the same section &mdash; at which point recall still looks fine and answers get worse.

## The missing-context problem

A chunk is retrieved and passed to the model on its own. Frequently it cannot be understood on its own:

<blockquote>"This must be requested at least 8 weeks in advance, and the form requires a manager's signature."</blockquote>

What must? Requested from whom? The chunk before it said "Parental leave", and that chunk is not in the context.

This is the central weakness of chunking, and it has nothing to do with where the boundaries fall &mdash; even a perfectly-placed boundary produces chunks that reference their surroundings. Documents are written to be read in order; chunks are retrieved out of order.

Context-aware chunking is the family of techniques that give each chunk enough surrounding information to stand alone.

## Four ways to add the missing context

|Technique|What is added|Cost|
|---|---|---|
|Heading path prefix|The document's section hierarchy|Free|
|Overlap|The neighbouring boundary text|Storage|
|Parent-child|Retrieve small, return the parent section|Storage, complexity|
|Contextual summary|A generated sentence describing the chunk's place|One model call per chunk|

<strong>The heading path</strong> is the cheapest and often the most effective. Prepending "Handbook &gt; Leave &gt; Parental Leave &gt; Applying" to that chunk resolves "this" immediately, improves the embedding, and costs nothing beyond capturing the structure at indexing time.

<strong>Overlap</strong> of 10&ndash;20% means a sentence spanning a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk. It handles the boundary case and not the "referenced three paragraphs earlier" case.

<strong>Parent-child</strong> indexes small chunks for precise matching and returns the larger enclosing section for context. It resolves references within the section by construction.

<strong>Contextual retrieval</strong> is the newest of the four: ask a model, at indexing time, to write a sentence or two situating each chunk within its document, and prepend that to the chunk before embedding.

## Contextual retrieval, concretely

The prompt is short and the effect is substantial:

```
Here is a document:
<document>{full_document}</document>

Here is a chunk from it:
<chunk>{chunk_text}</chunk>

Write 1-2 sentences of context situating this chunk within the document,
so it can be understood on its own. Reply with the context only.
```

The generated context is prepended to the chunk text, and the combined text is embedded and stored.

<blockquote><strong>Generated:</strong> "This passage is from the parental leave section of the employee handbook, describing the application process for eligible employees."</blockquote>
<blockquote><strong>Chunk:</strong> "This must be requested at least 8 weeks in advance..."</blockquote>

Now the chunk is retrievable by a query about applying for parental leave, and interpretable when retrieved.

The reported effect on retrieval failure rates is large &mdash; substantial reductions when combined with hybrid search and reranking. The cost is one model call per chunk at indexing time, which for a large corpus is significant. Two mitigations: use a small fast model, and exploit prompt caching, since the full document is repeated across all of its chunks' prompts.

## Testing whether a chunk can stand alone

The problem is coreference -- a chunk that says "it" or "the policy" is meaningless once separated from what it referred to. This measures that damage directly, applies the four repairs the article names, and shows which one is worth its cost.

```python-run
import numpy as np

# a document. each chunk carries the concepts it EXPLICITLY names.
AXES = ["refund", "warranty", "days", "process"]

DOC = [
    ("C1", "Our refund policy is described below.", [0.90, 0.00, 0.05, 0.10]),
    ("C2", "It allows returns within 30 days.",      [0.00, 0.00, 0.85, 0.20]),
    ("C3", "To use it, email support.",              [0.00, 0.00, 0.00, 0.90]),
    ("C4", "The hardware warranty is separate.",     [0.05, 0.90, 0.05, 0.05]),
    ("C5", "It lasts 24 months.",                    [0.00, 0.00, 0.85, 0.00]),
]
QUERY = [0.85, 0.00, 0.50, 0.10]        # "how many days for a refund"

def unit(v):
    v = np.asarray(v, float)
    return v / (np.linalg.norm(v) + 1e-12)

def sim(a, b):
    return float(unit(a) @ unit(b))

print("A DOCUMENT SPLIT INTO 5 CHUNKS. read them as a reader would, in")
print("order, and then read C2 on its own:")
for cid, text, _ in DOC:
    print("   %s  %s" % (cid, text))
print()
print("   C2 says 'It allows returns within 30 days.' In context, 'it' is")
print("   the refund policy. Alone, in a vector index, 'it' is nothing --")
print("   and C5 has exactly the same problem pointing at the warranty.")
print("   the two chunks that carry the ANSWERS are the two that cannot")
print("   say what they are about.")
print()

print("THE QUERY: 'how many days do I have for a refund'")
print("%-6s %-40s %14s" % ("chunk", "text", "similarity"))
base = {}
for cid, text, v in DOC:
    base[cid] = sim(QUERY, v)
    print("%-6s %-40s %14.4f" % (cid, text[:38], base[cid]))
order = sorted(base, key=lambda x: -base[x])
print("   top result: %s -- %s"
      % (order[0], dict((c, t) for c, t, _ in DOC)[order[0]]))
print("   the retriever returns C1, which ANNOUNCES the policy and does")
print("   not state it. the chunk holding '30 days' is ranked %d, and the"
      % (order.index("C2") + 1))
print("   chunk holding the warranty's 24 months scores %.4f -- almost as"
      % base["C5"])
print("   high as the correct one, because neither of them says which")
print("   thing it is describing.")
print("   C2 and C5 are INDISTINGUISHABLE to the index: %.4f against %.4f."
      % (base["C2"], base["C5"]))
print()

print("FOUR REPAIRS. each one adds context to the chunk before embedding:")
REPAIRS = {
    "1. prepend the document title":
        {"C2": [0.45, 0.00, 0.85, 0.20], "C5": [0.30, 0.45, 0.85, 0.00]},
    "2. prepend the section heading":
        {"C2": [0.70, 0.00, 0.85, 0.20], "C5": [0.05, 0.75, 0.85, 0.00]},
    "3. overlap with the previous chunk":
        {"C2": [0.60, 0.00, 0.85, 0.20], "C5": [0.05, 0.65, 0.85, 0.00]},
    "4. an LLM writes a one-line context":
        {"C2": [0.85, 0.00, 0.85, 0.20], "C5": [0.02, 0.88, 0.85, 0.00]},
}
print("%-40s %12s %12s %14s"
      % ("repair", "C2 (right)", "C5 (wrong)", "separation"))
print("%-40s %12.4f %12.4f %14.4f"
      % ("0. no repair", base["C2"], base["C5"], base["C2"] - base["C5"]))
for name, fix in REPAIRS.items():
    a, b = sim(QUERY, fix["C2"]), sim(QUERY, fix["C5"])
    print("%-40s %12.4f %12.4f %14.4f" % (name, a, b, a - b))
print("   the SEPARATION column is the one that matters. it is the gap")
print("   between the chunk that answers the question and the chunk that")
print("   looks like it does, and with no repair it is %.4f -- the index"
      % (base["C2"] - base["C5"]))
print("   genuinely cannot tell them apart.")
print("   every repair widens it. the LLM-written context widens it most,")
print("   because it is the only one that can say 'this describes the")
print("   refund policy' when the surrounding text never says so in words")
print("   the chunk happens to contain.")
print()

print("BUT PRICE THEM BEFORE CHOOSING. for a corpus of %s chunks:"
      % "{:,}".format(200000))
N = 200000
print("%-40s %16s %16s %s"
      % ("repair", "LLM calls", "cost at $0.30/1k", "when it fails"))
rows = [
    ("1. document title", 0, 0.0, "all chunks look alike"),
    ("2. section heading", 0, 0.0, "no headings in the source"),
    ("3. overlap", 0, 0.0, "the antecedent is 3 chunks back"),
    ("4. LLM-written context", N, N * 0.30 / 1000, "cost, and staleness"),
]
for name, calls, cost, fails in rows:
    print("%-40s %16s %16s %s"
          % (name, "{:,}".format(calls),
             "$%.2f" % cost if cost else "free", fails))
print("   the first three are free and mechanical. the fourth costs a")
print("   generation per chunk, once, at indexing time -- and it has to be")
print("   paid again for every re-index.")
print("   the honest reading of the table above is that repair 2 gets")
print("   %.0f%% of repair 4's separation for nothing at all, so the LLM"
      % (100 * (sim(QUERY, REPAIRS["2. prepend the section heading"]["C2"])
                - sim(QUERY, REPAIRS["2. prepend the section heading"]["C5"]))
         / (sim(QUERY, REPAIRS["4. an LLM writes a one-line context"]["C2"])
            - sim(QUERY, REPAIRS["4. an LLM writes a one-line context"]["C5"]))))
print("   version earns its money only where there are no headings to")
print("   use -- transcripts, scanned documents, long unstructured prose.")
print()

print("AND THE DETAIL THAT IS EASY TO GET WRONG: what you EMBED and what")
print("you RETURN do not have to be the same text.")
print("%-30s %s" % ("embed", "chunk + its added context"))
print("%-30s %s" % ("return to the model", "the chunk, or its parent"))
print("   if you return the enriched text, the model reads your generated")
print("   context as though it were source material -- and it is not")
print("   source material, it is a retrieval aid you wrote. a wrong or")
print("   hallucinated context line then becomes a fact in the prompt.")
print("   keep the enrichment on the INDEX side, where a mistake costs a")
print("   ranking place, rather than on the ANSWER side, where it costs")
print("   correctness.")
print()
print("HOW TO TELL IF A CHUNK NEEDS THIS AT ALL -- read it cold:")
for cid, text, _ in DOC:
    needs = text.strip().lower().startswith(("it ", "this ", "they ", "the "))
    print("   %-6s %-40s %s"
          % (cid, text[:38], "needs context" if needs else "stands alone"))
print("   the rule is that a chunk opening with a pronoun or a bare")
print("   definite article is probably pointing outside itself -- and")
print("   look at what it got wrong on five sentences:")
print("      C3 'To use it, email support.' -- called STANDS ALONE, and")
print("         it contains an 'it' with no antecedent. the rule only")
print("         looks at the first word.")
print("      C4 'The hardware warranty is separate.' -- called NEEDS")
print("         CONTEXT because it starts with 'The', and it names its")
print("         own subject perfectly well.")
print("   so it is a cheap screen with errors in both directions, useful")
print("   for finding candidates to look at rather than for deciding")
print("   anything. the reliable version of the same test is to hand the")
print("   chunk to a model with no other context and ask what it refers")
print("   to -- which costs a call per chunk, and is the same price as")
print("   just fixing them all with repair 4.")
```

## Things to try

1. Start with everything off. The raw chunk is about refunds, never says so, and cannot be retrieved.
2. Turn on <strong>resolve pronouns</strong>. Similarity jumps, because the searchable nouns are now in the text.
3. Turn on <strong>over-enrich</strong>. The chunk stays findable and stops being distinguishable from its sibling &mdash; the cost of adding too much shared context.

## What to remember

Context-aware chunking changes what is stored rather than where the cut falls. A chunk full of pronouns and back-references is unusable by the generator and invisible to retrieval, so title, heading path and resolved references are added back. Keep the enrichment short relative to the chunk, or shared context dominates the embedding and chunks stop being distinguishable.

## Choosing among them

The techniques are not alternatives so much as layers, and they have a clear cost ordering.

<strong>Always do:</strong> capture the structure and prepend the heading path. Free, and it addresses the most common case.

<strong>Usually do:</strong> 10&ndash;20% overlap. Cheap insurance against boundary loss.

<strong>Often worth it:</strong> parent-child retrieval, if chunks need to be small for precision. It resolves intra-section references without a model call.

<strong>Consider:</strong> contextual retrieval, when the corpus is high-value, the documents are long, and retrieval quality is the bottleneck. Measure the gain against the indexing cost.

<strong>Emerging:</strong> late chunking &mdash; embed the whole document with a long-context embedding model, then pool token embeddings into chunk vectors. Each chunk's vector is informed by the entire document without any per-chunk generation. Promising, and it requires a suitable embedding model.

## Testing whether a chunk stands alone

There is a simple and effective test, and it is worth running on a sample before indexing a whole corpus.

Take twenty chunks at random and read each one cold. For each, ask:

1. Can I tell what topic this is about?
2. Do all the pronouns and references resolve?
3. If someone asked a question this chunk answers, would this text answer it?

If the answer to any is no, the chunk needs more context. That is a five-minute exercise that predicts retrieval quality better than most metrics, and it directly identifies which of the four techniques above is needed.

Following it up quantitatively: measure recall@k with and without the context addition on a question set. The heading path alone frequently moves recall by several points.

## Common mistakes

- <strong>Adding context after embedding.</strong> The context must be present in the text that gets embedded, or it improves readability without improving retrieval.
- <strong>Prepending so much context that it dominates the embedding.</strong> A 200-token context on a 100-token chunk means the vector is mostly about the context. Keep it to a sentence or two.
- <strong>Not storing the original chunk separately,</strong> so citations quote generated context as if it were source text.
- <strong>Generating context per chunk without prompt caching,</strong> paying for the full document repeatedly.
- <strong>Assuming overlap solves reference resolution.</strong> It handles boundaries, not distant antecedents.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Is the heading path enough?</strong> Often, for well-structured documents. It is always worth doing first because it is free.

<strong>How much does contextual retrieval cost?</strong> One model call per chunk. With a small model and prompt caching over the shared document, considerably less than it first appears.

<strong>Does the generated context risk hallucination?</strong> It can misdescribe a chunk. Keep it to situating rather than summarising content, and store the original text separately for citation.

<strong>Should context be embedded or only stored?</strong> Embedded &mdash; that is the point. Store the original separately for display.

<strong>Does this replace good chunking?</strong> No, it complements it. Bad boundaries plus added context is worse than good boundaries plus added context.

<strong>What is late chunking?</strong> Embedding the whole document first and pooling token vectors into chunk vectors, so each chunk's embedding already carries document context.

## Recap in one screen

- Chunks routinely reference material outside themselves, and retrieval delivers them out of order.
- Prepend the heading path always &mdash; free, and it fixes the most common case.
- Overlap handles boundary loss; parent-child retrieval handles intra-section references.
- Contextual retrieval generates a situating sentence per chunk at indexing time, and measurably reduces retrieval failures.
- Read twenty chunks cold and ask whether each stands alone &mdash; the cheapest diagnostic available.
