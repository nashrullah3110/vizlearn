title: Divide and Conquer
intro: Break it up, solve the pieces, stitch them back &mdash; and the log n falls out of the tree depth.

## The problem it solves

<strong>Divide and conquer</strong> solves a problem by splitting it into smaller instances of itself, solving those recursively, and combining the results. Three phases: divide, conquer, combine.

## Where log n Comes From

The recursion tree makes it obvious. Each level halves the problem size, so it takes <span class="mono-font">log&#8322;n</span> levels to reach size 1 &mdash; that is the <strong>height</strong> of the tree.

In merge sort, every level does O(n) total work (each element is touched once during merging), and there are log n levels. Multiply: <strong>O(n log n)</strong>. Raise n in the lab and watch the level count grow by exactly one each time you double.

## The Master Theorem

For recurrences of the form <span class="mono-font">T(n) = a&middot;T(n/b) + f(n)</span> &mdash; a subproblems of size n/b, plus f(n) to combine &mdash; the answer depends on which dominates:

- <strong>Leaves dominate</strong> &mdash; most work at the bottom.
- <strong>Balanced</strong> &mdash; equal work per level, giving the extra log factor. Merge sort sits here: <span class="mono-font">T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n)</span> &rarr; O(n log n).
- <strong>Root dominates</strong> &mdash; the combine step is the expensive part.

Binary search is <span class="mono-font">T(n) = T(n/2) + O(1)</span>: one subproblem, trivial combine &mdash; so O(log n) total. It discards half the work rather than recursing into both halves, which is why it beats merge sort's complexity.

## The Combine Step Is Where the Work Lives

Dividing is usually trivial &mdash; compute a midpoint. The interesting engineering is almost always in combining.

- <strong>Merge sort</strong> &mdash; merging two sorted halves is O(n) and needs O(n) scratch space.
- <strong>Quick sort</strong> &mdash; inverts the pattern: it partitions <em>before</em> recursing, so the combine step is free. That is why it sorts in place.
- <strong>Maximum subarray</strong> &mdash; the answer may straddle the midpoint, so combining must scan outward from the centre.

## Why It Matters Beyond Sorting

Divide and conquer parallelises naturally &mdash; independent subproblems can run on different cores, which is why it underpins MapReduce and most parallel frameworks. It is also the pattern behind Karatsuba multiplication, the Fast Fourier Transform, and closest-pair-of-points.

## Experiments to try

1. <strong>Step through merge sort at n = 8.</strong> Watch the tree split down to single elements, then merge back upward.
2. <strong>Count the levels: 3 for n = 8.</strong> That is log&#8322;8, and it is exactly where the log n factor comes from.
3. <strong>Double n to 16.</strong> The level count rises by one, not by double &mdash; logarithmic growth, visible.
4. <strong>Switch to binary search.</strong> Only <em>one</em> branch is followed at each level, so the tree is a path &mdash; O(log n) rather than O(n log n).
5. <strong>Compare work per level.</strong> Merge sort does O(n) at every level; binary search does O(1). That single difference produces the whole complexity gap.

## Where that leaves you

Divide and conquer gets its log factor from tree depth: halving repeatedly takes log n levels. Multiply that by the work per level and you have the complexity. Recursing into every branch gives O(n log n); discarding all but one gives O(log n).
