title: Entropy and Information: A Practical Guide
intro: One number for "how uncertain is this?", and the quantity every classification loss is built from.

## Quick Context

Suppose I am about to tell you the outcome of an event. And you want to know how much you are about to learn. If the event is a coin flip, you will learn something. If it is "will the sun rise tomorrow", you will learn in effect nothing, since you now knew.

Entropy puts a count on that. It measures the uncertainty in a chance spread. Equivalently, how much information you gain when the outcome is revealed. It is the foundation of cross-entropy loss, of how decision trees pick their splits. And of compression.

The formula is H = &minus;&Sigma; p(x) log p(x), summed over all possible outcomes x. When the log is base 2 the unit is bits. A fair coin has entropy 1 bit: you need one yes-or-no question to find the outcome. A fair six-sided die has entropy log&sub2;(6) &asymp; 2.585 bits. A loaded die that lands on 6 with chance 0.99 has entropy close to zero, since you now know what will happen.

Two properties make entropy the right measure. First, a certain outcome adds zero: if p = 1, then p log p = 0. So knowing what you now knew teaches you nothing. Second, the more spread-out the spread, the more the entropy. A uniform spread over n outcomes has the max entropy of log&sub2;(n). And any departure from uniformity reduces it.

In machine learning, entropy appears most right in the cross-entropy loss function used to train sorters. The net outputs a predicted spread over classes. And cross-entropy measures how far that prediction is from the true label. Cutting cross-entropy pushes the predicted spread toward the truth. And when the prediction matches just the cross-entropy equals the entropy of the true spread. Decision trees use a closely tied quantity. Information gain, the drop in entropy after a split. To choose which feature to split on at each node.

## Surprise first

Start with a single outcome. How surprising is it? Two things should be true: a certain outcome (p = 1) should carry zero surprise. And rarer outcomes should be more surprising. One function does this cleanly:

surprise(p) = &minus;log<sub>2</sub>(p)

An outcome with p = 1 gives 0 bits. p = 0.5 gives 1 bit. p = 0.25 gives 2 bits. p = 0.01 gives about 6.6 bits. Halving the chance adds just one bit, each time. Which is the property that makes the logarithm the right choice rather than an arbitrary one.

## Entropy is average surprise

Now take the whole spread and average the surprise, weighting each outcome by how often it really occurs:

H = &minus; &Sigma; p<sub>i</sub> log<sub>2</sub>(p<sub>i</sub>)

That is the whole definition. The minus sign is there just since logs of counts below 1 are negative. And we want a positive answer.

The reading worth memorising: <strong>entropy is the average count of yes/no questions needed to find the outcome</strong>, if you ask the smartest possible questions. A fair coin needs just 1. A fair 8-sided die needs just 3, since 2&sup3; = 8.

## Surprise, measured in bits

Entropy measures uncertainty. Concretely, it is the average count of yes/no questions needed to find an outcome. Or equivalently, how surprised you should expect to be.

<p class="mono-font">H = &minus;&Sigma; p&#7522; log&#8322;(p&#7522;)</p>

Three cases make the formula concrete:

- <strong>A fair coin.</strong> Two outcomes at 0.5: H = &minus;(0.5&times;&minus;1 + 0.5&times;&minus;1) = <strong>1 bit</strong>. One question, and you know.
- <strong>A biased coin (0.9 / 0.1).</strong> H = &minus;(0.9&times;log&#8322;0.9 + 0.1&times;log&#8322;0.1) = <strong>0.47 bits</strong>. You can often guess correctly. So there is less to learn.
- <strong>A two-headed coin.</strong> One outcome at chance 1: H = <strong>0 bits</strong>. No uncertainty, no information in the answer.

The pattern: <strong>entropy is highest when outcomes are equally likely and zero when one outcome is certain</strong>. For n equally likely outcomes it is just log&#8322;(n). It is why a fair die is 2.58 bits and a fair 8-sided die is just 3.

## Why rare events carry more information

The term inside the sum, &minus;log&#8322;(p), is the <strong>information content</strong> of a single outcome. How surprising it is.

|Probability|Information|
|---|---|
|1.0|0 bits|
|0.5|1 bit|
|0.25|2 bits|
|0.01|6.6 bits|
|0.001|10 bits|

"The sun rose today" carries almost no information since it was certain. "A meteor landed in the garden" carries a great deal. Entropy is the average of these surprises, weighted by how often each occurs.

The logarithm is not arbitrary: it makes information <em>additive</em>. Two free events with chances p and q have joint chance pq. And &minus;log(pq) = &minus;log(p) &minus; log(q). Learning two free facts gives you the sum of their information. It is the property any sensible measure must have.

## Where it does work in machine learning

<strong>Decision trees.</strong> Each split is chosen to reduce entropy the most. A node with 50/50 classes has 1 bit of entropy. A perfectly pure node has 0. The reduction is the information gain. And the algorithm greedily takes the largest.

<strong>Cross-entropy loss.</strong> The standard sorting loss measures how many extra bits are needed to encode the true labels with the net's predicted spread. Cutting it is making the net's beliefs match reality.

<strong>Feature choice.</strong> Mutual information &mdash; how much knowing one var reduces uncertainty about another. Ranks features without assuming a linear relationship. It correlation cannot do.

<strong>Compression.</strong> Shannon's source coding theorem says entropy is the hard less bound on average bits per symbol. English text has roughly 1&ndash;1.5 bits of entropy per character. It is why text compresses to a fraction of its size and random bytes do not compress at all.

<strong>Language nets.</strong> Perplexity is 2 raised to the cross-entropy. The effective count of equally likely choices the net is deciding between at each step. Less is better. And it is entropy wearing a friendlier scale.

## Weigh a spread's surprise

Entropy in bits for a few spreads over the same four outcomes, plus the coin curve that peaks just at a fair coin.

```python-run
import numpy as np

def entropy(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    p = p[p > 0]                       # 0 log 0 is defined as 0
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum() + 0.0

cases = {
    "uniform over 4":    [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25],
    "slightly skewed":   [0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10],
    "very skewed":       [0.90, 0.06, 0.03, 0.01],
    "certain":           [1.00, 0.00, 0.00, 0.00],
}
for name, p in cases.items():
    print("  %-18s H = %.4f bits" % (name, abs(entropy(p))))
print()
print("4 equally likely outcomes need log2(4) = %.0f bits. certainty needs 0."
      % np.log2(4))
print()
print("a coin, as it becomes unfair:")
for q in (0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999):
    print("  P(heads)=%-6s H = %.4f bits" % (q, entropy([q, 1 - q])))
print()
print("surprise per outcome:")
for p in (0.9, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01):
    print("  an event of probability %-5s carries %5.2f bits" % (p, -np.log2(p)))
print()
print("entropy is the average of those surprises, weighted by probability.")
```

## Why machine learning cares

- <strong>Decision trees</strong> choose the split that reduces entropy the most. That reduction is called information gain. And it is literally "how much uncertainty did this question remove".
- <strong>Cross-entropy loss</strong> measures the average surprise of the true labels <em>under your net's spread</em>. Cutting it means making the truth unsurprising to the net.
- <strong>KL divergence</strong> is the gap between the cross-entropy and the true entropy. The extra bits you pay for with the wrong spread.
- <strong>Compression</strong> gets the same count from the other way: entropy is the theoretical floor on the average bits per symbol. It is why a file of random bytes will not compress.

## Bits, nats and why it rarely matters

With log base 2 gives bits. It is the interpretable unit and the one this page uses. Machine learning code almost always uses the natural log instead, giving <em>nats</em>. The two differ by a constant factor of ln(2) &asymp; 0.693. And since optimisation just cares about which way is downhill, a constant factor changes nothing about training. It does mean a cross-entropy loss printed by a framework is in nats. So 0.693 is the "fair coin" value there rather than 1.0.

## Common mistakes

- <strong>Feeding it a zero.</strong> log(0) is undefined. The convention is that 0·log(0) = 0, which is the correct limit. But a naive build returns NaN. Clamp chances away from zero.
- <strong>Reading entropy as a chance.</strong> It is measured in bits and is unbounded above as outcomes are added. Eight outcomes can reach 3, sixteen can reach 4. Just the fraction-of-max reading is comparable across other-sized spreads.
- <strong>Confusing it with variance.</strong> Both measure spread. But entropy ignores the <em>vals</em> fully. Relabelling the outcomes changes variance and leaves entropy untouched.
- <strong>Expecting Gini and entropy to disagree.</strong> On sorting splits they almost never pick other features. Choosing between them is not worth tuning.

## Where that leaves you

Entropy is the average surprise of a spread, &minus;&Sigma;p·log(p). And reads as the count of yes/no questions needed to pin down the outcome. It is maximal when each outcome is equally likely and just zero when one outcome is certain. It is why it works as a measure of uncertainty. Each sorting loss in machine learning is built from it: cross-entropy is the average surprise of the true labels under your net. And information gain &mdash; the entropy a split removes. Is how a decision tree decides what to ask.

## Information gain, worked through

A decision tree splitting 10 samples that are 5 yes and 5 no:

- <strong>Before</strong>: H = 1.0 bit (max uncertainty).
- <strong>Split on "is it sunny?"</strong>: sunny gives 4 samples, all yes (H = 0). Not sunny gives 6 samples, 1 yes and 5 no (H = 0.65).
- <strong>Weighted entropy after</strong>: (4/10)&times;0 + (6/10)&times;0.65 = 0.39 bits.
- <strong>Information gain</strong>: 1.0 &minus; 0.39 = <strong>0.61 bits</strong>.

The tree computes this for each feature and each threshold. And keeps the winner. Gini impurity is the cheaper option that almost always picks the same split. It approximates the same idea without the logarithm.

There is a known bias worth guarding against: information gain favours features with many distinct vals, since splitting into many small pure groups always looks good. A unique ID column would score perfectly and transfer not at all. The <em>gain ratio</em>, which divides by the entropy of the split it, corrects for this.

## The family of tied measures

|Measure|Question it answers|
|---|---|
|Entropy H(X)|How uncertain is X?|
|Conditional entropy H(X\|Y)|How uncertain is X once Y is known?|
|Mutual information I(X;Y)|How much does knowing Y reduce uncertainty about X?|
|Cross-entropy H(p, q)|Cost of encoding p using a code built for q|
|KL divergence D(p\|\|q)|The extra cost &mdash; cross-entropy minus entropy|

Mutual information deserves particular attention as a feature-choice tool. Unlike correlation, it detects <em>any</em> dependence, with non-linear ones: a perfect parabolic relationship has near-zero correlation and high mutual information. It is zero just when the vars are free.

KL divergence is not symmetric &mdash; D(p||q) &ne; D(q||p). So it is a divergence rather than a distance. That asymmetry has real consequences in variational test time. There which way you minimise finds whether the fitted spread covers all the modes or concentrates on one.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Why base 2?</strong> Since it gives the answer in bits. It correspond to yes/no questions. Base e gives nats. And base 10 gives dits &mdash; just the units change.

<strong>What is the max entropy?</strong> log&#8322;(n) for n outcomes, reached when all are equally likely.

<strong>Is high entropy good or bad?</strong> Neither &mdash; it depends. High entropy in your labels means a hard problem. High entropy in a decision tree leaf means the split did not help. High entropy in a made spread means diversity.

<strong>What is the gap between entropy and cross-entropy?</strong> Entropy is the uncertainty of one spread. Cross-entropy measures one spread against another. And equals entropy plus KL divergence.

<strong>Why do trees use Gini instead of entropy?</strong> It is cheaper (no logarithm) and agrees with entropy on the chosen split the overwhelming majority of the time.

<strong>Can entropy be negative?</strong> Not for discrete spreads. Differential entropy for continuous ones can be. It is one of a few reasons the continuous version needs more care.

## Recap in one screen

- Entropy is average surprise, in bits: `H = -Σ p log₂ p`.
- Equally likely outcomes give max entropy. A certain outcome gives zero.
- Rare events carry more information. And the log makes information additive.
- Decision trees split to reduce entropy. Cross-entropy loss trains sorters. Perplexity is entropy for language nets.
- Mutual information detects any dependence, not just linear ones. It makes it a stronger feature-choice signal than correlation.
