title: Entropy and Information: A Practical Guide
intro: One number for "how uncertain is this?", and the quantity every classification loss is built from.

## Quick Context

Suppose I am about to tell you the outcome of an event, and you want to know how much you are about to learn. If the event is a coin flip, you will learn something. If it is "will the sun rise tomorrow", you will learn essentially nothing, because you already knew.

Entropy puts a number on that. It measures the uncertainty in a probability distribution &mdash; equivalently, how much information you gain when the outcome is revealed. It is the foundation of cross-entropy loss, of how decision trees pick their splits, and of compression.

## Surprise first

Start with a single outcome. How surprising is it? Two things should be true: a certain outcome (p = 1) should carry zero surprise, and rarer outcomes should be more surprising. One function does this cleanly:

surprise(p) = &minus;log<sub>2</sub>(p)

An outcome with p = 1 gives 0 bits. p = 0.5 gives 1 bit. p = 0.25 gives 2 bits. p = 0.01 gives about 6.6 bits. Halving the probability adds exactly one bit, every time &mdash; which is the property that makes the logarithm the right choice rather than an arbitrary one.

## Entropy is average surprise

Now take the whole distribution and average the surprise, weighting each outcome by how often it actually occurs:

H = &minus; &Sigma; p<sub>i</sub> log<sub>2</sub>(p<sub>i</sub>)

That is the entire definition. The minus sign is there only because logs of numbers below 1 are negative, and we want a positive answer.

The interpretation worth memorising: <strong>entropy is the average number of yes/no questions needed to determine the outcome</strong>, if you ask the smartest possible questions. A fair coin needs exactly 1. A fair 8-sided die needs exactly 3, because 2&sup3; = 8.

## Surprise, measured in bits

Entropy measures uncertainty. Concretely, it is the average number of yes/no questions needed to identify an outcome &mdash; or equivalently, how surprised you should expect to be.

<p class="mono-font">H = &minus;&Sigma; p&#7522; log&#8322;(p&#7522;)</p>

Three cases make the formula concrete:

- <strong>A fair coin.</strong> Two outcomes at 0.5: H = &minus;(0.5&times;&minus;1 + 0.5&times;&minus;1) = <strong>1 bit</strong>. One question, and you know.
- <strong>A biased coin (0.9 / 0.1).</strong> H = &minus;(0.9&times;log&#8322;0.9 + 0.1&times;log&#8322;0.1) = <strong>0.47 bits</strong>. You can usually guess correctly, so there is less to learn.
- <strong>A two-headed coin.</strong> One outcome at probability 1: H = <strong>0 bits</strong>. No uncertainty, no information in the answer.

The pattern: <strong>entropy is highest when outcomes are equally likely and zero when one outcome is certain</strong>. For n equally likely outcomes it is exactly log&#8322;(n), which is why a fair die is 2.58 bits and a fair 8-sided die is exactly 3.

## Why rare events carry more information

The term inside the sum, &minus;log&#8322;(p), is the <strong>information content</strong> of a single outcome &mdash; how surprising it is.

|Probability|Information|
|---|---|
|1.0|0 bits|
|0.5|1 bit|
|0.25|2 bits|
|0.01|6.6 bits|
|0.001|10 bits|

"The sun rose today" carries almost no information because it was certain. "A meteor landed in the garden" carries a great deal. Entropy is the average of these surprises, weighted by how often each occurs.

The logarithm is not arbitrary: it makes information <em>additive</em>. Two independent events with probabilities p and q have joint probability pq, and &minus;log(pq) = &minus;log(p) &minus; log(q). Learning two independent facts gives you the sum of their information, which is the property any sensible measure must have.

## Where it does work in machine learning

<strong>Decision trees.</strong> Every split is chosen to reduce entropy the most. A node with 50/50 classes has 1 bit of entropy; a perfectly pure node has 0. The reduction is the information gain, and the algorithm greedily takes the largest.

<strong>Cross-entropy loss.</strong> The standard classification loss measures how many extra bits are needed to encode the true labels using the model's predicted distribution. Minimising it is making the model's beliefs match reality.

<strong>Feature selection.</strong> Mutual information &mdash; how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another &mdash; ranks features without assuming a linear relationship, which correlation cannot do.

<strong>Compression.</strong> Shannon's source coding theorem says entropy is the hard lower bound on average bits per symbol. English text has roughly 1&ndash;1.5 bits of entropy per character, which is why text compresses to a fraction of its size and random bytes do not compress at all.

<strong>Language models.</strong> Perplexity is 2 raised to the cross-entropy &mdash; the effective number of equally likely choices the model is deciding between at each step. Lower is better, and it is entropy wearing a friendlier scale.

## Weigh a distribution's surprise

Entropy in bits for several distributions over the same four outcomes, plus the coin curve that peaks exactly at a fair coin.

```python-run
import numpy as np

def entropy(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    p = p[p > 0]                       # 0 log 0 is defined as 0
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum() + 0.0

cases = {
    "uniform over 4":    [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25],
    "slightly skewed":   [0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10],
    "very skewed":       [0.90, 0.06, 0.03, 0.01],
    "certain":           [1.00, 0.00, 0.00, 0.00],
}
for name, p in cases.items():
    print("  %-18s H = %.4f bits" % (name, abs(entropy(p))))
print()
print("4 equally likely outcomes need log2(4) = %.0f bits. certainty needs 0."
      % np.log2(4))
print()
print("a coin, as it becomes unfair:")
for q in (0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999):
    print("  P(heads)=%-6s H = %.4f bits" % (q, entropy([q, 1 - q])))
print()
print("surprise per outcome:")
for p in (0.9, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01):
    print("  an event of probability %-5s carries %5.2f bits" % (p, -np.log2(p)))
print()
print("entropy is the average of those surprises, weighted by probability.")
```

## Why machine learning cares

- <strong>Decision trees</strong> choose the split that reduces entropy the most. That reduction is called information gain, and it is literally "how much uncertainty did this question remove".
- <strong>Cross-entropy loss</strong> measures the average surprise of the true labels <em>under your model's distribution</em>. Minimising it means making the truth unsurprising to the model.
- <strong>KL divergence</strong> is the gap between the cross-entropy and the true entropy &mdash; the extra bits you pay for using the wrong distribution.
- <strong>Compression</strong> gets the same number from the other direction: entropy is the theoretical floor on the average bits per symbol, which is why a file of random bytes will not compress.

## Bits, nats and why it rarely matters

Using log base 2 gives bits, which is the interpretable unit and the one this page uses. Machine learning code almost always uses the natural log instead, giving <em>nats</em>. The two differ by a constant factor of ln(2) &asymp; 0.693, and since optimisation only cares about which direction is downhill, a constant factor changes nothing about training. It does mean a cross-entropy loss printed by a framework is in nats, so 0.693 is the "fair coin" value there rather than 1.0.

## Common mistakes

- <strong>Feeding it a zero.</strong> log(0) is undefined. The convention is that 0·log(0) = 0, which is the correct limit, but a naive implementation returns NaN. Clamp probabilities away from zero.
- <strong>Reading entropy as a probability.</strong> It is measured in bits and is unbounded above as outcomes are added &mdash; eight outcomes can reach 3, sixteen can reach 4. Only the fraction-of-maximum reading is comparable across different-sized distributions.
- <strong>Confusing it with variance.</strong> Both measure spread, but entropy ignores the <em>values</em> entirely. Relabelling the outcomes changes variance and leaves entropy untouched.
- <strong>Expecting Gini and entropy to disagree.</strong> On classification splits they almost never pick different features. Choosing between them is not worth tuning.

## Where that leaves you

Entropy is the average surprise of a distribution, &minus;&Sigma;p·log(p), and reads as the number of yes/no questions needed to pin down the outcome. It is maximal when every outcome is equally likely and exactly zero when one outcome is certain, which is why it works as a measure of uncertainty. Every classification loss in machine learning is built from it: cross-entropy is the average surprise of the true labels under your model, and information gain &mdash; the entropy a split removes &mdash; is how a decision tree decides what to ask.

## Information gain, worked through

A decision tree splitting 10 samples that are 5 yes and 5 no:

- <strong>Before</strong>: H = 1.0 bit (maximum uncertainty).
- <strong>Split on "is it sunny?"</strong>: sunny gives 4 samples, all yes (H = 0); not sunny gives 6 samples, 1 yes and 5 no (H = 0.65).
- <strong>Weighted entropy after</strong>: (4/10)&times;0 + (6/10)&times;0.65 = 0.39 bits.
- <strong>Information gain</strong>: 1.0 &minus; 0.39 = <strong>0.61 bits</strong>.

The tree computes this for every feature and every threshold, and keeps the winner. Gini impurity is the cheaper alternative that almost always picks the same split &mdash; it approximates the same idea without the logarithm.

There is a known bias worth guarding against: information gain favours features with many distinct values, because splitting into many small pure groups always looks good. A unique ID column would score perfectly and generalise not at all. The <em>gain ratio</em>, which divides by the entropy of the split itself, corrects for this.

## The family of related measures

|Measure|Question it answers|
|---|---|
|Entropy H(X)|How uncertain is X?|
|Conditional entropy H(X\|Y)|How uncertain is X once Y is known?|
|Mutual information I(X;Y)|How much does knowing Y reduce uncertainty about X?|
|Cross-entropy H(p, q)|Cost of encoding p using a code built for q|
|KL divergence D(p\|\|q)|The extra cost &mdash; cross-entropy minus entropy|

Mutual information deserves particular attention as a feature-selection tool. Unlike correlation, it detects <em>any</em> dependence, including non-linear ones: a perfect parabolic relationship has near-zero correlation and high mutual information. It is zero exactly when the variables are independent.

KL divergence is not symmetric &mdash; D(p||q) &ne; D(q||p) &mdash; so it is a divergence rather than a distance. That asymmetry has real consequences in variational inference, where which direction you minimise determines whether the fitted distribution covers all the modes or concentrates on one.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Why base 2?</strong> Because it gives the answer in bits, which correspond to yes/no questions. Base e gives nats, and base 10 gives dits &mdash; only the units change.

<strong>What is the maximum entropy?</strong> log&#8322;(n) for n outcomes, reached when all are equally likely.

<strong>Is high entropy good or bad?</strong> Neither &mdash; it depends. High entropy in your labels means a hard problem; high entropy in a decision tree leaf means the split did not help; high entropy in a generated distribution means diversity.

<strong>What is the difference between entropy and cross-entropy?</strong> Entropy is the uncertainty of one distribution. Cross-entropy measures one distribution against another, and equals entropy plus KL divergence.

<strong>Why do trees use Gini instead of entropy?</strong> It is cheaper (no logarithm) and agrees with entropy on the chosen split the overwhelming majority of the time.

<strong>Can entropy be negative?</strong> Not for discrete distributions. Differential entropy for continuous ones can be, which is one of several reasons the continuous version needs more care.

## Recap in one screen

- Entropy is average surprise, in bits: `H = -Σ p log₂ p`.
- Equally likely outcomes give maximum entropy; a certain outcome gives zero.
- Rare events carry more information, and the log makes information additive.
- Decision trees split to reduce entropy; cross-entropy loss trains classifiers; perplexity is entropy for language models.
- Mutual information detects any dependence, not just linear ones, which makes it a stronger feature-selection signal than correlation.
