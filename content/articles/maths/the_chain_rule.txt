title: The Chain Rule
intro: Derivatives of nested functions multiply &mdash; and that fact is the entire mechanism of backpropagation.

## Before the details

When a function feeds into another &mdash; <span class="mono-font">y = f(g(x))</span> &mdash; a change in <span class="mono-font">x</span> must travel through <em>both</em> to reach <span class="mono-font">y</span>. The chain rule says the sensitivities simply multiply.

## The Gear Analogy

Picture two gears. Turning the first makes the second turn 3&times; as fast; that second gear drives a third at 2&times;. Turn the first gear once and the last one spins <strong>6</strong> times &mdash; the ratios multiplied.

Derivatives are exactly these ratios: <em>how much does the output move per unit of input?</em> Press <strong>Nudge x</strong> and watch a small change in x produce a change in u, which produces a change in y &mdash; each link scaling the one before.

## Reading the Diagram

The chain strip shows <span class="mono-font">x &rarr; u &rarr; y</span> with each link's local derivative underneath. The app also computes a <strong>numeric check</strong> &mdash; the slope measured by actually nudging x by a tiny amount &mdash; and it always matches the product. The rule is not an approximation.

## This Is Backpropagation

A neural network is a deep composition: input &rarr; layer 1 &rarr; layer 2 &rarr; &hellip; &rarr; loss. To learn, you need the derivative of the loss with respect to a weight buried near the start. The chain rule provides it by multiplying every local derivative along the path:

Backpropagation is nothing more than this product, computed efficiently from the output backwards so shared terms are reused.

## Why Deep Networks Struggle

Multiplying many numbers together is unstable. If each local derivative is <strong>less than 1</strong>, the product shrinks toward zero &mdash; the <em>vanishing gradient</em>. If each is greater than 1, it explodes.

Choose the <strong>sigmoid</strong> outer function and slide x outwards. Its derivative never exceeds 0.25, so stacking ten sigmoid layers scales the gradient by at most 0.25&#185;&#8304; &asymp; 0.00000095. That is precisely why ReLU replaced sigmoid in deep networks, and why LSTMs added a gated path that avoids repeated multiplication.

## Exploration guide

1. <strong>Start with g = 2x+1, f = u&sup2;.</strong> The product of local slopes matches the numeric check exactly.
2. <strong>Press Nudge.</strong> Watch a small &Delta;x become a larger &Delta;u and then a larger &Delta;y &mdash; magnification compounding through the chain.
3. <strong>Set the inner function to sin(x)</strong> and slide to where its slope is zero. The <em>total</em> derivative collapses to zero too &mdash; one flat link kills the whole chain, however steep the others are.
4. <strong>Switch the outer to sigmoid</strong> and push x to &plusmn;3. dy/du shrinks toward nothing: the saturation that stalls deep networks.

## The short of it

Nested functions multiply their slopes. That gives you the derivative of arbitrarily deep compositions &mdash; and because multiplication compounds, it also explains why gradients vanish or explode in deep networks.
