title: Lagrange Multipliers
intro: At a smooth constrained optimum, the objective’s gradient is aligned with the constraint’s gradient.

## The tangency condition

To optimise f(x,y) subject to g(x,y) = c, form the Lagrangian L = f − λ(g − c). At a regular constrained optimum, the gradients satisfy ∇f = λ∇g. The constraint curve and a level curve of f are tangent there: moving along the constraint gives no first-order improvement.

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="gradient f equals lambda times gradient g, with g equals c">∇f = λ∇g, &nbsp; g = c</div>

Example: maximise xy subject to x + y = 10 with x,y > 0. Set ∇(xy) = (y,x) and ∇(x+y) = (1,1), so y = λ and x = λ; hence x = y = 5 and the maximum product is 25. The boundary condition and positivity are part of the problem, not optional details.

## What the multiplier means

When the optimum changes smoothly as c changes, λ measures the local change in the optimal objective per unit change in the constraint. This interpretation is often called a shadow price. It can fail at nonsmooth points or when the active constraint set changes. Inequality constraints require the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, not just this equality rule.

See [convexity and optimisation](convexity_and_optimisation.html) for global guarantees and [derivatives](derivatives_and_slope.html) for the gradient condition.

## Check candidates against the actual constraint

For the example x+y=10, substituting y=10−x reduces xy to x(10−x)=10x−x², a downward parabola with its maximum at x=5. Thus (5,5) is the constrained maximum, not just a point satisfying the gradient equation. If the feasible set included endpoints or were unbounded, those boundaries would need checking too. The multiplier method finds stationary candidates where the constraint is smooth and its gradient is nonzero; it does not classify every candidate automatically.

Geometrically, the objective's level curves meet the constraint at the optimum without a first-order direction along the constraint that improves f. The gradients are therefore parallel. With several equality constraints, the gradient of f is a linear combination of the active constraint gradients. If gradients are dependent or the constraint has a corner, the simple tangency condition may fail and other methods are needed. For inequality constraints, KKT conditions add complementary slackness and feasibility. Compare with [convex optimisation](convexity_and_optimisation.html): convexity can turn a valid local candidate into a global optimum.

## Try this above

Move the constraint and watch where it touches a level curve of the objective. Compare the optimum and multiplier as the constraint value changes.

With two equality constraints, a stationary solution satisfies ∇f=λ₁∇g₁+λ₂∇g₂ together with both constraint equations. The multipliers describe sensitivity to the constraint levels when the optimum changes smoothly. Their values depend on how each constraint is scaled: replacing g=c by 10g=10c divides its associated multiplier by 10. The feasible problem is unchanged, but the numeric multiplier changes. Interpret a multiplier only after checking the units and exact form of the constraint.

The example has a maximum because xy=25 at (5,5) and the product falls as x moves away while y=10−x. The equality condition alone also has stationary candidates that require classification in other problems. For an unconstrained boundary or an unbounded feasible set, an optimum may not exist. Solve the constraint equations, check feasibility and boundaries, and compare objective values when necessary; a multiplier equation is a candidate-generation tool, not a complete proof in every setting.
