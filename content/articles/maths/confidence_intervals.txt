title: Confidence Intervals
intro: A confidence interval is a procedure that uses sample data to produce a range for an unknown population quantity.

## Construct and interpret the interval

For a sample mean with known population standard deviation under a normal model (or an appropriate large-sample approximation), a two-sided confidence interval is x̄ ± z*σ/√n. In practice, σ is often unknown and a t critical value with estimated standard error is used under the relevant assumptions.

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="estimate plus or minus critical value times standard error">estimate ± critical value × standard error</div>

A 95% confidence procedure produces intervals that contain the fixed parameter in about 95% of repeated samples under the model. After one interval is calculated, frequentist interpretation does not assign a 95% probability to the fixed parameter being inside that particular interval.

## Width and assumptions

Larger samples usually narrow the interval because standard error shrinks; greater variability or higher confidence widens it. The interval’s validity depends on the sampling design, model, and method. A narrow interval from biased or dependent data is not reliable. See [sampling distributions](sampling_distributions.html), [standard deviation](mean_variance_standard_deviation.html), and [hypothesis testing](hypothesis_testing_and_p_values.html).

## Work through an interval calculation

The pattern is estimate ± critical value × standard error. For a sample mean with known population σ, suppose x̄=50, σ=10, and n=100. Then SE=10/√100=1. A 95% normal critical value is about 1.96, giving 50±1.96, or approximately (48.04, 51.96). In most real problems σ is unknown; a t critical value and sample standard deviation are used when the design and assumptions support them. For a proportion or a complex survey, the formula may be different.

The confidence level describes repeated performance of the procedure: under the model, about 95% of intervals from repeated samples would cover the fixed parameter. It is not a 95% probability statement about this already-calculated interval in the usual frequentist interpretation. A confidence interval also differs from a prediction interval for an individual future observation, which includes individual variation and is wider. State the parameter and method along with the endpoints. A narrow interval cannot fix bias, nonresponse, or dependence in the sample.

## Try this above

Increase the sample size and then the confidence level. Observe how each change affects the interval width.

The half-width in the example is 1.96 standard errors. If the confidence level stays fixed, quadrupling n halves the standard error and approximately halves the interval's width. To make a margin of error half as large, one generally needs about four times as many independent observations. This planning rule addresses random sampling variation only. If participants self-select or the measurement systematically misses the target, collecting more of the same biased data can produce a narrower but still misleading interval.

Two intervals can overlap while a test of their difference is statistically significant, or appear separate while a formal comparison has different assumptions. Comparing separate confidence intervals is not always equivalent to a confidence interval for the difference. If the scientific question is a contrast between groups, calculate an interval for that contrast directly. Also distinguish a confidence interval for a population mean from a prediction interval for a new measurement; they target different quantities and have different widths.
