title: Vector Norms
intro: A norm measures vector size; the choice of norm changes what “small” means in geometry and optimisation.

## Three common norms

For x = (x₁, …, xₙ), the L1 norm sums absolute components, the L2 norm is Euclidean length, and the L∞ norm is the largest absolute component:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="L one norm is sum absolute x i; L two norm is square root sum x i squared; L infinity norm is maximum absolute x i">‖x‖₁ = Σ|xᵢ| &nbsp; ‖x‖₂ = √(Σxᵢ²) &nbsp; ‖x‖∞ = max|xᵢ|</div>

For x = (3, −4), these are 7, 5, and 4. The L2 unit ball is a circle in two dimensions; the L1 ball is a diamond; the L∞ ball is a square. Those shapes explain why different norms favour different solutions.

## Why the choice matters

L2 penalises large components smoothly and is common in weight decay. L1 can encourage exact zeros in optimisation, producing sparse coefficients. L∞ limits the largest single component. These are tendencies, not guarantees: the resulting solution also depends on the objective, constraints, and data. For regularisation details, see [convexity and optimisation](convexity_and_optimisation.html).

Every norm is nonnegative, is zero only for the zero vector, scales as ‖cx‖ = |c|‖x‖, and obeys the triangle inequality. Squared L2, ‖x‖₂², is useful in losses but is not itself a norm because it fails the triangle inequality.
