title: Vector Norms
intro: A norm measures vector size; the choice of norm changes what “small” means in geometry and optimisation.

## Three common norms

For x = (x₁, …, xₙ), the L1 norm sums absolute components, the L2 norm is Euclidean length, and the L∞ norm is the largest absolute component:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="L one norm is sum absolute x i; L two norm is square root sum x i squared; L infinity norm is maximum absolute x i">‖x‖₁ = Σ|xᵢ| &nbsp; ‖x‖₂ = √(Σxᵢ²) &nbsp; ‖x‖∞ = max|xᵢ|</div>

For x = (3, −4), these are 7, 5, and 4. The L2 unit ball is a circle in two dimensions; the L1 ball is a diamond; the L∞ ball is a square. Those shapes explain why different norms favour different solutions.

## Why the choice matters

L2 penalises large components smoothly and is common in weight decay. L1 can encourage exact zeros in optimisation, producing sparse coefficients. L∞ limits the largest single component. These are tendencies, not guarantees: the resulting solution also depends on the objective, constraints, and data. For regularisation details, see [convexity and optimisation](convexity_and_optimisation.html).

Every norm is nonnegative, is zero only for the zero vector, scales as ‖cx‖ = |c|‖x‖, and obeys the triangle inequality. Squared L2, ‖x‖₂², is useful in losses but is not itself a norm because it fails the triangle inequality.

## A norm is a length rule with consistency properties

A norm must be nonnegative, equal zero only for the zero vector, scale by |c| when the vector is multiplied by c, and obey the triangle inequality ‖x+y‖≤‖x‖+‖y‖. The last rule says that going through an intermediate point cannot create a shorter route than the direct distance. These conditions distinguish a norm from an arbitrary formula that happens to return a number.

For x=(3,−4), L1=7 is the number of axis-aligned units, L2=5 is straight-line length, and L∞=4 is the largest coordinate change. In optimisation, adding an L1 penalty may make some fitted coefficients exactly zero, while L2 usually shrinks coefficients continuously; neither effect is guaranteed without considering the rest of the objective. Squared L2 is convenient because it is differentiable, but it fails the scaling rule: ‖2x‖₂²=4‖x‖₂² rather than 2‖x‖₂². That is why it is a useful penalty but not a norm.

## Try this above

Compare the displayed unit shapes, then change the vector components. Notice that the same vector has a different length under L1, L2, and L∞.

Norms connect naturally to distances: the distance from x to y induced by a norm is ‖x−y‖. In two dimensions, the L2 unit circle contains points whose Euclidean length is one; the L1 unit diamond instead limits the sum of absolute coordinates. Thus the direction of a vector may be unchanged while its reported size differs. The choice is consequential in a regularised model: L1 and L2 penalties encode different preferences, so compare them using validation and the problem's interpretation rather than assuming one is universally superior.

The triangle inequality gives a useful bound in numerical analysis: ‖x+y‖≤‖x‖+‖y‖, so small perturbations cannot change a vector by more than the perturbation's norm. For a matrix, induced norms measure its maximum stretching under a chosen vector norm. Norms therefore appear in error bounds and convergence guarantees as well as geometry. Always identify which norm is used because a statement in L2 need not have the same numerical value in L1 or L∞.
