title: Logarithms
intro: The inverse of raising to a power &mdash; and the reason loss functions are full of logs.

## Quick Context

A logarithm asks: <em>what exponent turns the base into this count?</em>

Logs and exponentials are the same relationship read in opposite ways. Tick <em>show b^x</em> and you will see the two curves mirror each other across the diagonal.

The notation is worth getting comfortable with early. log&#8322;(8) = 3 since 2&sup3; = 8. log&#8321;&#8320;(1000) = 3 since 10&sup3; = 1000. ln(e) = 1 since e&sup1; = e. Each logarithm is a question about an exponent, answered by the base.

Logarithms appear throughout machine learning for two reasons. The first is numerical: probabilities multiply. And multiplying many counts below 1 gives vals too small for a computer to represent. Taking logs turns that product into a sum of negative counts. It stays within floating-point range. Cross-entropy loss, log-likelihood, and the log-probability scores in language nets all rely on this change.

The second reason is geometric. A logarithmic scale compresses large ranges into manageable ones. A learning-rate search from 0.0001 to 1.0 covers four orders of magnitude. And plotting it on a log scale gives each order the same visual space. Loss curves, histogram bins. And feature distributions that span a few orders of magnitude are all clearer on a log axis.

The log function grows slowly. Doubling the input adds a constant to the output, no matter how large the input now is. That property makes log(n) the time complexity of binary search and balanced tree steps. And it is why algorithms with logarithmic factors scale well to large inputs.

Three bases dominate: base 2 in information theory, base 10 in engineering and scientific notation. And base e (the natural logarithm) in calculus and machine learning. Switching between them is a multiplication by a constant. So the choice of base changes the scale but not the shape of any curve.

## The Property That Matters Most

A logarithm turns <strong>multiplication into addition</strong>. Change the two counts in the product demo and watch both sides stay equal. This is not a curiosity &mdash; it is why logs are all over in machine learning.

Probabilities multiply. Multiply a thousand counts below 1 and you get something so small the computer rounds it to zero &mdash; <em>numerical underflow</em>. Take logs and that product turns a sum of manageable negative counts. Each build of max likelihood, cross-entropy loss and Naive Bayes relies on this exact substitution.

## The Three Bases You Will Meet

- <strong>Base 2</strong> &mdash; information theory. log&#8322; counts <em>bits</em>: how many yes/no questions to pin something down. Entropy is measured this way.
- <strong>Base e &asymp; 2.71828</strong> &mdash; the natural log, written <span class="mono-font">ln</span>. It appears all over in calculus since its derivative is simply <span class="mono-font">1/x</span>. This is the default in ML loss functions.
- <strong>Base 10</strong> &mdash; human-readable scales: decibels, pH, the Richter scale.

The base just rescales the curve &mdash; each log is a constant multiple of each other. Watch the three readouts stay in fixed proportion as you slide x.

## Log Scales Tame Huge Ranges

Notice how brutally the curve flattens. Going from 1 to 10 climbs as much as going from 10 to 100. Or 100 to 1000 &mdash; each <em>multiplication</em> by 10 adds a constant amount. Tick <em>log-scale the x axis</em> and those equal jumps turn evenly spaced.

That compression is why loss curves are so often plotted on a log axis: it makes an improvement from 0.001 to 0.0001 as visible as one from 1.0 to 0.1.

## The Domain Restriction

Logs are just set for <strong>x &gt; 0</strong>. As x ways 0 the curve dives to negative infinity. At 0 and below there is no answer at all, since no power of a positive base ever gives zero or a negative count.

This causes real bugs. Cross-entropy computes <span class="mono-font">&minus;log(p)</span>. So a net that confidently predicts probability 0 for the correct answer gives infinite loss and NaNs all over. Each framework quietly clamps p away from zero for just this reason. Slide x below about 0.1 and watch the value plunge.

## The inverse of exponentiation

A logarithm answers one question: <strong>what power do I need?</strong>

<p class="mono-font">log&#8322;(8) = 3&nbsp;&nbsp;since&nbsp;&nbsp;2&sup3; = 8</p>

The base (2 here) is the count being raised. The logarithm is the exponent. Three bases account for nearly all you will meet:

|Base|Written|Used for|
|---|---|---|
|2|log&#8322;|Information, bits, algorithm complexity|
|e &asymp; 2.718|ln|Calculus, statistics, machine learning|
|10|log&#8321;&#8320;|Decibels, pH, orders of magnitude|

The natural log is the default in machine learning since its derivative is the tidiest possible: d/dx ln(x) = 1/x. Each log in a loss function is a natural log unless it says otherwise.

## The property that does all the work

<p class="mono-font">log(a &times; b) = log(a) + log(b)</p>

Logarithms turn multiplication into addition. That one identity is why they were invented. And why they are still all over in machine learning.

The tied rules:

- log(a / b) = log(a) &minus; log(b)
- log(a&#8319;) = n &times; log(a)
- log(1) = 0, for any base
- log(0) is undefined and tends to &minus;&infin;

The real consequence appears at once in probability. A net computing the joint probability of 1,000 independent events multiplies 1,000 counts below 1 &mdash; and the result underflows to just zero in floating point, destroying the math.

```python
0.1 ** 400        # 0.0  -- underflow: the information is gone

import math
sum(math.log(0.1) for _ in range(400))    # -921.03  -- fine
```

Taking logs turns that product into a sum of 1,000 manageable counts. This is why each build works in log-probabilities: Naive Bayes, hidden Markov nets, language nets. And the log-likelihood that underlies most of statistics.

## Compressing scale

Logarithms squash large ranges into small ones. Data spanning 1 to 1,000,000 spans 0 to 6 after a log&#8321;&#8320;.

That makes them the standard fix for skewed features. Income, city population, website visits and word frequencies all have long right tails. There a few enormous vals dominate each distance math and each squared error. A log transform pulls the tail in and often turns a curved relationship into a straight one. It is just what a linear net wants.

Two real notes. `log(0)` is undefined, so use `log1p(x)` (which computes log(1 + x)) when zeros are present. And remember to invert the transform before reporting counts &mdash; a mean of logged vals back-transforms to a geometric mean, not the ordinary one.

Log scales on charts do the same job visually: exponential growth turns a straight line. And small vals stop being invisible next to large ones.

## What logs do to counts

Logs turn multiplication into addition and squash range. Both properties are the reason they show up throughout machine learning.

```python-run
import numpy as np

print("log turns multiplication into addition:")
a, b = 400.0, 25.0
print("  log(a*b)      = %.6f" % np.log(a * b))
print("  log(a)+log(b) = %.6f" % (np.log(a) + np.log(b)))
print()
print("and powers into multiplication:")
print("  log(a**7)     = %.6f" % np.log(a ** 7))
print("  7*log(a)      = %.6f" % (7 * np.log(a)))
print()
print("it squashes a huge range into a small one:")
for x in (1, 10, 1_000, 1_000_000, 10 ** 12):
    print("  %14d -> log10 %5.1f   ln %7.3f" % (x, np.log10(x), np.log(x)))
print()
print("which is why likelihoods are summed as logs, not multiplied:")
print("%8s %26s %16s" % ("n", "0.6 multiplied n times", "logs summed"))
for n in (100, 800, 2000, 5000):
    probs = np.full(n, 0.6)
    print("%8d %26s %16.2f" % (n, probs.prod(), np.log(probs).sum()))
print()
print("past about n=1500 the product jams against the smallest number a float")
print("can hold and stops moving, so every further term is invisible to it.")
print("the sum of logs keeps counting, linearly, forever.")
print()
print("changing base is just a constant factor:")
x = 64.0
print("  log2(64)     = %.4f" % np.log2(x))
print("  ln(64)/ln(2) = %.4f" % (np.log(x) / np.log(2)))
```

## Guided tour

1. <strong>Set base 2 and x = 8.</strong> The answer is just 3 &mdash; three doublings from 1 reaches 8.
2. <strong>Keep the base and try x = 16, then 32.</strong> Each doubling of x adds just 1 to the log.
3. <strong>Slide x toward 0.</strong> The curve dives without limit &mdash; the underflow trap in one picture.
4. <strong>Change p and q in the product demo</strong> and confirm log(p&times;q) = log(p) + log(q) each time.
5. <strong>Raise the base toward 10.</strong> The curve flattens: a more base needs fewer powers to reach the same count.

## In one line

A log is an exponent in disguise. It turns products into sums, compresses vast ranges into readable ones. And is undefined at zero &mdash; three facts that between them explain log-likelihood, cross-entropy loss, entropy in bits. And most NaN bugs in training code.

## Logs inside loss functions

Cross-entropy, the standard classification loss, is built from logarithms:

<p class="mono-font">loss = &minus;[ y log(p) + (1 &minus; y) log(1 &minus; p) ]</p>

Read what it does for a true label of 1. If the net predicts 0.9, the loss is &minus;log(0.9) = 0.105 &mdash; small. If it predicts 0.1, the loss is &minus;log(0.1) = 2.303 &mdash; over twenty times more. And as the prediction ways 0, the loss grows without bound.

That unbounded growth is the design. A net that is <em>confidently</em> wrong is punished far more than one that is uncertainly wrong. It is precisely the behaviour you want from a probability estimator. Squared error, by contrast, caps the cost at 1 and gives confidently wrong predictions a gentle nudge.

The other reason logs appear here is calculus: log turns the product of many probabilities into a sum. And derivatives of sums are trivial. Maximising a likelihood turns maximising a log-likelihood. And the optimum is in the same place since log is monotonic.

## Logs in complexity

Algorithm review uses log&#8322;, and the intuition is halving. Binary search halves the search space each step. So a list of 1,000,000 takes about 20 comparisons, since 2&sup2;&#8304; &asymp; 1,000,000.

|n|log&#8322;(n)|
|---|---|
|8|3|
|1,000|~10|
|1,000,000|~20|
|1,000,000,000|~30|

That table is worth internalising, since it explains why O(log n) algorithms feel instantaneous at any realistic scale. And why O(n log n) sorting is close enough to linear that nobody worries about it.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is the gap between `log` and `ln`?</strong> `ln` is base e. Plain `log` means base 10 in mathematics and base e in most programming languages &mdash; check the library rather than assuming.

<strong>Why is log(0) undefined?</strong> No power of any base gives zero. The vals tend to &minus;infinity. In code, add a small epsilon or use `log1p`.

<strong>Can I take the log of a negative count?</strong> Not with real counts. Shift the data first if you need to.

<strong>When should I log-transform a feature?</strong> When it is strongly right-skewed, spans a few orders of magnitude. Or has a multiplicative relationship with the target.

<strong>Does the base matter?</strong> For the mathematics, no &mdash; changing base multiplies by a constant. For interpretation it does: bits are base 2, nats are base e.

<strong>Why do nets use log-probabilities?</strong> To avoid underflow and to turn products into sums. It are numerically stable and easy to differentiate.

## Recap in one screen

- A logarithm is the exponent: log&#8322;(8) = 3 since 2&sup3; = 8.
- log(a&times;b) = log(a) + log(b) &mdash; multiplication turns addition, which stops underflow.
- Logs compress skewed data, straighten multiplicative relationships and make charts readable.
- Cross-entropy uses log so that confident mistakes are punished without limit.
- log&#8322;(n) is "how many halvings". It is why binary search on a million items takes 20 steps.
