title: Projections: A Practical Guide
intro: The nearest point on a line, and the error you cannot get rid of.

## Quick Context

Projection answers one question: of all the points on the line through a. It is closest to b? The answer is b's shadow. And the line from b down to that shadow is perpendicular. Those two facts &mdash; closest point, perpendicular error &mdash; are the same fact. And almost each fitting way in machine learning is built on it.

Linear regression fits a line (or a plane, or a hyperplane) by projecting the target vector onto the column space of the feature matrix. The projection is the predicted vals. The perpendicular drop is the residual. The normal equation computes this right. And gradient descent converges to the same point. Grasp projections makes the connection between geometry and the algebra of least squares visible: the residual is orthogonal to each feature column. It is another way of saying the features have zero correlation with the error.

The same idea extends beyond regression. Principal part review projects data onto the ways of max variance. The Gram-Schmidt process builds an orthonormal basis by projecting each vector onto the span of the last ones and subtracting. In signal handling, the Fourier transform projects a signal onto sine and cosine basis functions. In each case, the projection extracts the part of the vector that lies along a chosen way. And the residual is all that does not.

Two special cases are worth visualising. When the vector being projected is now on the line, the projection is the vector it and the residual is zero. When the vector is perpendicular to the line, the projection is zero and the residual is the whole vector. Each other case falls between these extremes. And the angle between the vector and the line finds how much of the first vector survives the projection.

## The formula, and where it comes from

proj&#8336;(b) = ( (a &middot; b) / (a &middot; a) ) a

The projection has to lie on the line. So it must be some multiple <span class="mono-font">t&middot;a</span>. The residual <span class="mono-font">b &minus; t&middot;a</span> has to be perpendicular to a. So its <a href="vectors_and_dot_product.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">dot product</a> with a is zero. Solve <span class="mono-font">a &middot; (b &minus; t&middot;a) = 0</span> for t and you get <span class="mono-font">t = (a &middot; b) / ‖a‖&sup2;</span>. That is the whole derivation.

Two things worth noticing. The length of a cancels out &mdash; just its way matters. It is why doubling a leaves the shadow just where it was. And t can be negative. It simply means the shadow falls on the far side of the origin.

The <strong>scalar</strong> projection is <span class="mono-font">(a &middot; b) / ‖a‖</span>, the signed length of the shadow. The <strong>vector</strong> projection is that length pointed along a. Mixing the two up is the most common slip here.

## Casting a shadow onto a way

A projection answers: how much of vector `a` lies along the way of vector `b`? Picture the sun right cost of `b` and `a` casting a shadow onto it.

<p class="mono-font">proj<sub>b</sub>(a) = ( (a &middot; b) / (b &middot; b) ) b</p>

The fraction is a count saying how far along b to go. Multiplying by b turns it back into a vector.

For a = [3, 4] and b = [1, 0] (the x-axis):

- a &middot; b = 3, b &middot; b = 1. So the projection is 3 &times; [1, 0] = <strong>[3, 0]</strong>.

Which is just what you would expect: the shadow of [3, 4] on the x-axis is [3, 0]. And the part left over, [0, 4], is perpendicular to b.

That decomposition is the point of the whole topic. Any vector splits uniquely into a part along a way and a part at right angles to it:

<p class="mono-font">a = proj<sub>b</sub>(a) + (a &minus; proj<sub>b</sub>(a))</p>

The second term is the <strong>residual</strong>. And it is perpendicular to b by construction.

## Least squares is a projection

Linear regression fits a line by minimising squared residuals. Geometrically it is projecting.

The target vector `y` lives in n-dimensional space (one dimension per data point). The predictions the net can give &mdash; each possible combination of the feature columns &mdash; form a subspace, often of far less dimension. Almost certainly, `y` is not in that subspace. It is why a perfect fit is impossible.

The best approximation is the point in the subspace <em>closest</em> to y. And that is just the projection of y onto it. The residual is what is left over. And the setting property is that it is perpendicular to each feature column:

<p class="mono-font">X&#7488;(y &minus; X&beta;) = 0&nbsp;&nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp;&nbsp;&beta; = (X&#7488;X)&#8315;&sup1;X&#7488;y</p>

That is the normal equation, derived without calculus. "Normal" here means perpendicular &mdash; the equation is stating that the residual is orthogonal to the columns of X.

This also explains a fact that puzzles people: the residuals of a fitted least-squares net sum to zero when an intercept is included. The constant column is one of the ways the residual must be perpendicular to.

## Orthogonality, and why it makes things easy

Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero &mdash; no shared way at all.

An <strong>orthonormal basis</strong> is a set of mutually orthogonal unit vectors. And it makes projection trivial: the part along each basis vector is just the dot product, with no division needed. Decomposing a vector turns a list of dot products.

This is why so much of applied linear algebra is spent constructing orthogonal bases:

- <strong>PCA</strong> gives orthogonal parts, so each carries information the others do not.
- <strong>QR decomposition</strong> factors a matrix into an orthogonal part and a triangular part. And is the numerically stable way to solve least squares &mdash; better than forming `XᵀX` and inverting it.
- <strong>Fourier and wavelet transforms</strong> project a signal onto an orthogonal basis of frequencies.
- <strong>Gram-Schmidt</strong> is the classic procedure for turning any independent set into an orthonormal one, by repeatedly subtracting projections.

## Split a vector in two

The projection and the residual. And the three properties that make least squares work.

```python-run
import numpy as np

a = np.array([4.0, 3.0])       # what we project
b = np.array([1.0, 0.0])       # onto this direction

proj = (a @ b) / (b @ b) * b
residual = a - proj

print("a        :", a)
print("onto b   :", proj)
print("residual :", residual)
print()
print("the residual is perpendicular to b: dot =", round(residual @ b, 12))
print("and the parts add back up:", np.allclose(proj + residual, a))
print()
print("|a|^2 = |proj|^2 + |residual|^2 :",
      np.allclose(a @ a, proj @ proj + residual @ residual))
print("which is Pythagoras, and is why least squares works.")
```

## Things to try

1. <strong>Read the default.</strong> a = (3, 1), b = (2, 3). The dot product is 9, ‖a‖&sup2; is 10. So t = 0.900 and the shadow lands at (2.70, 0.90) &mdash; short of b. And on the line.
2. <strong>Confirm the right angle.</strong> proj &middot; r reads 0.000. Drag anything you like and it stays 0.000, since that orthogonality is what sets the projection rather than a coincidence of these counts.
3. <strong>Stretch a.</strong> Double a to (6, 2). t halves to 0.450 and the shadow stays just where it was, at (2.70, 0.90). Just the way of a was ever used.
4. <strong>Make the shadow vanish.</strong> Point b at right angles to a &mdash; with a = (3, 1), try b = (-1, 3). The dot product is 0. So the projection collapses to the origin and the residual is the whole of b. a explains nothing about b.
5. <strong>Make the residual vanish.</strong> Point b along a instead. Now the shadow is b it and ‖r‖ is 0 &mdash; a explains all of b.
6. <strong>Go negative.</strong> Swing b behind the origin relative to a. t goes negative and the shadow points the other way down the line. It is just what a negative dot product means.

## Why this is the root of least squares

Fitting a line to data means solving <span class="mono-font">Xw = y</span> when there is no exact solution. The vector y almost never lies in the space that the columns of X can reach. The best you can do is find the point in that space closest to y &mdash; the projection of y onto the column space of X.

The residual has to be perpendicular to each column. So <span class="mono-font">X&#7488;(y &minus; Xw) = 0</span>. It rearranges into the normal equations <span class="mono-font">X&#7488;Xw = X&#7488;y</span>. That is where the closed-form solution of <a href="../machine_learning/linear_regression_with_ols.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">ordinary least squares</a> comes from: not calculus, geometry. The same picture on this page, in as many dimensions as you have features.

It is also why the residuals of a fitted linear net are uncorrelated with its predictors by construction. And why "the errors look structured" means your net is missing a way rather than being unlucky.

## Where else it shows up

- <strong>PCA.</strong> Projecting points onto a way and asking which way keeps the most spread &mdash; the residuals on that page are these residuals.
- <strong>Gram-Schmidt.</strong> Building an orthogonal basis by repeatedly subtracting off the projection onto what you now have.
- <strong>Cosine similarity.</strong> The projection of a unit vector onto another unit vector <em>is</em> the cosine of the angle between them.
- <strong>Attention.</strong> A dot product against a key vector is a projection, scaled &mdash; it measures how much of the query points along that key.

## The short of it

The projection of b onto a is <span class="mono-font">((a&middot;b)/‖a‖&sup2;)a</span>: the point on the line through a that is closest to b. And the just one whose residual is perpendicular to a. Just a's way matters, so scaling a changes nothing. The scale factor goes negative when the shadow falls behind the origin, zero when the vectors are perpendicular. And equals b just when they are parallel. Least squares is this picture with a subspace in place of a line. The fitted vals are a projection of y, the residual is orthogonal to each predictor. And the normal equations are just that orthogonality written down.

## Projections in machine learning

<strong>Dimensionality reduction.</strong> PCA projects data onto the subspace spanned by the top principal parts. All perpendicular to that subspace is discarded. And the explained variance ratio measures how much of the data survived the projection.

<strong>Attention.</strong> Each token is projected into query, key and value spaces by learned matrices &mdash; three other views of the same vector, chosen so the comparison the net needs turns a simple dot product.

<strong>Embeddings.</strong> Projecting high-dimensional embeddings down to two or three dimensions is how they are visualised. The projection is lossy by construction. It is why t-SNE and UMAP plots should be read for structure, not for exact distances.

<strong>Regularised regression.</strong> Ridge regression shrinks the projection along low-variance ways more than along high-variance ones. It is just why it stabilises collinear problems.

<strong>Gradient projection.</strong> Constrained optimisation keeps params on a permitted surface by projecting each step back onto it after taking it.

## The projection matrix

Projecting onto a subspace can be written as a single matrix, applied to any vector:

<p class="mono-font">P = X (X&#7488;X)&#8315;&sup1; X&#7488;</p>

In statistics this is called the <strong>hat matrix</strong>, since it puts the hat on y: `ŷ = Py`.

It has two properties that find any projection matrix. It is idempotent &mdash; `P² = P`, since projecting something that is now in the subspace changes nothing. And it is symmetric.

Its diagonal entries are the <strong>uses</strong> vals, measuring how much each observation influences its own fitted value. A point with high uses sits far from the centre of the feature space and can pull the whole fit towards it. It is why uses is one of the standard regression diagnostics alongside residuals.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is the gap between projection and rotation?</strong> A rotation preserves lengths and is reversible. A projection discards a part and is not.

<strong>Can I project onto more than one way?</strong> Yes &mdash; onto any subspace. Project onto each vector of an orthonormal basis and add the pieces.

<strong>Why is the residual perpendicular?</strong> Since if it had any part along the subspace, you could move within the subspace and get closer &mdash; so the closest point is precisely the one where nothing is left pointing that way.

<strong>Is projection the same as dropping a column?</strong> Just in the special case of projecting onto an axis. General projections mix features rather than discarding them.

<strong>How does this relate to cosine similarity?</strong> The scalar projection of a onto b is `|a| cos θ`. Divide by |a| and you have the cosine it.

<strong>Why use QR instead of the normal equation?</strong> Numerical stability. Forming `XᵀX` squares the condition count. So QR (or SVD) gives more exact coefficients on ill-conditioned data.

## Recap in one screen

- A projection is the shadow of one vector onto a way or subspace.
- Each vector splits into a projected part and a perpendicular residual.
- Least squares projects the targets onto the space of achievable predictions. It is where the normal equation comes from.
- Orthonormal bases make projection a set of dot products. It is why PCA, QR and Fourier ways build them.
- The hat matrix is the projection in matrix form. And its diagonal gives each point's uses.
