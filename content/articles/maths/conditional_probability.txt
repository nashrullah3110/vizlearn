title: Conditional Probability: A Practical Guide
intro: What "given" actually does, and why swapping the two sides is the most expensive mistake in statistics.

## Quick Context

<a href="probability_basics.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Chance</a> counts favourable outcomes against all possible outcomes. Conditional chance changes one thing: it shrinks what counts as possible.<p>P(A | B) is read "the chance of A <em>given</em> B". The bar does not mean division and it does not mean "and". It means: assume B happened, throw away each outcome where it did not. And ask about A within what stays.</p>

The formula is P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). The denominator shrinks the sample space to B. The numerator counts just the outcomes where A is also true. It is not a new concept. It is the same ratio that chance always computes, just within a less universe.

The most common mistake is confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A). They are other counts unless A and B are free. P(has disease | tests positive) is not the same as P(tests positive | has disease). Swapping them leads to drastically wrong conclusions. And it happens constantly in medical testing, legal evidence and risk assessment. Bayes' theorem exists to turn one into the other, with the base rate of the disease as the bridge.

Independence has a precise meaning: A and B are free when P(A | B) = P(A), meaning that learning B happened does not change the chance of A. Equivalently, P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B). The multiplication rule that everyone memorises is just valid when this condition holds. Multiplying chances of events that are not free always understates the joint chance when the events tend to occur together. And always overstates it when they tend to exclude each other.

Conditional chance is the foundation of Bayesian reasoning, Markov chains, Naive Bayes sorters. And each probabilistic graphical net. The step is always the same: restrict the universe, recount within it.

## The formula is just a recount

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Look at what this does with the grid above. The denominator was 400 &mdash; the whole sample space. Conditioning on B replaces it with however many outcomes are in B. The numerator counts the outcomes that are in <em>both</em>, since those are the just ones in the shrunken world where A is also true.

Tick <strong>Condition on B</strong> and the picture does just this: all outside B is discarded. And the readout underneath shows the fraction as a plain count of dots. The formula is bookkeeping for that step, not a split idea.

## Independence, set well

A and B are free when knowing B tells you nothing about A:

P(A | B) = P(A)

Shrinking the world to B leaves A's share of it unchanged. Equivalently, and more symmetrically, P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B). Which is the multiplication rule everyone learns, now with the condition attached that makes it valid.

Multiplying chances when the events are <em>not</em> free is one of the most common errors in applied stats. And it always understates joint risk when the events tend to occur together.

## Chance, once you know something

Conditional chance is the chance of one event <em>given</em> that another has happened.

<p class="mono-font">P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)</p>

Read it as: restrict attention to the cases where B happened. And ask what fraction of those also have A. The denominator is the new, less universe.

A worked case on 100 people:

|&nbsp;|Owns a car|No car|Total|
|---|---|---|---|
|<strong>Lives in a city</strong>|20|40|60|
|<strong>Lives rurally</strong>|35|5|40|
|<strong>Total</strong>|55|45|100|

- P(owns a car) = 55/100 = <strong>0.55</strong>
- P(owns a car | lives rurally) = 35/40 = <strong>0.875</strong>
- P(owns a car | lives in a city) = 20/60 = <strong>0.33</strong>

Knowing where someone lives changes the answer sharply. It is the whole point: <strong>conditioning is what turns a population statistic into a prediction about an individual</strong>. Each supervised net is an estimate of P(target | features).

## Independence, stated well

Two events are free when conditioning changes nothing:

<p class="mono-font">P(A|B) = P(A)&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;equivalently&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B)</p>

In the table above, car ownership and location are clearly <em>not</em> free: 0.875 against 0.55. Two coin flips are free. Two words in the same sentence are not.

The distinction matters since independence is what lets chances multiply. Assume it wrongly and you double-count evidence, giving nets that are far too confident. Which is precisely the known behaviour of Naive Bayes on correlated features.

<strong>Mutually exclusive is not the same as free.</strong> If two events cannot both happen, then knowing one occurred tells you the other definitely did not. Which is maximal dependence, not independence.

## Chaining conditions

The definition rearranges into the multiplication rule. It extends to any count of events:

<p class="mono-font">P(A and B) = P(B) &times; P(A|B)</p>
<p class="mono-font">P(A, B, C) = P(A) &times; P(B|A) &times; P(C|A, B)</p>

This chain rule is the backbone of sequence modelling. A language net assigns a chance to a sentence by multiplying the chance of each word given all before it:

<p class="mono-font">P(w&#8321;, w&#8322;, &hellip;, w&#8345;) = &prod; P(w&#7522; | w&#8321; &hellip; w&#7522;&#8331;&#8321;)</p>

That is just what "predict the next token" means. And it is why a net trained just to guess the next word can give a chance to an whole document.

Since these products underflow, implementations sum log-chances instead. Which is where perplexity, the exponentiated average negative log-chance, comes from.

## Both ways, measured

P(A given B) and P(B given A) are other counts. Here is a population where you can count both.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 400_000

# 30% of people own a car; owners commute by car 80% of the time, others 5%
owns = rng.random(n) < 0.30
p_drive = np.where(owns, 0.80, 0.05)
drives = rng.random(n) < p_drive

print("P(owns)          %.4f" % owns.mean())
print("P(drives)        %.4f" % drives.mean())
print("P(owns and dr.)  %.4f" % (owns & drives).mean())
print()
print("P(drives | owns) %.4f   <- measured   0.80 by construction"
      % drives[owns].mean())
print("P(owns | drives) %.4f   <- the other direction, a different number"
      % owns[drives].mean())
print()
print("division, not magic:")
print("  P(A and B) / P(B) = %.4f / %.4f = %.4f"
      % ((owns & drives).mean(), drives.mean(),
         (owns & drives).mean() / drives.mean()))
```

## Try it yourself

1. <strong>Watch the world shrink.</strong> Tick <strong>Condition on B</strong>. The dots outside B fade out and P(A given B) is the fraction of the survivors that are blue. Untick it and the denominator goes back to all 400.
2. <strong>Find independence.</strong> Set the <strong>Preset</strong> to Free. P(A given B) and P(A) now read the same count. Knowing B happened has told you nothing at all about A.
3. <strong>Break it deliberately.</strong> Drag the <strong>Separation</strong> slider away from that setting. The two counts come apart at once. And Free flips to no. Independence is a knife-edge condition, not a typical one.
4. <strong>Make them incompatible.</strong> Set the <strong>Preset</strong> to Mutually exclusive. P(A and B) is zero. So P(A given B) is zero: if B happened, A certainly did not. Note that this is the <em>opposite</em> of free &mdash; B is now maximally informative.
5. <strong>See a certainty.</strong> Set the <strong>Preset</strong> to A inside B. Each outcome in A is also in B. So P(B given A) is just 1 while P(A given B) is much less. The two conditionals are nothing like each other.
6. <strong>Meet the base-rate trap.</strong> Set the <strong>Preset</strong> to Rare A, common B and check P(A given B) with P(B given A). One is small, the other is large, from the same two events. Reversing them is the error behind almost each misread medical test.

## P(A|B) is not P(B|A)

This is worth stating in its own section since it causes more real-world damage than any other confusion in chance.

"Given the patient has the disease, the test is positive 99% of the time" is a statement about P(positive | disease). What a patient wants to know is P(disease | positive). Those are other counts. And when the disease is rare they are wildly other. The second can be under 10% while the first is 99%.

The Rare A preset above shows the same asymmetry with nothing but dots. Turning between the two ways needs knowing how common the disease is to begin with. And the machinery for doing it is <a href="bayes_theorem.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Bayes' theorem</a>. It is the next module and is built right on this one.

## Where it shows up in machine learning

- <strong>Each sorter</strong> is estimating P(class | features). That is a conditional chance and nothing else.
- <strong><a href="../machine_learning/naive_bayes.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Naive Bayes</a></strong> is named for the assumption that features are conditionally free given the class. Which is often false. And works anyway.
- <strong>Precision and recall</strong> are the two ways of the same pair. Precision is P(really positive | predicted positive). Recall is P(predicted positive | really positive). Reporting one when you meant the other is just this mistake.
- <strong>Language nets</strong> predict P(next token | all so far). A conditional chance with a very large condition.

## Where this goes wrong

- <strong>Swapping the two sides.</strong> Covered above. And worth checking each single time you read a conditional claim in the wild.
- <strong>Multiplying without independence.</strong> P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) just holds when the events are free. Applied to correlated failures it sharply understates the chance of both happening.
- <strong>Confusing exclusive with free.</strong> They are close to opposites. Mutually exclusive events are maximally dependent: one occurring guarantees the other did not.
- <strong>Conditioning on something with chance zero.</strong> The formula divides by P(B). So it is undefined when B cannot happen. Shrink the world to nothing and there is nothing left to count.

## Worth remembering

Conditioning on B does not change the outcomes, it discards the ones where B did not happen. So P(A | B) is simply the count of outcomes in both events divided by the count in B. Independence is the special case where that shrinking leaves A's share unchanged. It is just when P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) is valid and not before. The two conditionals P(A|B) and P(B|A) are other counts and can differ enormously when one event is rare. Turning between them is what Bayes' theorem is for.

## Confounding, and why conditioning matters for causation

Conditional chances are also how you check whether an association is real or an artefact.

The classic illustration: ice cream sales and drowning incidents rise together. P(drowning | high ice cream sales) is genuinely more than P(drowning). But condition on temperature as well. And within any given temperature band the association disappears. Temperature was driving both.

That structure &mdash; a third var causing both &mdash; is a <strong>confounder</strong>. And it is why "controlling for" vars is the central move in observational stats.

<strong>Simpson's paradox</strong> is the extreme case: a trend that holds in each subgroup can reverse when the groups are pooled. A treatment can be better for mild cases and better for severe cases. And appear worse total, simply since it was given more often to severe cases. The lesson is not that stats lie. It is that the conditional and unconditional questions are genuinely other. And you must decide which one you are asking.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Does P(A|B) equal P(B|A)?</strong> Almost never. P(rain | clouds) is high. P(clouds | rain) is in effect 1. Confusing the two is the most common chance error there is.

<strong>What if P(B) is zero?</strong> The conditional chance is undefined. You cannot condition on something impossible.

<strong>How is this tied to Bayes' theorem?</strong> Bayes' theorem is a rearrangement of this definition. And it is what lets you turn P(B|A) into P(A|B).

<strong>Is machine learning just conditional chance?</strong> Supervised learning largely is: estimate P(target | features) from cases. Sorters estimate it right. Regressors estimate the conditional mean.

<strong>What does "conditionally free" mean?</strong> Free once you know a third var. Two symptoms may be correlated total but free given the disease. Which is just the assumption Naive Bayes makes.

<strong>Why do dependent features hurt Naive Bayes?</strong> Since it multiplies their chances as if each were fresh evidence. So correlated features count the same signal a few times and the output turns too sure.

## Recap in one screen

- P(A|B) restricts attention to the cases where B happened: P(A and B) / P(B).
- Conditioning is what turns a population rate into an individual prediction. Supervised learning is P(target | features).
- Independence means conditioning changes nothing. And it is what lets chances to multiply.
- The chain rule factorises a joint chance into conditionals. It is how language nets score sentences.
- Confounders and Simpson's paradox show that conditional and unconditional answers can differ. And still reverse.
