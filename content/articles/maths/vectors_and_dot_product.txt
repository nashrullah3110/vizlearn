title: Vectors and the Dot Product
intro: One multiplication-and-add that quietly powers every neural network on this site.

## The problem it solves

A <strong>vector</strong> is a list of counts that also has a geometric meaning: a way and a length. The <strong>dot product</strong> mixes two vectors into a single count that measures <em>how much they point the same way</em>.

The dot product is the single most key step in machine learning. Each forward pass through a neural network is a sequence of dot products between weight vectors and input vectors. Each similarity search in an embedding space computes dot products to rank results. Each linear regression prediction is a dot product between a coefficient vector and a feature vector.

Geometrically, the dot product of two vectors is positive when they point in roughly the same way, zero when they are perpendicular. And negative when they point in roughly opposite ways. The magnitude tells you how aligned they are: a large positive value means they are nearly parallel. And zero means they are orthogonal &mdash; fully unrelated in the way sense.

Cosine similarity, used throughout NLP and retrieval, is the dot product divided by the product of the two vectors' lengths. That division normalises away magnitude and leaves just way. So two vectors of very other lengths can still have a cosine similarity of 1 if they point the same way. That is why embedding similarity measures use cosine rather than the raw dot product: a document that mentions "machine learning" ten times should not be rated more close just since it is longer.

Vectors in machine learning are almost always high-dimensional. A word embedding might have 300 dimensions, and an pic embedding 2048. The dot product and cosine similarity work identically in these spaces &mdash; the geometry generalises cleanly, still though you cannot visualise it.

## The Sign Is the Whole Story

Since <span class="mono-font">cos&thinsp;&theta;</span> is positive below 90&deg;, zero at just 90&deg;. And negative above it, the dot product acts as an <strong>agreement meter</strong>:

- <strong style="color: var(--c-good)">Positive</strong> &mdash; the vectors broadly point the same way.
- <strong>Zero</strong> &mdash; they are perpendicular, or <em>orthogonal</em>. They share nothing.
- <strong style="color: var(--c-bad)">Negative</strong> &mdash; they point in opposing ways.

## Where You Have Now Met It

- <strong>Each neuron</strong> computes <span class="mono-font">w &middot; x + b</span> before its activation function.
- <strong>Attention</strong> in transformers scores tokens with <span class="mono-font">q &middot; k</span>, scaled by &radic;d.
- <strong>Cosine similarity</strong> is the dot product of two unit-length vectors &mdash; the angle alone, with magnitude divided out.
- <strong>Matrix multiplication</strong> is nothing but a grid of dot products. It is the next module.

## A vector is a list of counts with a way

In machine learning a vector is simply an ordered list of counts &mdash; and each count is a measurement of something.

<p class="mono-font">house = [120, 3, 2, 1995]&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(sq m, bedrooms, bathrooms, year)</p>

Geometrically, that list is an arrow from the origin to a point in four-dimensional space. Nobody can picture four dimensions. And it does not matter: each step is set arithmetically and behaves the same way in 2 dimensions as in 768.

Two steps are all you need to start:

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.array([3, 4])
b = np.array([1, 2])

a + b        # [4, 6]     - add corresponding entries
3 * a        # [9, 12]    - scale every entry
np.linalg.norm(a)   # 5.0 - the length, sqrt(3^2 + 4^2)
```

The length (or norm) is Pythagoras extended to any count of dimensions: square each entry, add them, take the square root.

## The dot product, and the two ways to read it

<p class="mono-font">a &middot; b = a&#8321;b&#8321. + a&#8322;b&#8322; + &hellip; + a&#8345;b&#8345;</p>

For [3, 4] and [1, 2]: 3&times;1 + 4&times;2 = 3 + 8 = <strong>11</strong>. Multiply matching entries, add them all up, get a single count.

The second reading is geometric, and it is where the meaning lives:

<p class="mono-font">a &middot; b = |a| |b| cos&theta;</p>

The dot product is the product of the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between them. That gives an immediate interpretation of its sign:

|Dot product|Angle|Meaning|
|---|---|---|
|Large positive|Near 0&deg;|Pointing the same way|
|Zero|Exactly 90&deg;|Perpendicular &mdash; no shared direction|
|Large negative|Near 180&deg;|Pointing opposite ways|

The zero case is the most useful fact in the whole topic: <strong>a dot product of zero means the vectors are at right angles</strong>. That single test underpins orthogonality, projections, PCA parts and the independence of basis ways.

## Where it appears in machine learning

- <strong>A neuron.</strong> `z = w · x + b` is a dot product between the weights and the inputs. All a network computes is built from this.
- <strong>Similarity.</strong> Cosine similarity is the dot product with the lengths divided out. It is how search, recommendation and retrieval systems check embeddings.
- <strong>Projection.</strong> How much of one vector lies along another is a dot product.
- <strong>Matrix multiplication.</strong> Each entry of a matrix product is a dot product between a row and a column.

The scale of it is worth noticing: a single forward pass of a large language net is billions of dot products. And nothing more exotic.

## Normalising, and why it matters

Dividing a vector by its length gives a <strong>unit vector</strong> &mdash; same way, length just 1.

```python
unit = a / np.linalg.norm(a)      # [0.6, 0.8], length 1
```

This matters since raw dot products mix two things: how aligned the vectors are. And how long they are. A long vector scores highly against all. Normalise first and the dot product measures just alignment &mdash; which is just cosine similarity.

That is why embedding libraries normalise vectors on the way in and then use plain dot products: on unit vectors the two are the same count. And a dot product is faster.

## Run the dot product

The sign is the whole story: positive means roughly the same way, zero means perpendicular, negative means opposing.

```python-run
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
b = np.array([4.0, 3.0])

dot = a @ b
print("a . b =", dot, "  (3*4 + 4*3)")
print("|a| =", np.linalg.norm(a), " |b| =", np.linalg.norm(b))

cos = dot / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
print("cos(angle) = %.4f -> %.1f degrees" % (cos, np.degrees(np.arccos(cos))))
print()
for label, v in [("same direction", np.array([6.0, 8.0])),
                 ("perpendicular ", np.array([-4.0, 3.0])),
                 ("opposite      ", np.array([-3.0, -4.0]))]:
    print("%s  a . v = %7.2f" % (label, a @ v))
print()
print("zero means perpendicular; the sign says which side.")
```

## Experiments to try

1. <strong>Drag b in a circle around a</strong> at constant length. The dot product traces a cosine wave: max when aligned, zero at right angles, most negative when opposed.
2. <strong>Load Parallel.</strong> The angle reads 0&deg; and the projection equals the full length of b &mdash; total agreement.
3. <strong>Double each part of b.</strong> The angle is unchanged but the dot product doubles &mdash; proof it measures magnitude <em>and</em> way. Cosine similarity removes that magnitude effect.
4. <strong>Set a to (0, 0).</strong> The dot product collapses to 0 and the angle turns undefined &mdash; a zero vector has no way to agree with.

## Worth remembering

Multiply matching parts and add. The result is positive when two vectors agree, zero when they are unrelated. And negative when they oppose &mdash; a single count capturing alignment. And the atom from which matrix multiplication, attention and similarity search are all built.

## Vectors in two, three and seven hundred dimensions

All above is set by arithmetic, not by pictures. It is why it survives the jump to high dimensions unchanged. A sentence embedding with 768 entries has a length, an angle to each other embedding. And a dot product with each of them, computed by just the formulas above.

What does change is intuition, and two effects are worth knowing.

<strong>Distances concentrate.</strong> In very high dimensions, the distance between the nearest pair of random points and the furthest pair turns proportionally close. "Nearest neighbour" gets less meaningful. It is why KNN degrades and why dimensionality reduction before distance-based ways is standard practice.

<strong>Random vectors are nearly orthogonal.</strong> Pick two random ways in 1000 dimensions and their dot product is almost certainly close to zero. There is simply an enormous amount of room. It is part of why high-dimensional embeddings can store so many distinguishable concepts.

## Other products, and what they are for

|Operation|Input|Output|Use|
|---|---|---|---|
|Dot product|Two vectors|One number|Similarity, projection, neurons|
|Elementwise product|Two vectors|A vector|Masking, gating in LSTMs|
|Cross product|Two 3D vectors|A vector at right angles to both|Geometry, graphics, physics|
|Outer product|Two vectors|A matrix|Building rank-1 updates, attention maths|

The cross product just exists in three dimensions (and seven, for exotic reasons). It is why it appears in graphics and almost never in machine learning.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Is a vector the same as an array?</strong> In code, effectively yes &mdash; a 1-D NumPy array. Mathematically a vector also carries the idea of way and magnitude.

<strong>Row vector or column vector?</strong> Maths convention is columns. NumPy's 1-D arrays are neither. And broadcast sensibly in both roles. It matters as soon as you write matrix products by hand.

<strong>What does a negative dot product mean?</strong> The vectors point in broadly opposite ways &mdash; more than 90&deg; apart.

<strong>Why normalise before checking?</strong> Otherwise long vectors score highly against all. And you are measuring magnitude rather than similarity.

<strong>Is the dot product the same as cosine similarity?</strong> Just for unit vectors. In general, cosine similarity is the dot product divided by both lengths.

<strong>How is this tied to matrix multiplication?</strong> Each entry in a matrix product is a dot product between a row of the first matrix and a column of the second.

## Recap in one screen

- A vector is an ordered list of counts. And geometrically an arrow with length and way.
- The dot product multiplies matching entries and sums them &mdash; one count out.
- Geometrically it is `|a||b|cos&theta;`: positive means aligned, zero means perpendicular, negative means opposed.
- Normalising to length 1 turns the dot product into pure similarity. It is cosine similarity.
- A neuron, a similarity search and each entry of a matrix product are all dot products.
