intro: Measuring how two things move together, and the three ways it misleads you.

## Quick Context

<a href="mean_variance_standard_deviation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Variance</a> describes how one var spreads out. Covariance is the same idea for two: when x is above its mean, is y often above its mean too?<p>Link is covariance with the units removed. That single change is what makes it comparable across datasets. And, as we will see, still leaves it blind to anything that is not a straight line.</p>

Covariance measures the way of the linear link: positive means both vars tend to increase together, negative means one raises while the other drops. And near zero means there is no linear trend. But the magnitude of covariance depends on the units. Heights in centimetres give covariances a hundred times more than heights in metres, still though the link is identical.

Link (Pearson's r) divides covariance by the product of the two standard deviations, giving a dimensionless count between &minus;1 and +1. r = 1 is a perfect positive line, r = &minus;1 is a perfect negative line. And r = 0 means no linear link. The normalisation is what makes link comparable across datasets with other scales.

The limitation is the word "linear". Two vars with a perfect parabolic link have r = 0, since the positive half and the negative half cancel just. A link of zero does not mean the vars are unrelated. It means they are not tied by a straight line. Before reporting a link, always plot the data. Anscombe's quartet is the classic demonstration: four datasets with identical correlations and wildly other shapes.

In machine learning, the covariance grid of a dataset is the foundation of PCA. Each off-diagonal entry is the covariance between a pair of features. The diagonal entries are the variances. Diagonalising this grid (finding its eigenvectors) is just what PCA does. And the eigenvalues tell you how much variance each principal part gets.

## Covariance, one point at a time

cov(x, y) = (1/n) &Sigma; (x&#7522; &minus; x&#772;)(y&#7522; &minus; y&#772;)

For each point, take how far x is from its mean, multiply by how far y is from its mean, and average. The sign of each product is all that matters:

- Both above their means. Or both below &rarr; the product is <strong>positive</strong>.
- One above, one below &rarr; the product is <strong>negative</strong>.

The scatter above colours points by just this. Green points are pushing covariance up. Orange are pulling it down. A cloud that leans up-and-right has mostly green. One that leans down-and-right has mostly orange. A shapeless cloud has both in equal measure and they cancel.

## Why link exists

Covariance has a fatal flaw for reporting: it carries the units of both vars multiplied together. Measure height in metres and weight in kilograms and you get one count. Switch height to centimetres and the same data gives a count a hundred times more. Nothing about the link changed.

Dividing by both standard deviations cancels the units out:

r = cov(x, y) / ( &sigma;&#8339; &sigma;&#7527; )

The result is always between &minus;1 and 1, whatever the data is measured in. That is the whole gap between the two quantities: link is standardised covariance.

## Do two vars move together?

Covariance measures whether two vars tend to rise and fall together.

<p class="mono-font">cov(X, Y) = &Sigma;(x &minus; x&#772;)(y &minus; y&#772;) / (n &minus; 1)</p>

The logic is in the product. When both vars are above their means, the product is positive. When both are below, negative times negative is positive again. When one is above and the other below, the product is negative. Add them up and the sign tells you the way of the link.

The problem is the size. Covariance is in the product of the two units. "pound-square-metres" for price against floor area. So the count it is uninterpretable and changes if you switch from metres to centimetres.

Link fixes that by dividing out both standard deviations:

<p class="mono-font">r = cov(X, Y) / (&sigma;&#8339; &sigma;&#8342;)</p>

The result is always between &minus;1 and 1, unitless. And comparable across any pair of vars.

|r|Meaning|
|---|---|
|1.0|Perfect straight-line increase|
|0.7|Strong positive|
|0.3|Weak positive|
|0|No <em>linear</em> relationship|
|&minus;0.7|Strong negative|
|&minus;1.0|Perfect straight-line decrease|

## Change the units, watch one move

The same two vars, with height measured in metres and then in centimetres. One count survives the change and one does not.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
height_m = rng.normal(1.7, 0.1, 500)          # metres
weight_kg = 40 * height_m + rng.normal(0, 5, 500)

for label, h in [("height in metres", height_m),
                 ("height in cm", height_m * 100)]:
    c = np.cov(h, weight_kg)[0, 1]
    r = np.corrcoef(h, weight_kg)[0, 1]
    print("%-18s covariance %10.3f   correlation %6.3f" % (label, c, r))
print()
print("the relationship did not change. covariance is in units of")
print("height times weight, so it moves with the units; correlation")
print("divides them out and does not.")
```

## What link cannot see

Two limitations matter more than any other. And both cause real mistakes.

<strong>It just measures straight-line links.</strong> Take y = x&sup2; over the range &minus;5 to 5. The link is perfect and deterministic. And the link is <strong>0</strong>, since the rising half cancels the falling half. Anscombe's quartet makes the same point: four datasets with identical correlations and utterly other shapes. Always plot the scatter before trusting an r.

<strong>Link is not causation.</strong> Three explanations give the same r: X causes Y, Y causes X. Or a third var causes both. Ice cream sales correlate with drownings since both follow temperature. Establishing causation needs an intervention. A randomised test &mdash; or careful causal modelling. No amount of link supplies it.

A third, quieter limitation: link is sensitive to outliers. A single extreme point can create an r of 0.8 in data that is otherwise a shapeless cloud. Or destroy a genuine link. Spearman's rank link. It correlates the ranks rather than the vals, is the reliable option and also detects monotonic non-linear links.

## Link matrices, and multicollinearity

With a few features, the link grid shows each pair at once. And it is one of the first things worth plotting on a new dataset.

```python
import pandas as pd
corr = df.corr(numeric_only=True)          # Pearson by default
corr = df.corr(method="spearman")          # rank-based, reliable
```

Two things to look for. High link between a feature and the <em>target</em> is promising. High link between two <em>features</em> is a warning: multicollinearity makes linear net coefficients unstable and uninterpretable, flipping sign when a row is added, still though the predictions stay fine.

The replies are to drop one of the pair, mix them, use PCA. Or move to ridge regression. It handles correlated features gracefully by shrinking them together.

Tree-based nets are largely unaffected in their predictions. But their feature importances get split arbitrarily between correlated columns, making both look less key than either really is.

2. <strong>Sweep the strength.</strong> Drag the <strong>Target Link</strong> slider from &minus;1 to 1 and watch the cloud tilt from down-right, through shapeless, to up-right. At &plusmn;1 the points collapse onto a single line.
3. <strong>Meet the big failure.</strong> Set the <strong>Pattern</strong> to U-shaped curve. Each point sits on a clean parabola. Y is fully found by x &mdash; yet r sits near zero. Link measures <em>straight-line</em> association just. And a symmetric curve has none.
4. <strong>Watch one point take over.</strong> Set the <strong>Pattern</strong> to One extreme outlier. A single far-off point drags r away from what the bulk of the data is doing. Link is not reliable.
5. <strong>See small samples lie.</strong> Set the <strong>Pattern</strong> to No link and the <strong>Sample Size</strong> slider to 10, then press <strong>Resample</strong> a few times. r bounces around, sometimes reaching 0.5 or beyond, purely by chance. Now set the sample size to 400 and resample again. It stays near zero.
6. <strong>Read the ellipse.</strong> With Show Spread Ellipse on, notice that its long axis tilts with the link and its axes stay perpendicular. Those axes are the eigenvectors of the covariance grid. It is precisely what PCA finds.

## Three ways it misleads

- <strong>Link is not causation.</strong> The oldest warning in stats and still the most ignored. Ice cream sales correlate with drownings. The summer heat causes both. A third var driving two others is called confounding and gives textbook-perfect correlations.
- <strong>Link is not dependence.</strong> The U-shaped preset shows a perfectly deterministic link with r near zero. "Uncorrelated" and "free" mean the same thing just for often distributed data. In general, independence implies zero link. But never the reverse.
- <strong>A single count hides the shape.</strong> Anscombe's quartet is four datasets with identical means, variances and correlations that look nothing alike when plotted. Always plot the scatter.

## Where it matters in machine learning

- <strong>PCA</strong> is the eigendecomposition of the covariance grid. The ellipse you can see above is the object it operates on.
- <strong>Multicollinearity</strong> &mdash; highly correlated features make <a href="../machine_learning/linear_regression_with_ols.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">linear regression</a> coefficients unstable and hard to interpret. It is one of the problems <a href="../machine_learning/ridge_and_lasso_regression.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Ridge</a> was designed to relieve.
- <strong>Feature choice</strong> by link with the target will silently discard any feature whose link is non-linear. It is just the failure the U-shaped preset shows.
- <strong>r&sup2;</strong> is the fraction of variance in one var explained by a straight-line fit on the other. The same quantity linear regression reports.

## Traps worth knowing

- <strong>Checking covariances across datasets.</strong> They are in other units and are not comparable. Check correlations instead.
- <strong>Trusting r on a small sample.</strong> With ten points a link of 0.5 is unremarkable noise. Report a confidence interval or a sample size alongside it.
- <strong>With Pearson's r on ranked or heavily skewed data.</strong> Spearman's rank link handles monotonic-but-not-linear links and is far less sensitive to outliers.
- <strong>Reading r = 0 as "no link".</strong> It means no <em>linear</em> link. Plot it before concluding anything.

## Where that leaves you

Covariance averages the product of each var's deviation from its own mean. So it is positive when two vars move together and negative when they move oppositely. But it carries the units of both. It makes its magnitude meaningless on its own. Link divides by both standard deviations to strip the units out, giving a count always between &minus;1 and 1 that is unchanged by rescaling. Both measure straight-line association just: a perfect U-shaped link gives a link near zero, a single outlier can dominate the result. And small samples give large correlations by chance. So the scatter plot is never optional.

## The covariance grid

For a few vars, covariances are collected into a square grid: variances on the diagonal, covariances off it. It is symmetric, since cov(X, Y) = cov(Y, X).

This object is more key than it first appears:

- <strong>PCA</strong> is the eigendecomposition of the covariance grid. Its eigenvectors are the principal parts and its eigenvalues are the variances along them.
- <strong>A multivariate normal spread</strong> is set by a mean vec and a covariance grid. And the grid finds the shape and orientation of the resulting ellipse.
- <strong>Mahalanobis distance</strong> uses its inverse to measure distance in units of the data's own spread. It is why it detects outliers that per-feature thresholds miss.
- <strong>Portfolio theory</strong> uses it to find the variance of a weighted combination. It is how diversification is quantified.

Standardising the data first turns the covariance grid into the link grid. The same information, expressed in comparable units. That is just why PCA is often run on standardised data.

## Choosing the right coefficient

|Coefficient|Detects|Use when|
|---|---|---|
|Pearson|Linear relationships|Continuous, roughly normal, no big outliers|
|Spearman|Any monotonic relationship|Skewed data, outliers, or ordinal variables|
|Kendall's tau|Monotonic, based on concordant pairs|Small samples, many ties|
|Mutual information|Any dependence at all|Non-monotonic relationships|
|Cram&eacute;r's V|Association between categorical variables|Two categorical columns|

A real routine on a new dataset: find Pearson and Spearman side by side. A large gap between them is a signal. Either the link is non-linear. Or outliers are distorting the Pearson value. Either way it points you at a scatter plot worth looking at.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What does r = 0 mean?</strong> No <em>linear</em> link. There may be a perfect non-linear one.

<strong>Is a link of 0.5 strong?</strong> It depends fully on the field. In physics it would be weak. In behavioural research it is large. Interpret against the domain, not a fixed table.

<strong>How is r tied to R&sup2;?</strong> For simple linear regression with one predictor, R&sup2; is just r squared. So r = 0.7 means about half the variance is explained.

<strong>Can link be used with categorical data?</strong> Not Pearson. Use Cram&eacute;r's V for two categoricals. Or the point-biserial link for one binary and one continuous.

<strong>Why does covariance have such odd units?</strong> Since it is a product of two deviations. That is the whole reason link exists.

<strong>Does a high link with the target mean the feature is useful?</strong> It is a good sign. But a feature that is uncorrelated alone can be very useful in combination with others. And a highly correlated feature can be a leak from the future. Check both.

## Recap in one screen

- Covariance gives the way of a linear link, in uninterpretable units.
- Link divides by both standard deviations, giving a unitless count from &minus;1 to 1.
- r = 0 means no <em>linear</em> link, not no link. Always look at the scatter plot.
- Link never establishes causation. A confounder gives the same count.
- Spearman is the reliable, monotonic option. Mutual information catches any dependence.
- The covariance grid is what PCA decomposes and what sets a multivariate normal.
