title: The Determinant: A Practical Guide
intro: An area factor with a sign, and everything that follows from it being zero.

## Quick Context

A grid is <a href="matrix_as_transformation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">a change</a> of space. And the determinant measures one thing about it: how much it scales area. A determinant of 3 means each region comes out three times as large. 0.5 means all shrinks by half. 1 means area is untouched, however much the shape has been rotated or sheared.

The sign is the second half of the story. A negative determinant means the plane was flipped over. A reflection is hiding inside the change. A determinant of zero is the critical case: the change squashes space into a less dimension. It means the columns are linearly dependent and the grid has no inverse. That single test &mdash; is the determinant zero?. Answers whether a linear system has a unique solution, whether a change is reversible. And whether a set of vecs spans the full space. For a 2&times;2 grid the formula is one line: ad &minus; bc. For 3&times;3 it expands along a row into three 2&times;2 determinants (cofactor expansion). And the pattern transfers but the cost grows factorially. Which is why real implementations use row reduction (Gaussian elimination) rather than the formula. NumPy's `np.linalg.det` runs in O(n&sup3;) via LU breakdown no matter size. This page finds the determinant of a few matrices, animates the parallelogram whose area it measures. And covers the row-reduction way that avoids the factorial blowup.

## Reading it off the grid

det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad &minus; bc

The columns of the grid are where the basis vecs land: <span class="mono-font">i</span> goes to (a, c) and <span class="mono-font">j</span> goes to (b, d). The unit square they used to span turns the parallelogram those two vecs span. And <span class="mono-font">ad &minus; bc</span> is just its signed area. That is not a coincidence to memorise. It is the formula for the area of a parallelogram written in coordinates.

In three dimensions the same count is the volume of the pic of the unit cube. And in n dimensions it is the n-dim volume. The mechanics get heavier. The meaning does not change.

## What zero means

If the determinant is zero, the parallelogram has no area: the two columns lie on the same line. And the whole plane has been squashed onto that line. All that goes wrong with a singular grid follows from this one picture.

- <strong>No inverse.</strong> Undoing the change would mean recovering a 2D plane from a 1D line. And that information is gone. This is why <span class="mono-font">det = 0</span> and "not invertible" are the same statement.
- <strong>Rank drops.</strong> The columns are linearly dependent. So the rank falls from 2 to 1 (or to 0 if the grid is all zeros).
- <strong>Systems break.</strong> <span class="mono-font">Ax = b</span> has either no solution or infinitely many, never just one.
- <strong>An eigenvalue is zero.</strong> The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues. So a zero determinant means at least one of them is zero. The way that got flattened.

Near-zero is its own problem. A determinant of 0.001 is technically invertible and numerically miserable: the grid is close to singular. And solving with it amplifies error. Real code checks the condition count rather than testing the determinant against zero.

## One count that says what a change does to size

The determinant of a grid answers a single question: <strong>by what factor does this change scale areas?</strong> (Volumes in three dimensions, and the transfer of volume beyond that.)

For a 2&times;2 grid:

<p class="mono-font">det [[a, b], [c, d]] = ad &minus; bc</p>

Take the unit square with corners (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1) &mdash; area 1. Apply the grid and it turns a parallelogram whose area is just |det|.

|det|Effect|
|---|---|
|2|Areas double|
|1|Areas preserved &mdash; rotations and reflections|
|0.5|Areas halve|
|0|Everything collapses onto a line or a point|
|&minus;3|Areas triple <em>and</em> orientation flips|

The negative case is worth a sentence: the sign records whether the change turned space inside out. A reflection has a negative determinant. A rotation does not.

## Zero is the case that matters

A determinant of zero means the change flattened the space. Two dimensions became one. A volume became a plane.

All else follows from that geometric fact:

- The grid is <strong>singular</strong>. It has no inverse, since many inputs now map to the same output and there is no way back.
- Its columns are <strong>linearly dependent</strong>. At least one is a combination of the others.
- The grid is not <strong>full rank</strong>.
- At least one <strong>eigenvalue is zero</strong>.
- The system `Ax = b` has either no solution or infinitely many, never just one.

Those are five ways of saying the same thing. And recognising that is most of what a linear algebra course is trying to teach.

In practice, exact zeros are rare in floating point. What you meet is a determinant very close to zero, meaning a nearly singular grid. Solutions then turn very sensitive to small changes in the data. Which is just what multicollinearity does to regression coefficients, making them large, unstable. And prone to flipping sign when a row is added.

## Finding it, and why you often should not

For 3&times;3 there is the cofactor expansion. And for more matrices it transfers. At a cost that grows factorially. Nobody uses it beyond textbook cases.

Real implementations use LU breakdown, which is cubic rather than factorial. And for the questions people really want answered, there is often a better tool:

```python
import numpy as np

np.linalg.det(A)              # the determinant itself
np.linalg.matrix_rank(A)      # better test of singularity
np.linalg.cond(A)             # how close to singular, numerically
np.linalg.slogdet(A)          # sign and log|det| - avoids overflow
```

`slogdet` matters more than it sounds. Determinants of large matrices overflow or underflow easily. And log-determinants appear right in the log-likelihood of a multivariate normal. So stat-based code uses the log version throughout.

Checking `det(A) == 0` is bad practice. Use `matrix_rank`, or check the condition count. It tells you how nearly singular the grid is rather than giving a yes/no answer that floating point cannot support.

## Three determinants

A stretch, a reflection. And a grid that flattens the plane onto a line. And what asking for the last one's inverse gets you.

```python-run
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[3.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
print("det =", round(np.linalg.det(A), 6))
print("the unit square's area is multiplied by that")
print()

flip = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 0.0]])
print("a reflection has a negative determinant:", np.linalg.det(flip))
print()

# Second row is twice the first, so the transform flattens the plane
# onto a line and nothing can undo it.
singular = np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 4.0]])
print("det of a flattening matrix:", round(np.linalg.det(singular), 12))
print("rank:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(singular), "- one direction survives, not two")
print()
print("asking for its inverse gives nothing usable:")
print(np.linalg.inv(singular))
```

## Guided tour

1. <strong>Start at det = 2.</strong> The default grid doubles area: the unit square turns a parallelogram of area 2.00. And the readout is just (2.0 &times; 1.5) &minus; (1.0 &times; 1.0).
2. <strong>Press Sweep d Through Zero.</strong> Entry d slides down and the parallelogram flattens. At d = 0.5 the determinant is just 0 and the shape collapses to a line. Past it the parallelogram reopens on the other side and the badge reads flipped.
3. <strong>Look at what flipped means.</strong> With a negative determinant, going from the pic of i to the pic of j turns the other way round the parallelogram. The plane has been turned over. No amount of rotating gets it back.
4. <strong>Find the identity.</strong> Set a = 1, b = 0, c = 0, d = 1. Determinant 1, area unchanged, nothing moved at all.
5. <strong>Shear it.</strong> From the identity, drag b up to 2. The square leans into a parallelogram but the determinant stays at just 1. A shear slides area sideways without creating or destroying any of it.
6. <strong>Scale one axis.</strong> Set a = 3 with b = 0, c = 0, d = 1: determinant 3, the square stretched into a 3&times;1 rectangle. The determinant does not care which way the stretching happened in.

## Properties worth knowing

|Property|Statement|
|---|---|
|Product|det(AB) = det(A) &times; det(B)|
|Transpose|det(Aᵀ) = det(A)|
|Inverse|det(A&#8315;&sup1;) = 1 / det(A)|
|Scaling|det(kA) = k&#8319; det(A) for an n&times;n matrix|
|Triangular|The product of the diagonal entries|
|Eigenvalues|The product of all eigenvalues|
|Identity|det(I) = 1|

The product rule has an clear reading: apply one change that doubles areas and another that triples them. And areas are six times more. Composition multiplies scale factors.

The eigenvalue rule connects this topic to the last one: if any eigenvalue is zero, the product is zero. So the grid is singular. And the scaling rule surprises people. Doubling each entry of a 3&times;3 grid multiplies its determinant by 8, not 2, since all three dimensions are scaled.

## Worth remembering

The determinant is the signed area factor of a change: <span class="mono-font">ad &minus; bc</span> is the area of the parallelogram the unit square turns, its magnitude says how much each region is scaled. And its sign says whether the plane was flipped over. Zero is the case that matters. The square has been flattened onto a line. So the columns are dependent, the rank has dropped, the inverse does not exist and Ax = b no longer has a unique solution. Determinants multiply under composition and invert under inversion. And in practice "close to zero" deserves as much suspicion as zero it.

## Where determinants appear in practice

<strong>Multivariate normal spreads.</strong> The density includes `1/√(det Σ)`. There Σ is the covariance grid. The determinant measures the total spread of the spread. A "generalised variance" &mdash; and a near-zero value means the data lies on a less-dim surface, at which point the density is undefined and the net needs regularising.

<strong>Change of vars.</strong> Transforming a chance spread needs multiplying by the full determinant of the Jacobian. It corrects for how the change stretches space. Scaling flows &mdash; a family of generative nets. Are designed fully around making that determinant cheap to find.

<strong>Detecting collinearity.</strong> A near-zero determinant of `XᵀX` warns that features are nearly redundant, before the regression coefficients start behaving strangely.

<strong>Geometry.</strong> The area of a triangle, the volume of a parallelepiped. And orientation tests in computational geometry are all determinants.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Can non-square matrices have a determinant?</strong> No. The concept is about scaling a space to it. It needs equal input and output dimensions.

<strong>What does a determinant of 1 mean?</strong> Areas and volumes are preserved. Rotations and shears are the typical cases.

<strong>Is a large determinant good?</strong> It carries no quality judgement. It just means the change expands space a lot. What matters practically is being far from zero.

<strong>How do I test for singularity in code?</strong> `np.linalg.matrix_rank(A) < min(A.shape)`. Or check `np.linalg.cond(A)` against a threshold. Do not check the determinant to zero.

<strong>Why is det(AB) = det(A)det(B)?</strong> Since scale factors compose multiplicatively when changes are applied in sequence.

<strong>Where does the log-determinant come from?</strong> From log-likelihoods of Gaussian nets. There taking logs turns the product into a sum and avoids overflow.

## Recap in one screen

- The determinant is the factor by which a change scales area or volume.
- A negative determinant means orientation was flipped. Zero means a dimension was collapsed.
- Zero determinant = singular = no inverse = dependent columns = a zero eigenvalue.
- Near-zero is the real case: unstable solutions and unreliable coefficients.
- Use rank or the condition count to test singularity. And `slogdet` when the value it is needed.
