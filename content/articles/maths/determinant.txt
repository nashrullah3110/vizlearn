title: The Determinant
intro: For a square matrix, the determinant records signed area or volume scaling and whether the transformation is reversible.

## Read the determinant geometrically

A 2 × 2 matrix maps the unit square to a parallelogram. The absolute determinant is the area scale factor; its sign records whether orientation is preserved or flipped. In three dimensions, the absolute determinant scales volume. A determinant of zero means the transformation collapses at least one dimension.

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="determinant of a b c d equals a d minus b c">det([[a,b],[c,d]]) = ad − bc</div>

For A = [[2, 1], [1, 1]], det(A) = 2·1 − 1·1 = 1, so areas are preserved, although the shape can still shear. For [[1, 2], [2, 4]], the determinant is zero: its columns are parallel and the plane collapses to a line.

## What it tells you—and what it does not

A square matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero. But determinant magnitude is not a general measure of numerical stability: a matrix can have a nonzero determinant and still be ill-conditioned. For computations, use a condition number or a suitable factorisation instead of relying only on det(A). See [rank](rank_and_linear_independence.html), [matrix transformations](matrix_as_transformation.html), and [QR decomposition](qr_decomposition.html).
