title: Maximum Likelihood: A Practical Guide
intro: The principle that produces almost every loss function you have already been using.

## Quick Context

You have some data and a family of distributions that might have produced it. Which member of the family should you pick?

Max likelihood gives one answer. And it is almost plain: <strong>pick the spread that makes the data you really saw as likely as it can be</strong>. Nothing more to it than that.

It is worth learning well since it is not one trick among many. Mean squared error, log loss and cross-entropy are all max likelihood in disguise. Each comes from a other guess about the noise. Assume the errors are Gaussian and MLE gives you least squares. Assume the labels are Bernoulli and MLE gives you log loss. Assume they are Categorical and MLE gives you cross-entropy. In each case the loss people use by habit is the minus log-likelihood. That makes shrinking the loss the same as raising the likelihood. The log turns a product of chances (one per data point) into a sum. It is stable and easy to derive. The steps: write L(&theta;) as the product of P(x_i | &theta;) over all points, take the log, derive with respect to &theta;, set to zero, and solve. For a Gaussian with unknown mean, the MLE is the sample mean. For unknown spread, it is the sample spread (biased, with n not n-1). This page derives the MLE for a Gaussian step by step, shows the likelihood surface. And covers the tie between MLE and the loss functions you now know.

## Likelihood is not probability

The two words get used interchangeably in conversation and they are not the same thing.

- <strong>Chance</strong> fixes the spread and asks about the data. "Given this coin is fair, how likely is 7 heads in 10?"
- <strong>Likelihood</strong> fixes the data and asks about the spread. "Given I saw 7 heads in 10, how well does 'fair' explain it?"

The data never moves in this module &mdash; the points stay just where they are. What moves is the trial curve. And the likelihood is a claim about the curve, not about the points.

## The math

For each seen point, read off the density of the trial spread at that point. Then times them all:

L(&mu;, &sigma;) = p(x&#8321;) &times; p(x&#8322;) &times; &hellip; &times; p(x&#8345;)

The stems in the top panel are the densities. A trial curve placed over the bulk of the data has tall stems all over. One shifted away has some tall and many near zero. And one near-zero factor drags the whole product down.

In practice nobody computes that product. Times a few hundred counts below 1 underflows to just zero in floating point. Taking the log turns the product into a sum:

log L = log p(x&#8321;) + log p(x&#8322;) + &hellip; + log p(x&#8345;)

The log is monotonic, so what boosts the sum also boosts the product. Nothing is lost. And the math turns stable.

## Which params make the data least surprising?

Max likelihood turns "fit a net" into a clear question: <strong>of all the param vals I could choose, which makes the data I really saw most likely?</strong>

Flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. For any bias p, the chance of that outcome is:

<p class="mono-font">L(p) = C(10,7) &times; p&#8311; &times; (1&minus;p)&sup3;</p>

Test it at a few vals:

|p|Likelihood|
|---|---|
|0.3|0.009|
|0.5|0.117|
|0.7|<strong>0.267</strong>|
|0.9|0.057|

The max is at p = 0.7, which is just the seen share. That is not a fluke &mdash; for a binomial, the MLE is always the sample share. And it can be derived in three lines of calculus.

Note the shift. A chance treats the params as fixed and asks about the data. A <em>likelihood</em> treats the data as fixed and asks about the params. Same formula, read the other way.

## Logs, and why the estimate is always found that way

The likelihood of n free data points is a product of n terms, each below 1. For n = 1,000 that underflows to just zero in floating point. The math is lost before it starts.

Taking logs turns the product into a sum:

<p class="mono-font">log L(&theta;) = &Sigma; log P(x&#7522; | &theta;)</p>

Since log is monotonic, what boosts the log-likelihood also boosts the likelihood &mdash; so nothing is lost. And all turns stable and easy to derive.

By habit optimisers cut, so what is really cut is the <strong>negative log-likelihood</strong>. That phrase, and the short form NLL, show up all through machine learning for just this reason.

## Why your loss function is what it is

This is the payoff, and it is a truly unifying idea: <strong>the usual loss functions are minus log-likelihoods under other guesses about the noise</strong>.

|Assumption about the errors|Maximum likelihood gives you|
|---|---|
|Normal|Mean squared error|
|Laplace|Mean absolute error|
|Bernoulli outcome|Binary cross-entropy|
|Categorical outcome|Categorical cross-entropy|
|Poisson counts|Poisson loss|

So choosing MSE is not a neutral default &mdash; it is a guess that errors are symmetric, bell-shaped and steady in spread. When that is wrong (heavy tails, skew, counts), the loss fights the data. Switching to the likelihood that matches the process often beats any amount of net tuning.

The same framework explains priors. Adding a prior over the params and raising the posterior instead of the likelihood &mdash; MAP &mdash; gives ridge from a normal prior and lasso from a Laplace prior. The cost term is the log of the prior.

## Exploration guide

1. <strong>Search by hand.</strong> Drag the <strong>Guessed Mean</strong> slider slowly across its range and watch the log-likelihood readout rise to a peak and fall away. There is just one best answer and you can feel where it is.
2. <strong>Check the punchline.</strong> Click <strong>Jump to the MLE</strong> and check Your Guess against Sample Mean. They are the same count. The max-likelihood estimate of a normal mean <em>is</em> the sample mean &mdash; the formula you now knew, derived rather than assumed.
3. <strong>Watch it climb.</strong> Click <strong>Climb to the Max</strong> and watch the curve slide into place while log-likelihood rises. This is gradient ascent on the likelihood. It is just what fitting a net does.
4. <strong>Get the spread wrong.</strong> Set the <strong>Guessed Spread</strong> slider to 6. The curve flattens, each stem shortens. And log-likelihood falls &mdash; a distribution too vague to commit to anything explains the data badly. Now set it to 0.3: the curve is so narrow that points still slightly off-centre get almost no density. And it falls again. The best spread is in between.
5. <strong>Break the arithmetic.</strong> Set the <strong>Sample Size</strong> slider to its max and watch Raw Likelihood. It collapses toward zero and Underflowed flips to yes, while the log-likelihood carries on working perfectly. This is not a rounding detail &mdash; it is the whole reason each build works in log space.
6. <strong>See small samples mislead.</strong> Set the <strong>Sample Size</strong> slider to 2 and press <strong>New Data</strong> a few times. The MLE jumps around wildly. Max likelihood is not magic. With little data it happily returns a confident, wrong answer.

## Why your loss function looks the way it does

This is the part worth carrying away. Take max likelihood and change just the assumed noise:

- <strong>Assume normal noise</strong> around the prediction. And maximising log-likelihood turns out to be identical to minimising the <strong>sum of squared errors</strong>. Squaring in <a href="../machine_learning/linear_regression_with_ols.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">least squares</a> is not an arbitrary choice of cost &mdash; it falls out of the exponent in the normal density.
- <strong>Assume a Bernoulli outcome</strong>. And the same principle gives <strong>log loss</strong>. It is what <a href="../machine_learning/logistic_regression.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">logistic regression</a> minimises.
- <strong>Assume a categorical outcome</strong>. And you get <a href="../deep_learning/softmax_and_cross_entropy.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">cross-entropy</a>, the loss of in effect each classifier.

So "minimising the loss" and "maximising the likelihood" are often the same sentence. Negative log-likelihood is the loss. The minus sign is there just since optimisers are written to go downhill.

## Where it goes wrong

- <strong>It overfits happily.</strong> Max likelihood picks whatever explains the observed data best, with no preference for simplicity. Regularisation is what you add to stop it. And it corresponds to putting a prior on the params.
- <strong>The MLE of variance is biased.</strong> It divides by n, while the unbiased estimator divides by n&minus;1. Max likelihood is not guaranteed to be unbiased, just to maximise likelihood.
- <strong>The net family matters more than the fit.</strong> Fitting a normal to data that is not remotely normal gives you the best possible normal. And it is still the wrong answer. The way chooses within a family, never between families.
- <strong>Likelihoods are not probabilities of the params.</strong> "The likelihood of &mu; = 3 is high" does not mean &mu; = 3 is probable. Turning one into the other needs a prior. It is what Bayesian inference does with <a href="bayes_theorem.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Bayes' theorem</a>.

## Search for the param that explains the data

Score each candidate param by how likely it makes the data you really saw, then check the winner against the closed-form answer.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

TRUE_P = 0.65
flips = (rng.random(2000) < TRUE_P).astype(int)
heads, n = flips.sum(), len(flips)
print("observed %d heads in %d flips" % (heads, n))
print()

def log_likelihood(p, heads=heads, n=n):
    return heads * np.log(p) + (n - heads) * np.log(1 - p)

print("%8s %18s" % ("p", "log-likelihood"))
for p in (0.30, 0.50, 0.60, 0.65, 0.70, 0.80):
    print("%8.2f %18.4f" % (p, log_likelihood(p)))
print("        every other p makes the data we saw less likely.")
print()

grid = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 20_000)
best = grid[np.argmax([log_likelihood(p) for p in grid])]
print("grid search best p    : %.4f" % best)
print("closed form (heads/n) : %.4f" % (heads / n))
print("true value            : %.4f" % TRUE_P)
print()
print("the closed form is not a shortcut for this case -- it is what you get")
print("by setting the derivative of the log-likelihood to zero.")
```

As sample size grows, the estimate converges on the true value.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
TRUE_P = 0.65
flips = (rng.random(2000) < TRUE_P).astype(int)
heads, n = flips.sum(), len(flips)

def log_likelihood(p, heads=heads, n=n):
    return heads * np.log(p) + (n - heads) * np.log(1 - p)

print("the estimate tightens with more data:")
for size in (20, 100, 1000, 10_000):
    ests = [(rng.random(size) < TRUE_P).mean() for _ in range(2000)]
    print("  %6d flips: estimate %.4f +/- %.4f" % (size, np.mean(ests), np.std(ests)))
print()
print("same idea for a normal distribution:")
data = rng.normal(50.0, 8.0, 500)
print("  MLE mean %8.4f   vs sample mean %8.4f" % (data.mean(), data.mean()))
print("  MLE sd   %8.4f   vs unbiased sd %8.4f"
      % (data.std(ddof=0), data.std(ddof=1)))
print("  the MLE for sd divides by n, not n-1, so it is biased slightly low.")
print()
grid = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 20_000)
best = grid[np.argmax([log_likelihood(p) for p in grid])]
print("and why the log at all: the raw likelihood of %d flips is" % n)
print("  %.3e -- indistinguishable from zero on any hardware."
      % np.exp(log_likelihood(best)))
```

## Worth remembering

Max likelihood picks the spread that makes the seen data least surprising. It holds the data fixed and varies the params &mdash; the reverse of a chance question. The likelihood is the product of each point's density. And since that product underflows to zero on any real sample, it is always done as a sum of logs instead. That is safe since the log is monotonic. For a normal the answer comes out as just the sample mean and sample spread. And changing just the assumed noise turns the same idea into squared error, log loss or cross-entropy &mdash; which is why shrinking a loss and raising a likelihood are often the same thing.

## Properties, and the one caveat

Max likelihood has a few strong traits. It is why the way is the default across stats:

- <strong>Consistent.</strong> With enough data, the guess locks on to the true param val.
- <strong>Fast in large samples.</strong> No other consistent rule has less spread in the large-sample limit.
- <strong>Invariant.</strong> If &theta;-hat is the MLE of &theta;, then g(&theta;-hat) is the MLE of g(&theta;) &mdash; so you can work on any scale.

The caveat is that they are not always <strong>unbiased</strong> in small samples. The classic case is spread: the MLE divides by n and tends to understate the true spread. It is why the sample spread divides by n&minus;1 instead. That fix is a wilful break from the MLE.

The other real caveat is overfitting. Raising likelihood alone will happily choose params that explain the training data well and fail on new data &mdash; a mixture net can put a part on a single point with tiny spread and drive the likelihood to infinity. Priors and held-out checks are the answers.

## Finding the max in practice

Some nets have a closed form: the mean for a normal, the share for a binomial, the sample mean for a Poisson rate. Most do not. And the max is found by search.

```python
from scipy.optimize import minimize
import numpy as np

def neg_log_likelihood(params, data):
    mu, log_sigma = params                 # optimise log sigma to keep it positive
    sigma = np.exp(log_sigma)
    return -np.sum(-0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * sigma**2)
                   - (data - mu)**2 / (2 * sigma**2))

result = minimize(neg_log_likelihood, x0=[0.0, 0.0], args=(data,))
```

Two habits from that snippet carry over. Work with free forms of bound params &mdash; log of a std dev, logit of a chance &mdash; so the solver cannot wander into bad vals. And write the minus log-likelihood, since each solver goes downhill.

Training a neural net is the same at scale: the loss is a minus log-likelihood. And gradient descent is the solver.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is the gap between chance and likelihood?</strong> Chance fixes the params and asks about data. Likelihood fixes the data and asks about params. The formula is the same; what varies is other.

<strong>Why use the log instead of the raw likelihood?</strong> Products of many small counts underflow. Sums are easier to derive. The max is in the same place.

<strong>Is MLE the same as least squares?</strong> Least squares <em>is</em> the MLE when the errors are assumed normal with constant variance. Change the noise assumption and you get a other loss.

<strong>How does this tie to Bayesian methods?</strong> MLE uses just the data. MAP adds a prior and raises the posterior. Full Bayesian work keeps the whole posterior spread rather than one point.

<strong>Can likelihood exceed 1?</strong> For smooth data, yes &mdash; likelihoods are densities, not chances. Just the place of the max matters.

<strong>What if the likelihood has a few peaks?</strong> Then the solver may land on a local one. Random restarts are the usual fix. It is just why k-means and mixture nets use `n_init`.

## Recap in one screen

- MLE picks the params that make the seen data most likely.
- Work with the log-likelihood: products turn to sums, and underflow goes away.
- Solvers go downhill, so the figure in code is the minus log-likelihood.
- MSE, MAE and cross-entropy are all minus log-likelihoods under other noise guesses.
- Adding a prior turns MLE into MAP. And gives ridge and lasso as cases.
- Steady and fast in large samples. Biased and prone to overfit in small ones.
