## Sample Space and Events

The <strong>sample space</strong> is the complete list of things that could happen &mdash; the tiles shown under the chart. An <strong>event</strong> is any subset of it: "even number", "greater than 4". Selecting events A and B highlights their tiles, and tiles in <span style="color: var(--accent-primary)">both</span> are the intersection.

Getting the sample space right is most of the work. For two dice there are 36 equally likely outcomes but only 11 possible sums &mdash; and those sums are <em>not</em> equally likely, which is why the two-dice chart is a triangle rather than a flat line.

## The Law of Large Numbers — and the Gambler's Fallacy

Press <strong>Run continuously</strong>. Early on the experimental line jumps around wildly; after a few thousand trials it settles onto the theoretical value and stays there.

That is the law of large numbers: averages converge <em>in the long run</em>. What it emphatically does <strong>not</strong> say is that outcomes "balance out". After ten heads, the next flip is still exactly 50/50 &mdash; the coin has no memory. Convergence happens because early flukes are diluted by the growing pile of trials, not because anything corrects them.

## Counting what can happen

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 saying how likely an outcome is. Zero means impossible, one means certain, and everything interesting lives in between.

For situations where every outcome is equally likely, it is a counting exercise:

<p class="mono-font">P(event) = favourable outcomes / total outcomes</p>

A fair die: P(rolling a 4) = 1/6 &asymp; 0.167. P(rolling an even number) = 3/6 = 0.5.

Three rules follow immediately and cover most of what you need:

- <strong>Complement.</strong> P(not A) = 1 &minus; P(A). Frequently the easy way round &mdash; "at least one" problems are almost always solved as 1 &minus; P(none).
- <strong>Addition.</strong> P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) &minus; P(A and B). The subtraction stops the overlap being counted twice.
- <strong>Multiplication.</strong> P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B|A). When the events are independent, P(B|A) is just P(B).

Worked example: two dice, what is P(at least one six)? Directly it is fiddly. Via the complement: P(no six) = (5/6)&times;(5/6) = 25/36, so the answer is 1 &minus; 25/36 = <strong>11/36 &asymp; 0.306</strong>.

## Independence, and why it is usually assumed

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Coin flips are independent; drawing cards without replacement is not.

Independence matters because it makes the multiplication rule simple &mdash; multiply the probabilities and stop. Without it you need conditional probabilities for every combination, and the number of those grows exponentially.

This is exactly the trade Naive Bayes makes: assume every feature is independent given the class, so a joint probability over 20,000 words becomes a product of 20,000 easy numbers. The assumption is false and the classifier works anyway, because the errors it causes affect the magnitudes more than the ranking.

Checking independence is a real modelling step, not a formality. Repeated measurements on the same patient, sensor readings a second apart, and words in the same sentence are all correlated, and treating them as independent produces overconfident models.

## Distributions in one page

A distribution describes the probabilities of every possible outcome at once.

|Distribution|Models|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Bernoulli|One yes/no trial|A single coin flip|
|Binomial|Successes in n trials|Heads in 10 flips|
|Poisson|Events in a fixed interval|Support tickets per hour|
|Uniform|All outcomes equally likely|A fair die|
|Normal|Values clustered around a mean|Heights, measurement error|
|Exponential|Time until the next event|Time between arrivals|

Two properties summarise most of them: the <strong>mean</strong> (where the centre is) and the <strong>variance</strong> (how spread out it is).

Discrete distributions assign probability to individual values; continuous ones assign probability to <em>ranges</em>, which is why the probability of any exact value is zero for a continuous variable. P(height = 180.000...) is zero; P(179.5 &lt; height &lt; 180.5) is not.

## Things to try

1. <strong>Roll 1 a few times.</strong> Individual results look nothing like the theory &mdash; probability says nothing about single trials.
2. <strong>Now run continuously</strong> and watch the gap between the bars and the theoretical markers close.
3. <strong>Reset and note the error at 10, 100 and 10,000 trials.</strong> It shrinks roughly with &radic;n &mdash; four times the data halves the error.
4. <strong>Switch to two dice.</strong> The distribution becomes a triangle peaking at 7, because 7 has six ways to occur while 12 has only one.
5. <strong>Try event pairs</strong> and watch the independence verdict flip between "independent" and "dependent".

## Summing up

Count the outcomes, define events as subsets, subtract the overlap when combining with OR, and multiply only when events are genuinely independent. Probability describes long-run behaviour &mdash; it makes no promise whatsoever about your next roll.

## Expected value: the number decisions are made on

The expected value is the long-run average outcome &mdash; each value weighted by its probability.

<p class="mono-font">E[X] = &Sigma; x &times; P(x)</p>

A fair die: (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = <strong>3.5</strong>. Note that 3.5 is not a possible roll; an expected value is an average, not a prediction.

The reason it matters is that it turns a set of uncertain outcomes into a single comparable number:

A model flags 1,000 transactions. Each flag costs &pound;5 to review, and each fraud caught saves &pound;200. At a 12% precision, the expected value is 1,000 &times; (0.12&times;200 &minus; 5) = 1,000 &times; 19 = <strong>&pound;19,000</strong>. Raise the threshold to reach 30% precision but only 400 flags: 400 &times; (0.3&times;200 &minus; 5) = <strong>&pound;22,000</strong>. The second is better, and no accuracy metric would have told you that.

Every threshold decision, every "should we ship this model", and every A/B test readout is an expected-value calculation underneath.

## Common misconceptions

- <strong>The gambler's fallacy.</strong> Five heads in a row does not make tails more likely next. Independent events have no memory.
- <strong>Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A).</strong> The probability of a positive test given the disease is not the probability of the disease given a positive test. These differ enormously for rare conditions, and Bayes' theorem is what converts between them.
- <strong>Ignoring the base rate.</strong> A 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 condition produces about 100 false positives for every true one.
- <strong>Reading small samples as signal.</strong> Three conversions from ten visitors is not a 30% conversion rate; the confidence interval spans most of the range.
- <strong>Assuming independence</strong> where measurements are repeated on the same subject.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is the difference between probability and statistics?</strong> Probability reasons from a known model to expected data; statistics reasons from observed data back to a plausible model.

<strong>Can a probability be greater than 1?</strong> No. A probability <em>density</em> can exceed 1, because it is a density over a range rather than a probability of a point &mdash; the area under it still totals 1.

<strong>What are odds?</strong> The ratio of "happens" to "does not": a probability of 0.75 is odds of 3 to 1. Logistic regression works in log-odds because they are unbounded and additive.

<strong>How many samples do I need?</strong> It depends on the effect you want to detect. A power calculation gives the number; guessing gives an inconclusive experiment.

<strong>Why is the normal distribution everywhere?</strong> The central limit theorem: sums and averages of many independent contributions tend towards a normal shape, whatever the individual distributions look like.

<strong>Is a p-value the probability that my hypothesis is wrong?</strong> No &mdash; it is the probability of seeing data this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. The two are routinely confused and are not the same thing.

## Recap in one screen

- Probability runs from 0 to 1; for equally likely outcomes it is a counting ratio.
- Complement, addition and multiplication cover most calculations &mdash; and "at least one" is usually easiest via the complement.
- Independence makes probabilities multiply, which is why models assume it even when it is not quite true.
- A distribution describes all outcomes at once; mean and variance summarise it.
- Expected value converts uncertainty into one number you can make a decision with.
