## What this is

Chance measures how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). When each outcome is equally likely it is pure counting:

P(event) = favourable outcomes / total outcomes.

A fair die has 6 outcomes, 3 of which are still. So P(still) = 3/6 = 0.5. A standard deck of 52 cards has 4 aces. So P(ace) = 4/52 &asymp; 0.077. The counting formula works just when each outcome is equally likely. It is true for fair dice and well-shuffled decks and false for almost all else in the real world.

When outcomes are not equally likely, chance is given by frequency (how often something happens in repeated trials), by a net (a spread fitted to data). Or by belief (a prior updated with evidence, as in Bayesian test time). Machine learning uses all three. And they converge on the same rules.

The rules them are few. The chance of an event is between 0 and 1. The chances of all possible outcomes sum to 1. The chance of A or B happening is P(A) + P(B) minus the chance of both. It corrects for double-counting the overlap. The chance of A and B happening depends on whether they are free: if A does not affect B, P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B). Otherwise, P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B given A).

These rules are the foundation for all that follows in this track: conditional chance, Bayes' theorem, spreads, expected value and the probabilistic nets that underpin sorting, regression and generative AI.

## Sample Space and Events

The <strong>sample space</strong> is the complete list of things that could happen. The tiles shown under the chart. An <strong>event</strong> is any subset of it: "still count", "greater than 4". Selecting events A and B highlights their tiles. And tiles in <span style="color: var(--accent-primary)">both</span> are the intersection.

Getting the sample space right is most of the work. For two dice there are 36 equally likely outcomes but just 11 possible sums. And those sums are <em>not</em> equally likely. It is why the two-dice chart is a triangle rather than a flat line.

## Mixing Events

That subtraction is the part people forget. Adding P(A) and P(B) double-counts each outcome living in both events. So you remove one copy. Pick two overlapping events and check the arithmetic against the live counts.

When events cannot happen together &mdash; <em>mutually exclusive</em>. The overlap is 0 and the rule simplifies to plain addition.

## The Law of Large Counts — and the Gambler's Fallacy

Press <strong>Run continuously</strong>. Early on the test-based line jumps around wildly. After a few thousand trials it settles onto the theoretical value and stays there.

That is the law of large counts: averages converge <em>in the long run</em>. What it emphatically does <strong>not</strong> say is that outcomes "balance out". After ten heads, the next flip is still just 50/50. The coin has no memory. Convergence happens since early flukes are diluted by the growing pile of trials, not since anything corrects them.

## Counting what can happen

A chance is a count between 0 and 1 saying how likely an outcome is. Zero means impossible, one means certain. And all interesting lives in between.

For cases where each outcome is equally likely, it is a counting exercise:

<p class="mono-font">P(event) = favourable outcomes / total outcomes</p>

A fair die: P(rolling a 4) = 1/6 &asymp; 0.167. P(rolling an still count) = 3/6 = 0.5.

Three rules follow at once and cover most of what you need:

- <strong>Complement.</strong> P(not A) = 1 &minus; P(A). Often the easy way round. "at least one" problems are almost always solved as 1 &minus; P(none).
- <strong>Addition.</strong> P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) &minus; P(A and B). The subtraction stops the overlap being counted twice.
- <strong>Multiplication.</strong> P(A and B) = P(A) &times; P(B|A). When the events are free, P(B|A) is just P(B).

Worked case: two dice, what is P(at least one six)? Right it is fiddly. Via the complement: P(no six) = (5/6)&times;(5/6) = 25/36. So the answer is 1 &minus; 25/36 = <strong>11/36 &asymp; 0.306</strong>.

## Independence, and why it is often assumed

Two events are free when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Coin flips are free. Drawing cards without replacement is not.

Independence matters since it makes the multiplication rule simple &mdash; multiply the chances and stop. Without it you need conditional chances for each combination. And the count of those grows exponentially.

This is just the trade Naive Bayes makes: assume each feature is free given the class. So a joint chance over 20,000 words turns a product of 20,000 easy counts. The assumption is false and the sorter works anyway, since the errors it causes affect the magnitudes more than the ranking.

Checking independence is a real modelling step, not a formality. Repeated measurements on the same patient, sensor readings a second apart. And words in the same sentence are all correlated. And treating them as free gives too sure nets.

## Spreads in one page

A spread describes the chances of each possible outcome at once.

|Distribution|Models|Example|
|---|---|---|
|Bernoulli|One yes/no trial|A single coin flip|
|Binomial|Successes in n trials|Heads in 10 flips|
|Poisson|Events in a fixed interval|Support tickets per hour|
|Uniform|All outcomes equally likely|A fair die|
|Normal|Values clustered around a mean|Heights, measurement error|
|Exponential|Time until the next event|Time between arrivals|

Two properties summarise most of them: the <strong>mean</strong> (where the centre is) and the <strong>variance</strong> (how spread out it is).

Discrete spreads give chance to individual vals. Continuous ones give chance to <em>ranges</em>. It is why the chance of any exact value is zero for a continuous var. P(height = 180.000...) is zero. P(179.5 &lt; height &lt; 180.5) is not.

## Roll it 600,000 times

Chance as a long-run frequency, measured rather than asserted.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=600_000)
print("a fair die, 600,000 rolls:")
for face in range(1, 7):
    print("  %d appeared %6.4f of the time" % (face, (rolls == face).mean()))
print()
two = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(600_000, 2)).sum(axis=1)
print("two dice, P(total = 7) measured %.4f, exact 6/36 = %.4f"
      % ((two == 7).mean(), 6 / 36))
print("            P(total = 2) measured %.4f, exact 1/36 = %.4f"
      % ((two == 2).mean(), 1 / 36))
print()
print("probabilities of all outcomes sum to 1:",
      round(sum((two == t).mean() for t in range(2, 13)), 6))
```

## Things to try

1. <strong>Roll 1 a few times.</strong> Individual results look nothing like the theory. Chance says nothing about single trials.
2. <strong>Now run continuously</strong> and watch the gap between the bars and the theoretical markers close.
3. <strong>Reset and note the error at 10, 100 and 10,000 trials.</strong> It shrinks roughly with &radic;n. Four times the data halves the error.
4. <strong>Switch to two dice.</strong> The spread turns a triangle peaking at 7, since 7 has six ways to occur while 12 has just one.
5. <strong>Try event pairs</strong> and watch the independence verdict flip between "free" and "dependent".

## Summing up

Count the outcomes, set events as subsets, subtract the overlap when mixing with OR. And multiply just when events are genuinely free. Chance describes long-run behaviour &mdash; it makes no promise whatsoever about your next roll.

## Expected value: the count decisions are made on

The expected value is the long-run average outcome &mdash; each value weighted by its chance.

<p class="mono-font">E[X] = &Sigma; x &times; P(x)</p>

A fair die: (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = <strong>3.5</strong>. Note that 3.5 is not a possible roll. An expected value is an average, not a prediction.

The reason it matters is that it turns a set of uncertain outcomes into a single comparable count:

A net flags 1,000 transactions. Each flag costs &pound;5 to review. And each fraud caught saves &pound;200. At a 12% precision, the expected value is 1,000 &times; (0.12&times;200 &minus; 5) = 1,000 &times; 19 = <strong>&pound;19,000</strong>. Raise the threshold to reach 30% precision but just 400 flags: 400 &times; (0.3&times;200 &minus; 5) = <strong>&pound;22,000</strong>. The second is better. And no score metric would have told you that.

Each threshold decision, each "should we ship this net". And each A/B test readout is an expected-value math underneath.

## Common misconceptions

- <strong>The gambler's fallacy.</strong> Five heads in a row does not make tails more likely next. Free events have no memory.
- <strong>Confusing P(A|B) with P(B|A).</strong> The chance of a positive test given the disease is not the chance of the disease given a positive test. These differ enormously for rare conditions. And Bayes' theorem is what turns between them.
- <strong>Ignoring the base rate.</strong> A 99%-exact test for a 1-in-10,000 condition gives about 100 false positives for each true one.
- <strong>Reading small samples as signal.</strong> Three conversions from ten visitors is not a 30% change rate. The confidence interval spans most of the range.
- <strong>Assuming independence</strong> where measurements are repeated on the same subject.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is the gap between chance and stats?</strong> Chance reasons from a known net to expected data. Stats reasons from observed data back to a plausible net.

<strong>Can a chance be greater than 1?</strong> No. A chance <em>density</em> can exceed 1, since it is a density over a range rather than a chance of a point. The area under it still totals 1.

<strong>What are odds?</strong> The ratio of "happens" to "does not": a chance of 0.75 is odds of 3 to 1. Logistic regression works in log-odds since they are unbounded and additive.

<strong>How many samples do I need?</strong> It depends on the effect you want to detect. A power math gives the count. Guessing gives an inconclusive test.

<strong>Why is the normal spread all over?</strong> The central limit theorem: sums and averages of many free contributions tend towards a normal shape, whatever the individual spreads look like.

<strong>Is a p-value the chance that my hypothesis is wrong?</strong> No. It is the chance of seeing data this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. The two are routinely confused and are not the same thing.

## Recap in one screen

- Chance runs from 0 to 1. For equally likely outcomes it is a counting ratio.
- Complement, addition and multiplication cover most math. And "at least one" is often easiest via the complement.
- Independence makes chances multiply. It is why nets assume it still when it is not quite true.
- A spread describes all outcomes at once. Mean and variance summarise it.
- Expected value turns uncertainty into one count you can make a decision with.
