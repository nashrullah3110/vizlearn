## What this is

Probability measures how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). When every outcome is equally likely it is pure counting:

## Sample Space and Events

The <strong>sample space</strong> is the complete list of things that could happen &mdash; the tiles shown under the chart. An <strong>event</strong> is any subset of it: "even number", "greater than 4". Selecting events A and B highlights their tiles, and tiles in <span style="color: var(--accent-primary)">both</span> are the intersection.

Getting the sample space right is most of the work. For two dice there are 36 equally likely outcomes but only 11 possible sums &mdash; and those sums are <em>not</em> equally likely, which is why the two-dice chart is a triangle rather than a flat line.

## The Law of Large Numbers — and the Gambler's Fallacy

Press <strong>Run continuously</strong>. Early on the experimental line jumps around wildly; after a few thousand trials it settles onto the theoretical value and stays there.

That is the law of large numbers: averages converge <em>in the long run</em>. What it emphatically does <strong>not</strong> say is that outcomes "balance out". After ten heads, the next flip is still exactly 50/50 &mdash; the coin has no memory. Convergence happens because early flukes are diluted by the growing pile of trials, not because anything corrects them.

## Things to try

1. <strong>Roll 1 a few times.</strong> Individual results look nothing like the theory &mdash; probability says nothing about single trials.
2. <strong>Now run continuously</strong> and watch the gap between the bars and the theoretical markers close.
3. <strong>Reset and note the error at 10, 100 and 10,000 trials.</strong> It shrinks roughly with &radic;n &mdash; four times the data halves the error.
4. <strong>Switch to two dice.</strong> The distribution becomes a triangle peaking at 7, because 7 has six ways to occur while 12 has only one.
5. <strong>Try event pairs</strong> and watch the independence verdict flip between "independent" and "dependent".

## Summing up

Count the outcomes, define events as subsets, subtract the overlap when combining with OR, and multiply only when events are genuinely independent. Probability describes long-run behaviour &mdash; it makes no promise whatsoever about your next roll.
