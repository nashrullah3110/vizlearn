title: Equation of a Line (y = mx + c): A Practical Guide
intro: Every straight line is two numbers: how steeply it climbs, and where it crosses the vertical axis. Fitting a line to data, and a neuron's decision boundary, are both this equation wearing different vocabulary.

## The two numbers

y = mx + c

- <strong>m is the slope</strong> &mdash; how much y changes for every 1 you move along x. Positive climbs left to right, negative falls, zero is flat.
- <strong>c is the y-intercept</strong> &mdash; the value of y when x is 0, which is exactly where the line crosses the vertical axis.

Slope is rise over run: take any two points on the line and divide the change in y by the change in x. Any two points give the same answer, which is what makes it a straight line.

## Finding a line through two points

Given (1, 5) and (3, 11):

m = (11 &minus; 5) / (3 &minus; 1) = 6 / 2 = 3

Now substitute either point back in to find c. Using (1, 5):

5 = 3(1) + c &nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp; c = 2

So the line is <span class="mono-font">y = 3x + 2</span>. Check it against the other point: 3(3) + 2 = 11. Correct.

## Why this shows up everywhere later

Linear regression fits exactly this equation &mdash; it searches for the m and c that put the line closest to a cloud of points.

A single neuron computes <span class="mono-font">z = wx + b</span>. That is the same equation: the weight <em>is</em> the slope and the bias <em>is</em> the intercept. When you read that a bias &quot;shifts the decision boundary&quot;, this is the shift being described.

In three dimensions the same idea becomes a plane, <span class="mono-font">z = m<sub>1</sub>x + m<sub>2</sub>y + c</span>, and beyond that a hyperplane. The intuition does not change; only the number of slopes does.

## Two numbers describe every straight line

<p class="mono-font">y = mx + c</p>

`m` is the slope &mdash; how much y changes for each unit of x. `c` is the intercept &mdash; the value of y when x is zero.

Change m and the line pivots. Change c and it slides up or down without tilting. Those are the only two things a straight line can do, which is why two numbers are enough.

Reading the slope's sign and size:

|m|Line|
|---|---|
|3|Climbs steeply|
|1|Climbs at 45&deg;|
|0.2|Climbs gently|
|0|Flat|
|&minus;2|Falls|

Slope is rise over run: pick any two points, divide the change in y by the change in x. Any two points on the same line give the same answer &mdash; that constancy is the definition of straightness.

## Finding the line through two points

Given (1, 5) and (3, 11):

<p class="mono-font">m = (11 &minus; 5) / (3 &minus; 1) = 6 / 2 = 3</p>

Substitute either point back in to get c. Using (1, 5): 5 = 3(1) + c, so c = <strong>2</strong>.

The line is y = 3x + 2. Check with the other point: 3(3) + 2 = 11. Correct.

Two other forms of the same line are worth recognising:

- <strong>Point-slope</strong>: y &minus; y&#8321; = m(x &minus; x&#8321;). The most convenient when you have a point and a slope.
- <strong>General</strong>: Ax + By + C = 0. Handles vertical lines, which y = mx + c cannot, and is the form that generalises to planes.

## Where it shows up in machine learning

<strong>Linear regression</strong> is the search for the m and c that put the line closest to a cloud of points. The whole method is this equation plus a definition of "closest".

<strong>A neuron</strong> computes z = wx + b. That is the same equation with different letters: the weight <em>is</em> the slope, the bias <em>is</em> the intercept. When you read that "the bias shifts the decision boundary", this is the shift being described.

<strong>Decision boundaries</strong> in logistic regression and linear SVMs are the line where the score crosses zero &mdash; on one side the model says class A, on the other class B.

<strong>Higher dimensions</strong> keep the idea and add slopes. With two inputs, z = m&#8321;x + m&#8322;y + c is a plane. With 700 inputs it is a hyperplane. You cannot picture it, and none of the algebra changes.

## Perpendicular, parallel, and distance

Two lines are <strong>parallel</strong> when their slopes are equal, and <strong>perpendicular</strong> when the product of their slopes is &minus;1 &mdash; so the perpendicular to a slope of 2 has slope &minus;&frac12;.

That fact does real work: in an SVM the margin is measured perpendicular to the boundary, and in linear regression the residuals are measured vertically rather than perpendicularly &mdash; a distinction that separates ordinary least squares from total least squares and changes the fitted line.

The distance from a point to a line in general form is:

<p class="mono-font">d = |Ax&#8320; + By&#8320; + C| / &radic;(A&sup2; + B&sup2;)</p>

That formula is exactly what an SVM maximises when it looks for the widest margin.

## What usually goes wrong

<strong>Confusing sign with steepness.</strong> A slope of &minus;5 is steeper than a slope of 2. The minus sign only says which way it leans.<strong>Expecting every line to fit this form.</strong> A vertical line has an undefined slope &mdash; x never changes, so the run is zero and you would be dividing by it. Vertical lines are written <span class="mono-font">x = k</span> instead, which is why regression cannot fit perfectly vertical relationships.

## Fitting a line to data

Real data does not sit on a line, so "the line through the points" becomes "the line closest to the points". Ordinary least squares defines closest as the smallest total squared vertical distance, and gives a formula for the answer:

<p class="mono-font">m = &Sigma;(x &minus; x&#772;)(y &minus; y&#772;) / &Sigma;(x &minus; x&#772;)&sup2;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;c = y&#772; &minus; m&thinsp;x&#772;</p>

Two things fall out of the second formula. The fitted line always passes through the point (mean of x, mean of y). And once you have the slope, the intercept costs nothing.

```python
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
y = np.array([2.1, 4.2, 5.8, 8.1, 9.9])

m, c = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)          # slope and intercept
print(f"y = {m:.2f}x + {c:.2f}")    # y = 1.96x + 0.10
```

Reading the result in the data's own units is the point of the exercise: a slope of 1.96 means each extra unit of x is associated with roughly two extra units of y.

## Common misconceptions

- <strong>Confusing sign with steepness.</strong> A slope of &minus;5 is steeper than a slope of 2; the sign only says which way it leans.
- <strong>Interpreting the intercept when x = 0 is impossible.</strong> "The price of a zero-square-metre house" is not a meaningful number; it is just where the line crosses the axis.
- <strong>Expecting every relationship to be a line.</strong> A vertical line has undefined slope, and many real relationships curve. Check a scatter plot before fitting one.
- <strong>Extrapolating beyond the data.</strong> A line fitted between x = 1 and x = 10 will confidently answer for x = 500, and that answer is fiction.
- <strong>Reading a slope as causation.</strong> It is an association, and nothing about fitting a line establishes that x causes y.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What if the line is vertical?</strong> The slope is undefined, because the run is zero. Vertical lines are written x = k, which is why regression cannot fit a perfectly vertical relationship.

<strong>What is the difference between m and a correlation coefficient?</strong> The slope is in the data's units (pounds per square metre); the correlation is unitless and between &minus;1 and 1. They share a sign and answer different questions.

<strong>How does this become a plane?</strong> Add another input and another slope: z = m&#8321;x + m&#8322;y + c. Each input contributes its own slope.

<strong>Why is it called linear regression if I can fit curves?</strong> Because it is linear in the <em>parameters</em>. Add x&sup2; as a feature and the model fits a parabola while remaining a linear model.

<strong>Does the intercept matter?</strong> Usually yes &mdash; forcing c = 0 makes the line pass through the origin, which is a strong assumption and rarely justified.

<strong>How do I know a line is a good fit?</strong> Look at the residual plot. A shapeless band around zero means the line captured the pattern; a curve means it did not.

## Recap in one screen

- y = mx + c: the slope tilts the line, the intercept slides it.
- Slope is rise over run, and it is identical between any two points on the line.
- A neuron's z = wx + b is the same equation; weight is slope, bias is intercept.
- More inputs means more slopes &mdash; a plane, then a hyperplane, with the algebra unchanged.
- Least squares finds the line minimising squared vertical distance, and it always passes through the means.
