title: Read a Line from Its Equation
intro: In y = mx + c, m gives the change in y for each unit of x; c gives y when x = 0. Change either value in the graph to see its effect.

## Read slope and intercept

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="y equals m times x plus c">y = mx + c</div>

The slope <code>m</code> is the rate of change: how much y changes for each unit change in x. A positive slope rises as x increases; a negative slope falls; zero makes a horizontal line. The intercept <code>c</code> is the y-coordinate where the line crosses the y-axis, at <code>(0, c)</code>.

For <code>y = 2x + 1</code>, x = 0 gives y = 1, and every increase of 1 in x raises y by 2. In the graph, set <strong>Slope (m)</strong> to 2 and <strong>Y-Int (c)</strong> to 1. Changing c to 3 moves every point up by 2; changing m changes how quickly y rises.

## Build a line from two points

For points <code>(1, 5)</code> and <code>(3, 11)</code>, the slope is

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="m equals eleven minus five divided by three minus one, which equals three">m = (11 − 5) / (3 − 1) = 3</div>

Substitute either point into <code>y = mx + c</code>. Using (1, 5), <code>5 = 3(1) + c</code>, so <code>c = 2</code>. The line is <code>y = 3x + 2</code>; checking the second point gives <code>11 = 3(3) + 2</code>.

The same line can be written in point-slope form as <code>y - 5 = 3(x - 1)</code>, which starts from a known point and slope. Rearranging gives slope-intercept form, <code>y = 3x + 2</code>, or standard form, <code>3x - y + 2 = 0</code>.

This method fails when the two points have the same x-coordinate: the run is zero, so the slope is undefined. That line is vertical and has the form <code>x = k</code>; it cannot be written as <code>y = mx + c</code>.

## Fit a line to measured data

Real measurements rarely land exactly on one line. Ordinary least squares chooses the slope and intercept that minimise the sum of squared vertical residuals. With an intercept, the fitted coefficients are

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="m equals the sum of x minus x bar times y minus y bar, divided by the sum of x minus x bar squared; c equals y bar minus m times x bar">m = Σ[(x − x̄)(y − ȳ)] / Σ[(x − x̄)²]　　c = ȳ − m x̄</div>

For example, fitting the points <code>(1, 2.1), (2, 4.2), (3, 5.8), (4, 8.1), (5, 9.9)</code> gives <code>y = 1.95x + 0.17</code>. In this range, a one-unit increase in x is associated with about a 1.95-unit increase in y. That describes an association; it does not show that x causes y. A straight-line fit can also be misleading when the relationship curves or when it is extrapolated far beyond the measured x-values.

The fitted line passes through <code>(x̄, ȳ)</code>. The [scikit-learn linear regression lesson](../sklearn/linear_regression.html) shows the same fit in code; the [official LinearRegression documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression.html) describes its ordinary least-squares objective.

## Connect the line to a classifier

A classifier with two input features can place its decision boundary where <code>w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0</code>. When <code>w₂ ≠ 0</code>, rearranging gives <code>x₂ = −(w₁/w₂)x₁ − b/w₂</code>, which is a line: its slope is <code>−w₁/w₂</code> and its intercept is <code>−b/w₂</code>. This is why a linear model's boundary can be drawn as a line, while its coefficients are not each individually “the slope” or “the intercept.” See the [perceptron lesson](../deep_learning/perceptron.html) for the weighted-sum form.

## Try this above

1. Press <strong>Horizontal</strong>. The slope is zero, so moving x does not change y.
2. Press <strong>Negative</strong>. The line falls from left to right; the sign sets direction, while the absolute value sets steepness.
3. Set <strong>Slope (m)</strong> to 2 and <strong>Y-Int (c)</strong> to 1. Move the x control and check that each increase of 1 raises y by 2.
4. Change only <strong>Y-Int (c)</strong>. The line shifts without changing its slope.
5. Switch to <strong>3D Space</strong> and vary the second slope. The visualiser traces a 3D line whose y and z coordinates each change with x.
