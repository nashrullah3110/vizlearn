title: Convexity and Optimisation
intro: Convexity rules out hidden local minima: for a convex objective, every local minimum is global.

## The chord condition

A set is convex when the line segment between any two of its points stays inside it. A function f is convex when its graph lies at or below every chord between two points:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="f of t x plus one minus t y is at most t f x plus one minus t f y for t between zero and one">f(tx + (1−t)y) ≤ tf(x) + (1−t)f(y), &nbsp; 0 ≤ t ≤ 1</div>

For a twice-differentiable one-variable function, f″(x) ≥ 0 everywhere on an interval is sufficient for convexity there. For a multivariable function, the Hessian must be positive semidefinite throughout the convex domain.

## Why it helps—and what it cannot promise

For a convex objective, any local minimum is global. This makes optimisation easier to reason about, though a minimum may still be non-unique and an algorithm may converge slowly. Many deep-learning objectives are non-convex; gradient methods can still find useful solutions, but convex guarantees do not apply. See [gradients](partial_derivatives_and_gradient.html) and [Hessians](jacobian_and_hessian.html).

A learning rate controls update size, not convexity. On a simple quadratic, too large a step can oscillate or diverge even though the objective is convex.

## Test convexity and understand the guarantee

For one variable, a nonnegative second derivative throughout an interval implies convexity there. For a twice-differentiable multivariable function, a positive-semidefinite Hessian throughout a convex domain is a sufficient test. For f(x)=x², f″=2, so every chord lies above the bowl and any local minimum is global. By contrast, a function with multiple dips can have local minima that are not global.

Convexity is a property of both the objective and its domain. A convex objective on a non-convex feasible set does not automatically give the same global guarantee. Even when the problem is convex, the minimum can be non-unique, lie at a boundary, or be difficult for a poor algorithm to reach. Gradient descent can diverge on a convex quadratic if its step size is too large. Convexity gives a landscape guarantee; it does not guarantee that a particular numerical procedure succeeds. Constrained problems introduce additional conditions; see [Lagrange multipliers](lagrange_multipliers.html).

## Try this above

Compare a bowl-shaped curve with a curve that has multiple dips. Move the optimiser step size and observe convergence separately from the shape of the objective.

For a convex differentiable function, the tangent at x lies below the function everywhere in its domain: f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)·(y−x). This gives a supporting lower bound that helps prove optimality. If ∇f(x)=0, then f(y)≥f(x) for all y, so x is a global minimiser. For a non-convex objective, zero gradient alone gives no such guarantee. This distinction is why a stationary point check is enough in some convex problems but only a candidate check in general optimisation.

For a convex differentiable function, a point with zero gradient is a global minimum, but a boundary-constrained optimum may have a nonzero gradient pointing outside the feasible set. This is why constraints require additional conditions such as Lagrange multipliers or KKT conditions. A convex function may also have a flat valley of multiple minimisers. Separate the geometry of the objective from the geometry of the feasible set before applying an optimisation guarantee.
