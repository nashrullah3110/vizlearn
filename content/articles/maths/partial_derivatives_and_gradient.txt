title: Partial Derivatives and the Gradient
intro: One slope per direction &mdash; and the vector that combines them into "uphill".

## Start here

Standing on a hillside, "how steep is it?" has no single answer &mdash; it depends which way you face. A <strong>partial derivative</strong> answers it for one fixed way. The <strong>gradient</strong> bundles those answers into a vector pointing straight up the slope.

A partial derivative &part;f/&part;x measures how f changes when you move in the x way while holding each other var fixed. For a function of two vars, there are two partial derivatives: one for x and one for y. For a function of a thousand vars (a loss function with a thousand weights), there are a thousand partial derivatives, each measuring the sensitivity of the loss to one weight while all others are frozen.

The gradient is the vector of all partial derivatives: &nabla;f = (&part;f/&part;x&#8321;, &part;f/&part;x&#8322;, ..., &part;f/&part;x&#8345;). It points in the way of steepest ascent. And its magnitude is how steep that ascent is. On a flat plateau the gradient is the zero vector. At a saddle point it is also zero. But the curvature differs by way.

Gradient descent is the direct application: to minimise a function, take a step in the way opposite to the gradient. The step size is the learning rate. And the gradient tells you both where to step and how urgently. Each weight update in neural network training is one step of gradient descent. And the gradient is computed by backpropagation &mdash; which is just the chain rule applied systematically to the computation graph.

The gradient is perpendicular to the contour lines of the function. Contours are paths of constant value. So the steepest way must be at right angles to them. This geometric fact is visible on any contour plot and is why gradient descent paths cross contour lines rather than next them.

## Partials: Freeze All Else

To compute <span class="mono-font">&part;f/&part;x</span> you treat <span class="mono-font">y</span> as a constant and differentiate often. That is the whole idea &mdash; you are asking how f changes if you step east while refusing to move north.

The blue and amber arrows on the plot are just these two directional slopes.

## The Gradient Vector

Stack the partials and you get the gradient:

It has two remarkable properties, both visible on the plot:

- It points in the way of <strong>steepest ascent</strong> &mdash; the fastest way up from where you stand.
- Its <strong>length</strong> is how steep that climb is. On a flat plateau the arrow shrinks to nothing.

Notice too that the gradient arrow always crosses the contour lines at right angles &mdash; contours are paths of constant height. So the steepest way must be perpendicular to them.

## Gradient Descent: Just Walk Backwards

Training a net means finding the <em>lowest</em> point of a loss surface. The gradient points uphill, so you step the other way:

Press <strong>Descend</strong> repeatedly and watch the point walk downhill, taking big strides where the surface is steep and tiny ones as it flattens out &mdash; automatic step-size control, straight out of the maths.

Real nets have millions of params instead of two. So the gradient is a million-dimensional vector. The principle is identical. Just the picture is impossible to draw.

## One var at a time

A partial derivative asks a deliberately narrow question: if I change <em>this</em> input by a tiny amount and hold each other input still, how much does the output move?

For f(x, y) = x&sup2; + 3xy + y&sup2;:

- Treat y as a constant: &part;f/&part;x = 2x + 3y.
- Treat x as a constant: &part;f/&part;y = 3x + 2y.

At the point (2, 1) those give 4 + 3 = <strong>7</strong> and 6 + 2 = <strong>8</strong>. So moving along x raises f at a rate of 7 per unit. And along y at 8 per unit.

That is all a partial derivative is &mdash; an ordinary derivative with the other vars frozen. The curly &part; is just there to remind you that other vars exist.

## The gradient: all the partials in one vector

Collect each partial derivative into a vector and you have the <strong>gradient</strong>:

<p class="mono-font">&nabla;f = [ &part;f/&part;x, &part;f/&part;y ] = [7, 8] at (2, 1)</p>

Two properties make this the central object in machine learning:

1. <strong>The gradient points in the way of steepest increase.</strong> Of all the ways you could step, that vector is the one where f grows fastest.
2. <strong>Its length is how steep that steepest way is.</strong> A long gradient means a cliff. A near-zero gradient means a plateau.

Both follow from the geometry: the rate of change in an arbitrary way is the dot product of the gradient with that way. And a dot product is maximised when the two vectors align.

The immediate consequence for training: to <em>decrease</em> a loss, step in the way of the negative gradient. That single sentence is gradient descent.

<p class="mono-font">w &larr; w &minus; &eta; &nabla;L(w)</p>

## Scaling to millions of params

A neural network's loss is a function of each weight in it. A net with 10 million params has a loss surface in 10-million-dimensional space. And its gradient is a vector with 10 million entries &mdash; one partial derivative per weight, each saying "if I nudge this one weight, how does the loss respond?"

Nobody computes those one at a time. Backpropagation tests the whole gradient in roughly the cost of one forward pass, by applying the chain rule backwards through the network and reusing the shared factors. That efficiency is the reason deep learning works at all.

The mental picture that survives the jump in dimensions: you are standing somewhere on a landscape, you can feel which way is downhill. And you take a step. You cannot see the whole landscape and you do not need to.

## Reading the gradient in training

Gradient vals are one of the most informative diagnostics on hand while a net trains.

|What you see|What it usually means|
|---|---|
|Gradient norm near zero early on|Dead neurons, saturated activations, or a bad initialisation|
|Gradient norm exploding to `NaN`|Learning rate too high, or no clipping in a recurrent model|
|Gradients fine at the output, tiny at the input|Vanishing gradients through depth|
|Loss falling but gradients steady|Healthy training|
|Loss flat and gradients large|Learning rate too high &mdash; bouncing across a valley|

Logging the gradient norm per layer costs almost nothing and answers most "why is this not training" questions faster than staring at the loss curve.

## Measure both partials

Each partial derivative holds the other var still. Together they are the gradient, which points the steepest way up.

```python-run
import numpy as np

# f(x, y) = x^2 + 3xy + y^2
def f(x, y): return x ** 2 + 3 * x * y + y ** 2

x, y, h = 1.0, 2.0, 1e-6

df_dx = (f(x + h, y) - f(x, y)) / h        # y held still
df_dy = (f(x, y + h) - f(x, y)) / h        # x held still

print("f(x,y) = x^2 + 3xy + y^2  at (1, 2)")
print("  df/dx numerically %.5f   by hand 2x+3y = %.1f" % (df_dx, 2 * x + 3 * y))
print("  df/dy numerically %.5f   by hand 3x+2y = %.1f" % (df_dy, 3 * x + 2 * y))
print()
grad = np.array([2 * x + 3 * y, 3 * x + 2 * y])
print("gradient =", grad, " length %.3f" % np.linalg.norm(grad))
print()
print("stepping along the gradient increases f the fastest:")
for step in (0.0, 0.01, 0.02):
    p = np.array([x, y]) + step * grad / np.linalg.norm(grad)
    print("  step %.2f -> f = %.6f" % (step, f(p[0], p[1])))
```

## Experiments to try

1. <strong>Click around the bowl.</strong> The gradient always points away from the centre &mdash; uphill &mdash; and shrinks as you way the min.
2. <strong>Press Run on the bowl.</strong> The path heads almost straight to the middle, decelerating as the gradient dies.
3. <strong>Switch to the valley (x&sup2; + 4y&sup2;)</strong> and run again. The path zig-zags, since the surface is far steeper in y than in x &mdash; the exact pathology that Adam and momentum were invented to fix.
4. <strong>Try the saddle</strong> and sit near the origin. One partial pushes up, the other down. The gradient nearly vanishes still though this is <em>not</em> a min &mdash; a genuine trap in high-dimensional optimisation.
5. <strong>Raise the learning rate above 0.4</strong> on the valley and run. The steps overshoot and the point diverges &mdash; the classic symptom of too large a learning rate.

## Summing up

Hold each other var still to get a partial derivative. Collect the partials to get the gradient. It points uphill, its length measures steepness. And stepping against it is what training a neural network literally means.

## Directional derivatives, and the Hessian

Two tied ideas complete the picture.

The <strong>directional derivative</strong> asks how fast f changes if you walk in some set way u (a unit vector). It is simply the dot product:

<p class="mono-font">D<sub>u</sub>f = &nabla;f &middot; u</p>

Walk along the gradient and you get its full magnitude. Walk at right angles to it and you get zero &mdash; you are moving along a contour, staying at the same height. That is why contour lines and gradients always meet at right angles in the pictures.

The <strong>Hessian</strong> is the matrix of second partial derivatives, describing curvature. Its eigenvalues classify a stationary point: all positive means a min, all negative a max, mixed signs a saddle.

That last case matters in practice. In high dimensions, points where the gradient vanishes are overwhelmingly likely to be saddles rather than local minima &mdash; for all the eigenvalues to share a sign is improbable when there are millions of them. This is a large part of why training deep networks works better than early theory feared: the algorithm is escaping saddles, not getting trapped in bad minima.

Second-order ways use the Hessian to choose better steps. But storing an n&times;n matrix for n = 10&#8310; is impossible. It is why first-order ways with clever momentum (Adam and its relatives) dominate in practice.

## Questions people ask

<strong>What is the gap between a derivative and a gradient?</strong> A derivative is for one input. A gradient is the vector of partial derivatives for many.

<strong>Why the minus sign in gradient descent?</strong> The gradient points uphill. And you want to go down.

<strong>Does a zero gradient mean training is finished?</strong> It means you are at a stationary point. It could be a min, a saddle. Or a plateau &mdash; and in deep learning the loss rarely reaches an exact zero gradient anyway.

<strong>What does the gradient's magnitude tell me?</strong> How steep the surface is. It is the count to log when diagnosing vanishing or exploding gradients.

<strong>Do I ever compute partials by hand?</strong> Rarely &mdash; autograd handles it. Grasp them is what makes learning rates, initialisation and design choices make sense.

<strong>What is a stochastic gradient?</strong> The gradient computed on a small batch rather than the whole dataset. It is a noisy estimate of the true gradient. And the noise turns out to help escape saddles.

## Recap in one screen

- A partial derivative measures the effect of one input with the others held fixed.
- The gradient collects them into a vector that points in the way of steepest increase.
- Its length measures steepness. A near-zero gradient means a flat region.
- Gradient descent steps against the gradient, scaled by the learning rate.
- Backpropagation computes the whole gradient for the price of about one forward pass.
