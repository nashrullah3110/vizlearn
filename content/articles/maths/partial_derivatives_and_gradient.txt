title: Partial Derivatives and the Gradient
intro: Partial derivatives measure one coordinate’s effect; together, they form the gradient direction of steepest increase.

## Change one input at a time

For a function of several variables, a partial derivative varies one input while holding the others fixed. For f(x,y) = x² + 3y², ∂f/∂x = 2x and ∂f/∂y = 6y. At (1, 2), the gradient is (2, 12): the local rate of increase is much larger along y than x.

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="gradient f is the vector of partial derivatives of f">∇f = (∂f/∂x₁, …, ∂f/∂xₙ)</div>

The gradient points in the direction of steepest local increase under the usual Euclidean geometry. Its negative points toward steepest local decrease. Gradient descent uses that direction, scaled by a learning rate; a large step can overshoot, and a small step can be slow. See [convexity and optimisation](convexity_and_optimisation.html).

## Limits of the picture

A gradient is local information, not a guarantee that one step reaches a minimum. At a stationary point it is zero, but the point may be a saddle or maximum. If features use different scales, the steepest direction also depends on that coordinate geometry. The [Jacobian and Hessian](jacobian_and_hessian.html) extend the derivative picture to vector outputs and curvature.

## Use a directional derivative to predict a change

At a point x, the gradient ∇f collects the partial derivatives. For a small displacement h, f(x+h) is approximately f(x)+∇f(x)·h. If h is a unit direction, the directional derivative is ∇f·h; its maximum is ‖∇f‖ in the gradient direction. For f(x,y)=x²+3y² at (1,2), the gradient is (2,12), so the steepest local increase points mostly in the y direction. The steepest decrease points in the negative-gradient direction.

This statement assumes the ordinary Euclidean notion of distance. If coordinates use different units or a different geometry, “steepest” depends on how step length is measured; feature scaling can therefore change gradient descent behaviour. Partial derivatives hold the other inputs fixed, which may not match a real process where inputs move together. A zero gradient at a differentiable point indicates no first-order change in any direction, but it can be a minimum, maximum, or saddle. Use the [Hessian](jacobian_and_hessian.html) to examine curvature, and remember that finite steps can overshoot the local approximation.

## Try this above

Move in x and y independently and watch their partial derivatives. Then compare a step along the negative gradient with a step that is too large.

For f(x,y)=x²+3y² at (1,2), a small step h=(0.01,0) predicts a change of about (2,12)·(0.01,0)=0.02. A step of the same Euclidean length in the y direction predicts about 0.12. The approximation works for sufficiently small steps; for larger steps, curvature adds error. This dot-product interpretation explains why the gradient is useful for local optimisation and why the learning rate must be controlled. It also gives a numerical check by comparing predicted and observed changes for a small displacement.

A partial derivative is not the same as the rate along every path through a point. For f(x,y)=xy at (1,1), each partial derivative is 1, but moving along the path y=x changes f=x² at rate 2 with respect to x. The gradient combines partials so a chosen direction's rate is their dot product with that direction. Specify the path or direction when interpreting change in a multivariable system.
