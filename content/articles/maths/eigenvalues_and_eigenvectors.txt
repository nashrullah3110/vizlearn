title: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
intro: An eigenvector keeps its direction under a linear map; its eigenvalue is the scale factor along that direction.

## The defining relationship

For a square matrix A, a nonzero vector v is an eigenvector when Av = λv. The scalar λ is its eigenvalue. A map may stretch, shrink, or reverse v, but it does not turn it into a different direction.

For A = [[2, 0], [0, 3]], the coordinate axes are eigenvectors: (1, 0) has eigenvalue 2 and (0, 1) has eigenvalue 3. A vector not on either axis generally changes direction. Eigenvectors are not unique in length; every nonzero multiple of one is also an eigenvector.

## Finding and interpreting them

Eigenvalues satisfy det(A − λI) = 0; for each eigenvalue, solve (A − λI)v = 0 to find its eigenvectors. Some real matrices have complex eigenvalues, so not every transformation has a real eigenvector direction. Symmetric real matrices are especially useful: they have real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis.

PCA uses eigenvectors of a covariance matrix to identify directions of variance. In practice, singular value decomposition is often preferred for numerical stability, especially for rectangular data; see [SVD](singular_value_decomposition.html) and [covariance](covariance_and_correlation.html). Eigenvalues describe scaling along special directions, not every feature of a transformation.
