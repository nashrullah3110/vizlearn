title: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors: A Practical Guide
intro: The directions a transformation leaves alone, and why PCA is built on them.

## Quick Context

A <a href="matrix_as_transformation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">grid moves space</a>. Almost each vec it touches gets both stretched <em>and</em> rotated. It comes out pointing somewhere new.

But for most matrices there are a small count of special ways where the rotation does not happen. A vec along one of those ways comes out pointing just the same way, just longer or shorter. Those ways are the <strong>eigenvectors</strong>. And the amount each is stretched by is its <strong>eigenvalue</strong>.

The setting equation is Av = &lambda;v: applying the grid A to the vec v gives the same vec scaled by &lambda;. Finding the eigenvalues means solving det(A &minus; &lambda;I) = 0. It is a polynomial of degree n for an n &times; n grid. Once the eigenvalues are known, each eigenvector is found by solving the linear system (A &minus; &lambda;I)v = 0.

An eigenvalue of 2 means the change doubles lengths along that way. An eigenvalue of &minus;1 means it reverses way without changing length. An eigenvalue of 0 means the change collapses that way fully. The grid is singular. And the eigenvector points along the dimension that is destroyed.

PCA is built right on eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The covariance grid of a dataset is symmetric. It guarantees real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue points in the way of greatest variance. The second largest gets the most left variance perpendicular to the first. And so on. Projecting the data onto the top k eigenvectors is dimensionality reduction. The core of PCA. And the eigenvalues tell you how much variance each part explains.

## The definition, and what it is really saying

A v = &lambda; v

Read it as an equation of behaviours rather than symbols. The left-hand side is "apply the whole change to v". The right-hand side is "just scale v by a count". A vec satisfying this is one for which the whole grid. All four entries, all that rotating and shearing. Collapses into a single multiplication.

That is why they matter. Along an eigenvector, a complicated change turns arithmetic.

## Finding them

Rearranging `Av = &lambda;v` gives `(A &minus; &lambda;I)v = 0`. It has a non-zero solution just when the grid `A &minus; &lambda;I` is singular. That is, when its determinant is zero:

<p class="mono-font">det(A &minus; &lambda;I) = 0</p>

For a 2&times;2 grid that is a quadratic in &lambda;. So there are two eigenvalues (possibly repeated, possibly complex). For n&times;n it is a degree-n polynomial. And solving it by hand stops being sensible almost at once.

```python
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[3, 1], [0, 2]])
values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)

values          # [3., 2.]
vectors[:, 0]   # the eigenvector for lambda = 3
```

Two facts worth carrying: the eigenvalues sum to the trace (the diagonal total) and multiply to the determinant. Both are quick sanity checks on a found result.

## The ways a change does not turn

Multiply a grid by a vec and the vec often moves and rotates. For a few special ways, it does not rotate at all. It just stretches or shrinks.

<p class="mono-font">A v = &lambda; v</p>

`v` is an <strong>eigenvector</strong>: a way the change leaves pointing the same way. `&lambda;` is its <strong>eigenvalue</strong>: how much it is stretched along that way.

A worked case. For A = [[3, 1], [0, 2]], try v = [1, 0]:

<p class="mono-font">A [1, 0]&#7488. = [3, 0]&#7488. = 3 &times; [1, 0]&#7488;</p>

Same way, three times as long. So [1, 0] is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 3.

Reading the eigenvalues tells you what the change does:

|Eigenvalue|Effect along that direction|
|---|---|
|&lambda; &gt; 1|Stretch|
|&lambda; = 1|Unchanged|
|0 &lt; &lambda; &lt; 1|Shrink|
|&lambda; = 0|Collapsed to a point &mdash; the matrix is singular|
|&lambda; &lt; 0|Flipped and scaled|

## Why this is the heart of PCA

PCA is just the eigendecomposition of the covariance grid.

The covariance grid describes how the data spreads. Its eigenvectors are the ways of that spread. And its eigenvalues are how much variance lies along each one. Sort by eigenvalue, keep the largest few. And you have compressed the data while preserving as much variation as possible.

That is the whole algorithm. The explained-variance percentages that PCA reports are the eigenvalues divided by their total.

The same breakdown powers other tools:

- <strong>Spectral clustering</strong> uses the eigenvectors of a graph's Laplacian to find groups that k-means cannot.
- <strong>PageRank</strong> is the principal eigenvector of the web's link grid.
- <strong>Vibration and stability review</strong> reads resonant modes as eigenvectors and their frequencies as eigenvalues.
- <strong>Recurrent network stability</strong> depends on the largest eigenvalue of the recurrent weight grid: above 1 the state explodes, below 1 it vanishes. The direct cause of exploding and vanishing gradients through time.

## Symmetric matrices, and why they are pleasant

Covariance matrices, link matrices and Gram matrices are all symmetric. And symmetric matrices have unusually good properties:

- All eigenvalues are real, with no complex counts to worry about.
- Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal &mdash; at right angles.
- They can be written as `A = QΛQᵀ` with Q orthogonal. So the inverse of Q is just its transpose.

That orthogonality is why principal parts are uncorrelated with each other. It is the property that makes them useful as replacement features.

Use `np.linalg.eigh` rather than `eig` for symmetric matrices: it is faster, more exact. And returns the eigenvalues in sorted order.

## See a vec survive the transform

For most vecs a grid changes both way and length. For an eigenvector it just changes the length. And the eigenvalue is by how much.

```python-run
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)

print("eigenvalues :", values.round(4))
print("eigenvectors (columns):"); print(vectors.round(4))
print()
for i in range(2):
    v = vectors[:, i]
    print("A @ v%d = %s" % (i, (A @ v).round(4)))
    print("  %.2f * v%d = %s   <- the same vector, only scaled"
          % (values[i], i, (values[i] * v).round(4)))
print()
print("an eigenvector is a direction the matrix does not rotate.")
```

## Why symmetric matrices are special

When b = c the grid is symmetric. And three things turn guaranteed rather than lucky: the eigenvalues are always real, the eigenvectors are always perpendicular. And the grid can always be diagonalised.

This is not a curiosity. <a href="mean_variance_standard_deviation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Covariance</a> matrices are symmetric by construction, since the covariance of x with y is the same count as the covariance of y with x. PCA is precisely the eigendecomposition of a covariance grid. And the guarantee above is what makes its principal parts perpendicular and its variances real, positive counts. The whole way rests on this property.

## Where else they turn up

- <strong>PCA</strong> &mdash; eigenvectors of the covariance grid are the ways of greatest variance. The eigenvalues are how much variance each one carries.
- <strong>PageRank</strong> &mdash; the ranking is the dominant eigenvector of the link grid.
- <strong>Stability</strong> &mdash; repeatedly applying a grid makes vecs blow up if the largest |&lambda;| exceeds 1 and shrink to nothing if it is below. This is the exploding-and-vanishing-gradient story in a other vocabulary.
- <strong>Conditioning</strong> &mdash; the ratio of the largest to smallest eigenvalue tells you how stretched the loss surface is. It is what makes plain gradient descent zig-zag.

## Traps worth knowing

- <strong>Expecting each grid to have real eigenvectors.</strong> Rotations have none. A negative discriminant is not an error.
- <strong>Forgetting eigenvectors have no fixed length.</strong> If v is an eigenvector so is 2v and &minus;v. Just the way is found. It is why implementations give unit vecs and the sign can differ between libraries.
- <strong>Assuming perpendicularity in general.</strong> That is guaranteed just for symmetric matrices. The Generic preset shows two eigenvectors that are not at right angles.
- <strong>Running PCA on unscaled features.</strong> The covariance grid is dominated by whichever feature has the largest units. So the first part just points at it. Standardise first.

## In one line

An eigenvector is a way a change does not rotate. And its eigenvalue is the factor it is stretched by. So along an eigenvector the whole grid collapses into a single multiplication. For a 2&times;2 grid they follow from the trace and determinant alone. And always satisfy &lambda;&#8321;+&lambda;&#8322. = trace and &lambda;&#8321;&lambda;&#8322; = det. A negative discriminant means no real eigenvectors exist. It is just the case for a rotation. Symmetric matrices are guaranteed real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. And since covariance matrices are symmetric, that guarantee is what PCA is built on.

## Eigen, singular vals, and which to use

Eigendecomposition needs a square grid. Real data is rarely square. It is n samples by p features.

<strong>Singular value breakdown</strong> transfers the idea to any grid:

<p class="mono-font">A = U &Sigma; V&#7488;</p>

The singular vals in &Sigma; play the role of eigenvalues. And V's columns are ways in feature space. For a covariance grid the two coincide: the singular vals are the square roots of the eigenvalues.

In practice, PCA implementations use SVD on the centred data grid rather than forming the covariance grid and decomposing it, since it is numerically more stable and avoids squaring the condition count. Scikit-learn's `PCA` does just this.

SVD also does jobs eigendecomposition cannot: low-rank approximation for compression, grid completion for recommender systems. And pseudo-inverses for least squares problems with no unique solution.

## Real notes

- <strong>Scale before decomposing.</strong> Eigenvalues of a covariance grid depend on units. So an unscaled column with a large range dominates the first part.
- <strong>Signs are arbitrary.</strong> An eigenvector and its negation describe the same way. Do not read meaning into which one a library returns.
- <strong>Order is not guaranteed</strong> by `np.linalg.eig`. Sort clearly, or use `eigh` for symmetric matrices.
- <strong>Repeated eigenvalues</strong> mean the corresponding eigenvectors are not unique. Any rotation within that subspace works. So single part ways turn meaningless.
- <strong>Very small eigenvalues</strong> show near-collinear features and an ill-conditioned grid. There solving linear systems turns numerically unreliable.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Can a grid have no eigenvectors?</strong> Over the real counts, yes. A pure rotation turns each way. So it has no real eigenvectors. Over the complex counts, each square grid has them.

<strong>What does a zero eigenvalue mean?</strong> The change collapses that way fully. The grid is singular and has no inverse.

<strong>How many eigenvalues does an n&times;n grid have?</strong> Just n, counting repeats and complex vals.

<strong>Why are eigenvectors often scaled?</strong> Since any scalar multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector. Fixing the length to 1 makes the answer unique up to sign.

<strong>Is this just for PCA?</strong> No. Spectral clustering, PageRank, graph review, differential equations, quantum mechanics and network stability all use the same breakdown.

<strong>How does this relate to the determinant?</strong> The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues. It is why a zero eigenvalue means a zero determinant and a singular grid.

## Recap in one screen

- An eigenvector is a way a grid does not rotate. Its eigenvalue is the factor it is stretched by.
- Solve `det(A - λI) = 0` for the eigenvalues, then find each eigenvector. Or call a library.
- The eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant.
- PCA is the eigendecomposition of the covariance grid: eigenvectors are parts, eigenvalues are explained variance.
- Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. It is why parts are uncorrelated.
- SVD transfers the idea to non-square matrices and is what implementations really use.
