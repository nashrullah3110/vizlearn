title: Central Limit Theorem
intro: Under suitable conditions, sums and averages of many independent contributions become approximately normal after standardisation.

## What the theorem says

For independent, identically distributed variables with finite mean μ and finite variance σ², the standardised sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution as sample size grows:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="square root n times sample mean minus mu divided by sigma converges in distribution to standard normal">√n(x̄−μ)/σ → N(0,1) in distribution</div>

The theorem explains why normal approximations appear in inference even when individual observations are not normal. It concerns the distribution of a sum or average across repeated samples, not the shape of the raw data itself.

## Conditions and practical limits

There is no universal sample size that guarantees a good approximation. Strong skew, heavy tails, dependence, or rare events can require much larger samples or invalidate the simple form. If variance is infinite, the usual result does not apply. The [sampling-distributions lesson](sampling_distributions.html) distinguishes this distribution from population spread.

The theorem does not claim every statistic is normal. Medians, maxima, and ratios can have different limiting behavior. Check the statistic and assumptions used by the approximation before applying normal-based intervals or tests.

## What becomes normal—and what does not

If independent observations have common mean μ and finite variance σ², the sample mean has mean μ and standard error σ/√n. The central limit theorem says the distribution of the standardised sample mean, √n(x̄−μ)/σ, approaches the standard normal distribution as n grows. The theorem describes repeated sample averages. It does not say that the raw observations become normal, nor is it the same as the law of large numbers, which describes sample averages moving toward μ.

A highly skewed population can produce a skewed distribution of sample means at small n; the needed sample size depends on the population's tails and skew. Dependence reduces the effective amount of information, and infinite variance can invalidate the usual result. The theorem does not repair biased sampling, make every statistic normal, or guarantee that a normal approximation is accurate at a universal sample size. Before using a normal interval or test, check which statistic is being approximated and whether its assumptions fit the sampling process. See [sampling distributions](sampling_distributions.html) for the distinction between data variation and estimator variation.

## Try this above

Compare repeated sample means as n grows for both a symmetric and a skewed population. Look at the mean’s distribution, not just the original observations.

A common use is approximating a sample mean by a normal distribution so that tail areas and intervals can be calculated. If σ is known, the standardised mean has approximate standard error σ/√n. For a Bernoulli outcome, σ²=p(1−p), so the approximation is weakest when expected successes or failures are very few. A large n alone is not enough; verify that the sample is representative, observations are not strongly dependent, and the distribution is not dominated by rare extreme values.
