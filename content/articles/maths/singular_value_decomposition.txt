title: Singular Value Decomposition
intro: SVD factors any real matrix into rotations or reflections and axis-wise scaling, exposing its most important directions.

## The factorisation

Every real m × n matrix A can be written as A = UΣVᵀ. The columns of U and V are orthonormal directions; the nonnegative diagonal entries of Σ are singular values, ordered from largest to smallest. Right singular vectors in V describe input directions; the matching columns of U show their output directions.

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="A equals U Sigma V transpose">A = UΣVᵀ</div>

A vector along a right singular direction is mapped to the matching left direction, scaled by its singular value. A zero singular value marks a collapsed direction. Unlike eigendecomposition, SVD applies to rectangular matrices and does not require the matrix to be symmetric.

## Low-rank approximation

Keeping only the k largest singular values gives the best rank-k approximation to A in the usual Frobenius and spectral norms. This supports compression and denoising, but small singular values are not automatically noise: choosing k requires evidence from the data and task. The rank is the number of nonzero singular values (or values above a numerical tolerance).

For a small example, A = [[3, 0], [0, 1]] already has singular values 3 and 1: its first axis is stretched three times as much as the second. See [rank](rank_and_linear_independence.html), [projections](projections.html), and [PCA](eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors.html).

## Read the three stages of the decomposition

For A=UΣVᵀ, Vᵀ first expresses an input in orthogonal right-singular directions. Σ scales each coordinate by its nonnegative singular value. U then expresses the output in orthogonal left-singular directions. For A=diag(3,1), the first axis is stretched by 3 and the second by 1. If a singular value is zero, its input direction is lost. Singular vectors can change sign in a computed decomposition without changing A, so their sign alone is not meaningful.

Keeping the k largest singular values gives the best rank-k approximation in Frobenius and spectral norm. This can compress an image or reduce a data matrix, but “small” singular values are not automatically noise; choosing k depends on the task and validation. For a covariance matrix, PCA directions are related to the right singular vectors of centred data, and explained variance is proportional to squared singular values. SVD works for rectangular matrices and is more robust than forming AᵀA to obtain eigenvectors, though large decompositions can still be computationally expensive. Rank is the count of nonzero singular values in exact arithmetic; numerical software uses a tolerance.

## Try this above

Reduce the number of retained singular directions and compare the reconstructed matrix with the original. Observe which structure disappears first.

The largest singular value is the maximum amount by which A can stretch a unit vector; the smallest nonzero singular value describes the least-stretched retained direction. Their ratio is the 2-norm condition number for a full-rank square matrix. A very small singular value means some directions are nearly collapsed, so solving Ax=b can amplify measurement error. Truncating such directions may stabilise a model, but it also discards information. Inspect the singular-value spectrum and validate the chosen cutoff rather than treating the smallest values as automatically irrelevant.
