title: Mean, Variance and Standard Deviation
intro: The mean locates a dataset’s centre; variance and standard deviation describe its spread.

## Calculate the summaries

For values x₁,…,xₙ, the mean is x̄ = Σxᵢ/n. Population variance averages squared deviations from the population mean. For a sample, the usual unbiased estimator of population variance divides by n−1:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="sample variance equals sum of squared deviations from sample mean divided by n minus one">s² = Σᵢ(xᵢ−x̄)² / (n−1)</div>

For 2, 4, and 6, the mean is 4 and the squared deviations sum to 8. The population variance is 8/3; the sample variance is 4. The sample standard deviation is √4 = 2, in the same units as the data.

## Why use n−1?

When the sample mean is estimated from the same observations, deviations around it are constrained to sum to zero. Dividing by n−1 corrects the average squared deviation so it is unbiased for population variance under independent, identically distributed sampling with finite variance. It does not make the sample standard deviation unbiased in general.

Variance and standard deviation are sensitive to outliers because deviations are squared. Compare with [median and quantiles](mean_mode_and_median.html), and see [expectation and variance](expectation_and_variance.html) for the random-variable definitions.

## Understand the units and the denominator

Population variance and sample variance answer different questions. If 2, 4, and 6 are the complete population, their deviations from 4 are −2, 0, and 2. Squared deviations sum to 8; divide by 3 to get population variance 8/3. If these are a sample used to estimate a larger population's variance, the usual estimator divides by n−1 and gives 4. The corresponding standard deviations are √(8/3)≈1.63 and 2. Use the population formula when the complete group is the object of interest; use the sample estimator when drawing an estimate from sampled data under its assumptions.

Variance has squared units, such as hours². Standard deviation takes the square root and returns to the units of the observations. It is not the average absolute distance from the mean, though both measure spread. Because deviations are squared, extreme values have extra influence. For skewed data, report a median and interquartile range alongside or instead of mean and standard deviation. See [mean, median, and mode](mean_mode_and_median.html) and [quantiles](quantiles_and_percentiles.html).

## Try this above

Add a constant to every data point, then multiply every point by the same factor. Check how the mean and spread respond.

If every measurement is converted from metres to centimetres, the mean and standard deviation are multiplied by 100, while variance is multiplied by 10,000. Adding 5 to every value raises the mean by 5 but leaves variance and standard deviation unchanged. These transformation rules are useful for checking both calculations and units. If comparing spread across quantities with different means and units, a coefficient of variation may be considered for positive ratio-scale data, but it becomes unstable near a zero mean and is not suitable for every context.

If the data are a sample, n−1 corrects the expected value of the sample variance under independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance. It does not account for clustering or unequal selection probabilities. If the sample is weighted or the design is complex, a survey-specific estimator may be required. State whether the values describe a complete population or estimate one; this choice determines which denominator is meaningful.
