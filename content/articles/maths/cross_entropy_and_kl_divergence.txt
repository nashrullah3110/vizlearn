title: Cross-Entropy and KL Divergence: A Practical Guide
intro: The loss function almost every classifier is trained with, and the quantity underneath it.

## Quick Context

<a href="entropy_and_information.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Entropy</a> is the average surprise of a spread. The shortest average code length you could reach if you knew the true chances. Cross-entropy asks a harsher question: what does it cost if you build your code for q and the data really comes from p?<p>The answer is always at least H(p). And the excess is the KL divergence. Each sorter trained with "cross-entropy loss" is being pushed to make that excess less.</p>

Three quantities sit in a clean relationship. Entropy H(p) is the irreducible cost of communicating outcomes drawn from p. Cross-entropy H(p, q) is the cost when you use the wrong spread q to encode outcomes that really come from p. KL divergence D(p || q) is the gap: the extra cost caused by with q instead of p. The equation is H(p, q) = H(p) + D(p || q). And since D is always non-negative, the cross-entropy is always at least the entropy.

In sorting, p is the one-hot label (all chance on the correct class) and q is the net's softmax output. H(p) is zero since the label is certain. So the cross-entropy loss equals the KL divergence just. The whole loss is the gap between the net's prediction and the truth. Cutting cross-entropy pushes the net to concentrate its chance on the correct class. It is why the loss drops as the predicted chance for the correct class raises.

KL divergence is not symmetric: D(p || q) and D(q || p) are other counts with other meanings. The forward KL, D(p || q), penalises the net heavily wherever p has mass and q does not. It makes the net cover all the modes of the true spread. The reverse KL, D(q || p), penalises the net for placing mass where p has none. It encourages the net to commit to a single mode. Variational autoencoders use the reverse KL for just this reason.

## The three quantities

H(p) &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;= &minus; &Sigma; p&#7522; log p&#7522;

H(p, q) = &minus; &Sigma; p&#7522; log q&#7522;

KL(p ‖ q) = &Sigma; p&#7522. Log(p&#7522; / q&#7522;) = H(p, q) &minus; H(p)

Read them as costs. Entropy is the unavoidable cost of the randomness in p. Cross-entropy is what you really pay with q. KL is the gap: pure waste, caused by believing the wrong thing.

Since H(p) does not depend on q at all, cutting cross-entropy over your net and cutting KL are the same optimisation. That is the reason the loss is written as cross-entropy still though the quantity anyone cares about is the divergence.

## Measuring how wrong a predicted spread is

Cross-entropy checks two chance spreads: the true one, `p`. And the net's, `q`.

<p class="mono-font">H(p, q) = &minus;&Sigma; p(x) log q(x)</p>

For sorting the true spread is one-hot. All the chance on the correct class. So the sum collapses to a single term:

<p class="mono-font">loss = &minus;log(chance the net gave the correct class)</p>

That is the whole loss function. And its behaviour is easy to read off:

|Predicted probability for the true class|Loss|
|---|---|
|0.99|0.01|
|0.90|0.11|
|0.50|0.69|
|0.10|2.30|
|0.01|4.61|
|0.001|6.91|

The cost grows without bound as the prediction ways zero. A net that is <em>confidently wrong</em> is punished far harder than one that is merely uncertain. It is just the incentive you want when the output is meant to be a chance.

Check with squared error, where being wrong by 1.0 caps the cost at 1.0. That is why cross-entropy trains sorters faster: its gradient stays large where the net is badly wrong, while squared error's gradient shrinks precisely there.

## Entropy, cross-entropy and KL, in one relationship

Three quantities that are constantly confused. And one equation that separates them:

<p class="mono-font">H(p, q) = H(p) + D<sub>KL</sub>(p &#8214; q)</p>

- <strong>H(p)</strong>, entropy: the irreducible uncertainty in the true spread. You cannot do better than this.
- <strong>D<sub>KL</sub>(p‖q)</strong>, KL divergence: the <em>extra</em> cost of with q when the truth is p. Zero when they match, positive otherwise.
- <strong>H(p, q)</strong>, cross-entropy: the total cost. It is the sum of the two.

Since H(p) does not depend on the net, cutting cross-entropy is just cutting KL divergence. Frameworks tune the first since it is simpler to compute. The second is what it means.

The coding reading makes it concrete. Entropy is the average count of bits needed to encode outcomes with an best code for p. Cross-entropy is the average when you use a code designed for q instead. KL divergence is the waste.

## KL divergence is not a distance

<p class="mono-font">D<sub>KL</sub>(p &#8214; q) &ne; D<sub>KL</sub>(q &#8214; p)</p>

The asymmetry is not a technicality. It changes the behaviour of nets built on it.

<strong>Forward KL, D(p‖q)</strong>, is heavily penalised wherever p has mass and q has almost none. Cutting it makes q spread out to cover all the truth does. <em>mode-covering</em>, at the cost of putting chance in places nothing happens.

<strong>Reverse KL, D(q‖p)</strong>, punishes q for putting mass where p has none. Cutting it makes q concentrate on one region and ignore the others &mdash; <em>mode-seeking</em>.

Variational autoencoders minimise reverse KL. It is part of why their samples can be blurry and why they sometimes ignore modes. Max likelihood training is forward KL. It is why language nets trained this way give small but non-zero chance to almost all.

When a symmetric measure is genuinely needed, Jensen-Shannon divergence. The average of both ways against their mixture &mdash; is the standard choice. And its square root is a true metric.

## The loss your sorter really pays

Cross-entropy is entropy plus the cost for with the wrong spread. This separates the two. And shows why KL is not symmetric.

```python-run
import numpy as np

def H(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, float); p = p[p > 0]
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()

def cross_H(p, q):
    p, q = np.asarray(p, float), np.asarray(q, float)
    return -(p * np.log2(q)).sum()

def KL(p, q):
    return cross_H(p, q) - H(p)

p = np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])          # the truth
for label, q in (("q = p (perfect)",  [0.70, 0.20, 0.10]),
                 ("q close",          [0.60, 0.25, 0.15]),
                 ("q wrong",          [0.10, 0.20, 0.70])):
    print("%-16s H(p)=%.4f  H(p,q)=%.4f  KL=%.4f"
          % (label, H(p), cross_H(p, q), KL(p, q)))
print()
print("cross-entropy = entropy + KL. the entropy part is fixed by the data,")
print("so minimising cross-entropy is minimising KL.")
print()
a, b = [0.9, 0.1], [0.5, 0.5]
print("KL is not symmetric:")
print("  KL(a||b) = %.4f" % KL(a, b))
print("  KL(b||a) = %.4f" % KL(b, a))
print()
print("one training example, in nats, the way a framework reports it:")
for true_class_prob in (0.99, 0.9, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01):
    print("  model gave the right class %.2f -> loss %.4f"
          % (true_class_prob, -np.log(true_class_prob)))
```

## Things to try

1. <strong>Read the default.</strong> p is 60/20/15/5 with an entropy of 1.533 bits. q is 40/30/20/10. So the cross-entropy is 1.655 and 0.122 bits per symbol are being wasted.
2. <strong>Close the gap.</strong> Press <strong>Set q Equal To p</strong>. Cross-entropy drops to just the entropy, KL reads 0.000. And no arrangement of q can do better. That is Gibbs' inequality: the divergence is zero just when the two spreads agree all over.
3. <strong>Try to beat it.</strong> Nudge any one q weight away from p. Cross-entropy goes up, never down &mdash; whichever way you move.
4. <strong>Watch the asymmetry.</strong> KL(p ‖ q) and KL(q ‖ p) are other counts for the same pair. Swapping the roles is not a relabelling. It is a other question.
5. <strong>Meet infinity.</strong> Drag <strong>Weight On D</strong> to 0 while p still gives D a 5% chance. The divergence turns infinite: your net has declared impossible something that genuinely happens. And no amount of the rest being right can rescue that.
6. <strong>Use a hard label.</strong> Set the true spread to <em>a hard label</em>. H(p) is 0. So cross-entropy and KL are the same count. And both collapse to &minus;log q(A). The negative log likelihood of the correct class. It is precisely what a sorter's loss computes.

## Why sorters use it

For a single labelled case, p is one-hot: chance 1 on the true class and 0 elsewhere. Each term of the cross-entropy sum vanishes except one, leaving

loss = &minus;log q(correct class)

which is the negative log likelihood. Cutting cross-entropy over a dataset is therefore <a href="maximum_likelihood_estimation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">max likelihood estimation</a>, arrived at from information theory rather than stats. Two other stories about the same formula.

The shape of &minus;log q is what gives the loss its bite: being 99% sure and right costs almost nothing, while being 99% sure and wrong costs a great deal. See <a href="../deep_learning/softmax_and_cross_entropy.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">Softmax and Cross-Entropy</a> for that curve in a network.

## Which way, and why it matters

KL(p ‖ q) &mdash; the "forward" way used in supervised training. Punishes giving low chance to things that really happen. A q that is too narrow gets an enormous cost. So the fitted q tends to spread out and cover all of p's mass. It is <em>mean-seeking</em>.

KL(q ‖ p) &mdash; the "reverse" way. It shows up in variational test time. Punishes putting mass where p has none. So the fitted q tends to hide inside a single mode. It is <em>mode-seeking</em>. Same pair of spreads, opposite behaviour. And the choice is a modelling decision rather than a convention.

## What trips people up

- <strong>A zero in q.</strong> One <span class="mono-font">log 0</span> makes the whole loss infinite. And in floating point it makes it NaN. This is why implementations clamp chances and why you should use the framework's fused softmax-cross-entropy rather than composing your own.
- <strong>Confusing it with a distance.</strong> KL is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality. If you need a true metric, the Jensen-Shannon divergence is the symmetric relative built out of it.
- <strong>Mixing bits and nats.</strong> log&#8322. Gives bits, natural log gives nats. And the ratio is 1.4427. Frameworks report nats; this page reports bits.
- <strong>Trusting perfectly confident labels.</strong> A one-hot target asks the net to drive its output to just 1. It it can just way by inflating logits without limit. Label smoothing exists to stop that.

## Where that leaves you

Cross-entropy H(p, q) is what your beliefs cost when reality is p, entropy H(p) is the part of that cost no net could avoid. And the KL divergence is the gap. The waste that is genuinely your net's fault. KL is never negative and is zero just when q matches p just. So cutting cross-entropy and cutting divergence are the same job. With a one-hot label it collapses to &minus;log of the chance given to the right class. It is why sorting loss, negative log likelihood and max likelihood are three names for one thing. It is not symmetric. And a single zero in q where p is non-zero sends it to infinity.

## With it correctly in code

Two build details cause most of the problems in practice.

<strong>Pass logits, not chances.</strong> Computing a softmax yourself and then taking a log is numerically fragile: large logits overflow the exponential, small chances underflow before the log sees them. Frameworks provide fused, stable versions:

```python
import torch.nn as nn

loss = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()        # expects raw logits, applies log-softmax
loss = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()       # binary version, also expects logits
```

`nn.CrossEntropyLoss` mixes `log_softmax` and negative log-likelihood in one numerically stable step. With `nn.NLLLoss` after a manual softmax is the classic double-softmax bug: the net trains slowly and never quite works, with no error message.

<strong>Guard the log.</strong> If you must write it yourself, add a small epsilon: `-log(p + 1e-12)`. Without it, a single prediction of just zero gives infinity and then `NaN` for the whole batch.

Two variants worth knowing: <strong>binary cross-entropy</strong> for two-class and multi-label problems (one sigmoid per label, not a softmax). And <strong>categorical cross-entropy</strong> for mutually exclusive classes. Choosing softmax for a multi-label problem is a common modelling error. Softmax forces the outputs to sum to 1. It is wrong when a few labels can be true at once.

## Where KL divergence turns up

- <strong>Variational autoencoders</strong> add a KL term pulling the learned latent spread towards a standard normal. It is what makes the latent space smooth enough to sample from.
- <strong>Knowledge distillation</strong> cuts the KL between a large teacher's output spread and a small student's. It transfers far more information per case than hard labels.
- <strong>Reinforcement learning from human feedback</strong> uses a KL cost to keep a fine-tuned policy close to the first net, stopping it from drifting into degenerate text that games the reward.
- <strong>Drift detection</strong> checks the spread of production inputs against the training spread. A rising KL or PSI is the alarm.
- <strong>Bayesian test time</strong> approximates an intractable posterior by cutting KL to a tractable family. The "variational" in variational test time.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Why not use score as the loss?</strong> Score is a step function of the predictions. So its gradient is zero almost all over. There is nothing for gradient descent to follow. Train on cross-entropy, test on score.

<strong>Can cross-entropy be zero?</strong> Just if the net puts chance 1 on the correct class. In practice it ways but never reaches zero.

<strong>What if my loss is `NaN`?</strong> Almost always a `log(0)`, an overflow in the exponential. Or a learning rate high enough to send weights to infinity. Use the fused loss functions and check the learning rate.

<strong>Why is my loss around 2.3 at the start?</strong> Since ln(10) = 2.30. A net predicting uniformly across 10 classes. Seeing just that at setup is a healthy sign. Not seeing it suggests a bug.

<strong>Is KL divergence ever negative?</strong> No, never. It is zero just when the spreads are identical.

<strong>What is label smoothing doing to this?</strong> It replaces the one-hot target with a slightly softened spread. So the net is no longer pushed towards infinite confidence. It often improves tuning at a small cost in training score.

## Recap in one screen

- Cross-entropy is `-log(chance given to the correct class)`. And it punishes confident mistakes without limit.
- Cross-entropy = entropy + KL divergence. So cutting one cuts the other.
- KL is asymmetric: forward KL covers all the modes, reverse KL concentrates on one.
- Always pass logits to the framework's fused loss. Never hand-roll softmax followed by log.
- KL powers VAEs, distillation, RLHF costs, drift detection and variational test time.
