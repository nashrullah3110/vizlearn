title: Exponentials
intro: Growth proportional to size &mdash; slow to start, then impossible to ignore.

## Before the details

In <span class="mono-font">y = b^x</span> the variable sits in the <em>exponent</em>. Each step of 1 in x <strong>multiplies</strong> y by b, rather than adding to it. That single difference is what separates exponential from linear behaviour.

## Three Regimes, Set by the Base

- <strong style="color: var(--c-good)">b &gt; 1 &mdash; growth.</strong> Each step multiplies up. Compound interest, viral spread, unchecked model divergence.
- <strong>b = 1 &mdash; flat.</strong> 1 to any power is 1. The boundary between the other two.
- <strong style="color: var(--c-b)">0 &lt; b &lt; 1 &mdash; decay.</strong> Each step multiplies down, approaching zero without ever arriving. Learning-rate schedules, radioactive decay, the vanishing gradient.

Slide the base across 1 and watch the curve flip from climbing to falling.

## Exponential Always Wins Eventually

Keep the polynomial comparison on and look at the left of the plot: <span class="mono-font">x&sup3;</span> is far ahead. Now slide x rightward. There is a crossover point, and after it <span class="mono-font">2^x</span> leaves <span class="mono-font">x&sup3;</span> hopelessly behind.

This holds for <em>any</em> polynomial and any base above 1 &mdash; exponential growth always overtakes eventually. It is the reason exponential-time algorithms are considered intractable no matter how fast the hardware, and the reason a "small" compounding effect is never actually small.

## Doubling Time and Half-Life

A defining feature of exponentials: the time to double is <strong>constant</strong>. It takes just as long to go from 1 to 2 as from a million to two million. The panel computes this live &mdash; at base 2 it is exactly 1 step, at base 1.1 about 7.3 steps.

## Why e Is Special

Set the base to <strong>e &asymp; 2.71828</strong>. This is the base at which the curve's <em>slope equals its own height</em> at every point:

No other base has this property, which is why e turns up throughout calculus and why <span class="mono-font">exp</span> is the exponential in almost every ML formula &mdash; softmax, sigmoid, Gaussian densities and exponential learning-rate decay all use it.

## Growth that feeds on itself

Linear growth adds the same amount each step. Exponential growth <em>multiplies</em> by the same factor each step, so the amount added grows too.

<p class="mono-font">f(x) = a &times; b&#7530;</p>

`a` is the starting value and `b` is the growth factor. b &gt; 1 grows, 0 &lt; b &lt; 1 decays.

The difference is easy to underestimate. Add 10 a day starting from 100 and after 30 days you have 400. Multiply by 1.1 a day and you have 1,745.

|Day|Linear (+10)|Exponential (&times;1.1)|
|---|---|---|
|0|100|100|
|10|200|259|
|20|300|673|
|30|400|1,745|
|60|700|30,448|

The exponential column starts slower and ends in a different universe. That is the shape behind compound interest, viral spread, and the growth of model sizes over the last decade.

## The number e, and why it is the default base

e &asymp; 2.71828 is the growth factor you get from compounding continuously &mdash; the limit of (1 + 1/n)&#8319; as n grows.

Its special property is the reason it is everywhere in calculus:

<p class="mono-font">d/dx e&#7530; = e&#7530;</p>

The function is its own derivative. No other base does this (any other base picks up a constant factor of ln(b)), and that is what makes e the natural choice for anything involving rates of change &mdash; which is to say, anything involving optimisation.

Its inverse is the natural logarithm: e<sup>ln(x)</sup> = x, and ln(e&#7530;) = x.

## Where exponentials appear in machine learning

<strong>The sigmoid.</strong> `1 / (1 + e^-x)` squashes any real number into (0, 1). The exponential is what makes it smooth, and its derivative &mdash; `s(x)(1 &minus; s(x))` &mdash; is what makes it trainable.

<strong>Softmax.</strong> `e^zi / Σ e^zj` turns a vector of scores into probabilities that sum to 1. Exponentiating first is what guarantees positivity and exaggerates the differences between scores, so a small lead in logits becomes a large lead in probability.

<strong>Learning rate decay.</strong> `lr = lr0 × e^(-kt)` reduces the step size smoothly as training proceeds.

<strong>Exponential moving averages.</strong> Momentum, Adam and batch normalisation all keep a running average that weights recent values more heavily, with the weights falling off exponentially.

<strong>Radial basis kernels.</strong> `e^(-γ||x-y||²)` measures similarity that falls away rapidly with distance, and is what gives an RBF SVM its local behaviour.

## Decay, and half-lives

With 0 &lt; b &lt; 1, or a negative exponent, the same shape runs downhill: fast at first, then a long thinning tail that never quite reaches zero.

The half-life is the time taken to halve. It is constant, which is the defining property of exponential decay: whatever the current amount, half of it disappears in the same interval.

That is exactly the behaviour of an exponential moving average with decay 0.9 &mdash; a value from ten steps ago carries 0.9&#185;&#8304; &asymp; 35% of its original weight, and from a hundred steps ago effectively none. Choosing the decay rate is choosing how long the memory is, and it is why Adam's default of 0.999 for the second moment corresponds to a window of roughly a thousand steps.

2. <strong>Watch the crossover with x&sup3;.</strong> Note how far right it happens &mdash; exponential growth hides for a surprisingly long time before it dominates.
3. <strong>Set the base to 0.5.</strong> Now it is halving: 1, 0.5, 0.25&hellip; approaching zero but never reaching it. This is exactly a learning-rate decay schedule.
4. <strong>Tick log-scale y.</strong> The exponential becomes a perfectly straight line &mdash; that is the signature of exponential data, and how you spot it in a real chart.
5. <strong>Try base 1.05</strong> (5% growth). It looks nearly flat, yet the doubling-time panel says about 14 steps &mdash; compound interest in one number.

## Where that leaves you

Exponentials multiply rather than add, so they have a constant doubling time and eventually beat any polynomial. Bases above 1 explode, bases below 1 decay toward zero, and base e is the one whose slope equals its value &mdash; which is why it underlies softmax, sigmoid and every decay schedule you will configure.

## Overflow, and the softmax trick

Exponentials grow fast enough to break floating-point arithmetic. `e^1000` is infinity as far as a 64-bit float is concerned, and once an infinity enters a calculation the result is `NaN`.

This is a real problem in softmax, where the inputs are unbounded logits. The standard fix is to subtract the maximum before exponentiating:

```python
import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    z = z - np.max(z)          # shift: does not change the result
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

softmax(np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.]))   # works
np.exp(np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.]))    # inf, inf, inf
```

Subtracting a constant from every logit leaves the softmax unchanged, because the constant cancels between numerator and denominator &mdash; but it moves the largest exponent to `e^0 = 1`, safely inside range. Every framework does this internally, which is one reason you should pass logits to a loss function rather than probabilities you computed yourself.

The mirror problem is underflow: `e^-1000` becomes exactly 0, and a subsequent log gives &minus;infinity. Working in log space throughout, with `logsumexp`, avoids both.

## Reading exponential growth honestly

Three habits stop exponential quantities from misleading you.

<strong>Use a log scale.</strong> On a logarithmic y-axis, exponential growth is a straight line, and a change in the growth rate becomes visible as a change in slope. On a linear axis everything before the last few periods looks flat and identical.

<strong>Quote the doubling time.</strong> "Growing 7% a month" is abstract; "doubling every ten months" is not. The rule of 72 gives it quickly: divide 72 by the percentage growth rate to get the doubling time.

<strong>Remember nothing grows exponentially for long.</strong> Real processes hit a limit and flatten into an S-curve &mdash; a logistic function, which is the sigmoid again. Extrapolating an exponential past that bend is how forecasts become absurd.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Why is e used instead of 2 or 10?</strong> Because e&#7530; is its own derivative, which makes every calculus expression cleaner. Other bases work and pick up a constant factor.

<strong>What is the difference between exponential and polynomial growth?</strong> A polynomial like x&sup2; grows by a shrinking proportion; an exponential grows by a constant proportion, and eventually overtakes any polynomial.

<strong>Why does softmax use exponentials?</strong> They are positive for any input, which is needed for probabilities, and they amplify differences between scores in a smooth, differentiable way.

<strong>What causes `NaN` in my training?</strong> Very often an overflow in an exponential, or a `log(0)`. Numerically stable implementations of softmax and cross-entropy exist precisely for this.

<strong>What is an exponential moving average?</strong> A running average weighting recent values more heavily, with older values fading geometrically. It is what momentum and Adam are built on.

<strong>How do I fit an exponential relationship?</strong> Take the log of the target and fit a straight line &mdash; log(y) = log(a) + x&thinsp;log(b) is linear in x.

## Recap in one screen

- Exponential growth multiplies by a constant factor each step, so the increments grow too.
- e is the base whose function is its own derivative, which is why calculus prefers it.
- Sigmoid, softmax, learning-rate decay, momentum and RBF kernels are all built on exponentials.
- Exponentials overflow easily &mdash; subtract the maximum before exponentiating, or work in log space.
- Plot on a log scale and quote a doubling time; nothing grows exponentially for ever.
