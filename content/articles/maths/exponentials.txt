title: Exponentials
intro: An exponential changes by the same factor over equal steps, producing growth or decay whose rate depends on the base.

## Read the base and exponent

In f(x) = bˣ, the base b is the factor applied for each one-unit increase in x. When b > 1, the function grows; when 0 < b < 1, it decays. The point at x = 0 is always (0, 1), since b⁰ = 1. The [logarithm lesson](logarithms.html) reverses this relationship.

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="b to the x plus one equals b times b to the x">bˣ⁺¹ = b · bˣ</div>

For b = 2, each step right doubles the value: 2⁰ = 1, 2¹ = 2, 2² = 4. Each step left halves it. The curve does not cross zero; it approaches zero as x decreases without bound.

## Continuous growth and the base e

The natural exponential eˣ is special because its derivative is itself. In a continuous growth model, dy/dt = ky has solution y(t) = y₀eᵏᵗ. Positive k gives growth; negative k gives decay. The doubling time for k > 0 is ln(2)/k, and the half-life for k < 0 is ln(2)/|k|.

Exponential growth eventually outpaces any fixed power xⁿ, but the phrase “eventually” matters: over a limited range, a polynomial can be larger. Real systems also have constraints, so indefinite exponential growth is rarely a realistic long-term model. See [the normal distribution](the_normal_distribution.html) for a contrasting bell-shaped model and [logarithms](logarithms.html) for linearising multiplicative change.

## Try this above

Change the base from below 1 to above 1. Compare equal horizontal steps and observe the constant multiplication factor; then compare the curve with its inverse.
