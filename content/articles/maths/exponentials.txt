title: Exponentials
intro: Growth proportional to size &mdash; slow to start, then impossible to ignore.

## Before the details

In <span class="mono-font">y = b^x</span> the var sits in the <em>exponent</em>. Each step of 1 in x <strong>multiplies</strong> y by b, rather than adding to it. That single gap is what separates exponential from linear behaviour.

Exponentials start deceptively slow. Double a penny each day and after ten days you have about five dollars. After twenty days you have five thousand. After thirty days, five million. The curve is flat for a long time, then suddenly vertical. That shape is why exponential processes catch people off guard: by the time the growth is visible, it is now enormous.

The base e (&asymp; 2.718) is the natural base for exponentials, not since of a convention but since of calculus. The function e^x is its own derivative: the rate of change of e^x at any point is e^x. That property makes it the natural net for continuous growth and decay. And it is why the exponential function appears in the loss landscape, in probability distributions. And in the softmax function that turns raw scores into probabilities.

In machine learning, exponentials appear in at least three places. The softmax function uses e^x to ensure all outputs are positive before normalising. Learning rate schedules multiply the rate by a constant less than 1 each epoch. It is exponential decay. The vanishing gradient problem is exponential decay of gradients through layers. There each layer multiplies by a factor less than 1.

Grasp exponentials means grasp the gap between additive and multiplicative processes. Addition is linear: 1, 2, 3, 4. Multiplication is exponential: 1, 2, 4, 8. Most of the surprising behaviours in training &mdash; exploding gradients, diminishing learning rates, the sharpness of the softmax &mdash; follow from this distinction.

## Three Regimes, Set by the Base

- <strong style="color: var(--c-good)">b &gt; 1 &mdash; growth.</strong> Each step multiplies up. Compound interest, viral spread, unchecked net divergence.
- <strong>b = 1 &mdash; flat.</strong> 1 to any power is 1. The edge between the other two.
- <strong style="color: var(--c-b)">0 &lt; b &lt; 1 &mdash; decay.</strong> Each step multiplies down, approaching zero without ever arriving. Learning-rate schedules, radioactive decay, the vanishing gradient.

Slide the base across 1 and watch the curve flip from climbing to falling.

## Exponential Always Wins Eventually

Keep the polynomial comparison on and look at the left of the plot: <span class="mono-font">x&sup3;</span> is far ahead. Now slide x rightward. There is a crossover point. And after it <span class="mono-font">2^x</span> leaves <span class="mono-font">x&sup3;</span> hopelessly behind.

This holds for <em>any</em> polynomial and any base above 1 &mdash; exponential growth always overtakes eventually. It is the reason exponential-time algorithms are considered intractable no matter how fast the hardware. And the reason a "small" compounding effect is never really small.

## Doubling Time and Half-Life

A setting feature of exponentials: the time to double is <strong>constant</strong>. It takes just as long to go from 1 to 2 as from a million to two million. The panel computes this live &mdash; at base 2 it is just 1 step, at base 1.1 about 7.3 steps.

## Why e Is Special

Set the base to <strong>e &asymp; 2.71828</strong>. This is the base at which the curve's <em>slope equals its own height</em> at each point:

No other base has this property. It is why e turns up throughout calculus and why <span class="mono-font">exp</span> is the exponential in almost each ML formula &mdash; softmax, sigmoid, Gaussian densities and exponential learning-rate decay all use it.

## Growth that feeds on it

Linear growth adds the same amount each step. Exponential growth <em>multiplies</em> by the same factor each step. So the amount added grows too.

<p class="mono-font">f(x) = a &times; b&#739;</p>

`a` is the starting value and `b` is the growth factor. b &gt; 1 grows, 0 &lt; b &lt; 1 decays.

The gap is easy to underestimate. Add 10 a day starting from 100 and after 30 days you have 400. Multiply by 1.1 a day and you have 1,745.

|Day|Linear (+10)|Exponential (&times;1.1)|
|---|---|---|
|0|100|100|
|10|200|259|
|20|300|673|
|30|400|1,745|
|60|700|30,448|

The exponential column starts slower and ends in a other universe. That is the shape behind compound interest, viral spread. And the growth of net sizes over the last decade.

## The count e, and why it is the default base

e &asymp; 2.71828 is the growth factor you get from compounding continuously &mdash; the limit of (1 + 1/n)&#8319. As n grows.

Its special property is the reason it is all over in calculus:

<p class="mono-font">d/dx e&#739; = e&#739;</p>

The function is its own derivative. No other base does this (any other base picks up a constant factor of ln(b)). And that is what makes e the natural choice for anything involving rates of change &mdash; which is to say, anything involving optimisation.

Its inverse is the natural logarithm: e<sup>ln(x)</sup> = x, and ln(e&#739;) = x.

## Where exponentials appear in machine learning

<strong>The sigmoid.</strong> `1 / (1 + e^-x)` squashes any real count into (0, 1). The exponential is what makes it smooth. And its derivative &mdash; `s(x)(1 &minus; s(x))` &mdash; is what makes it trainable.

<strong>Softmax.</strong> `e^zi / Σ e^zj` turns a vector of scores into probabilities that sum to 1. Exponentiating first is what guarantees positivity and exaggerates the gaps between scores. So a small lead in logits turns a large lead in probability.

<strong>Learning rate decay.</strong> `lr = lr0 × e^(-kt)` reduces the step size smoothly as training proceeds.

<strong>Exponential moving averages.</strong> Momentum, Adam and batch normalisation all keep a running average that weights recent vals more heavily, with the weights falling off exponentially.

<strong>Radial basis kernels.</strong> `e^(-γ||x-y||²)` measures similarity that falls away rapidly with distance. And is what gives an RBF SVM its local behaviour.

## Decay, and half-lives

With 0 &lt; b &lt; 1. Or a negative exponent, the same shape runs downhill: fast at first, then a long thinning tail that never quite reaches zero.

The half-life is the time taken to halve. It is constant, which is the setting property of exponential decay: whatever the current amount, half of it disappears in the same interval.

That is just the behaviour of an exponential moving average with decay 0.9 &mdash; a value from ten steps ago carries 0.9&#185;&#8304. &asymp; 35% of its first weight. And from a hundred steps ago effectively none. Choosing the decay rate is choosing how long the memory is. And it is why Adam's default of 0.999 for the second moment corresponds to a window of roughly a thousand steps.

## Growth that outruns all

Linear, quadratic and exponential growth on the same table, plus the decay form used for learning-rate schedules.

```python-run
import numpy as np

print("%5s %10s %12s %18s" % ("n", "10n", "n^2", "2^n"))
for n in (1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 60):
    print("%5d %10d %12d %18d" % (n, 10 * n, n ** 2, 2 ** n))
print()
print("at n=5 the linear term is still the biggest of the three.")
print("by n=20 the exponential is %d times the quadratic." % (2 ** 20 // 400))
print("by n=60 nothing else is on the chart.")
print()
print("e is the base where the growth rate equals the value:")
h = 1e-7
for x in (0.0, 1.0, 2.0):
    slope = (np.exp(x + h) - np.exp(x)) / h
    print("  at x=%.0f  e^x = %10.6f   d/dx e^x = %10.6f" % (x, np.exp(x), slope))
print()
print("decay is the same shape with a negative rate:")
lr0, half_life = 0.1, 10.0
for epoch in (0, 10, 20, 30, 50):
    lr = lr0 * np.exp(-np.log(2) * epoch / half_life)
    print("  epoch %2d  lr = %.6f" % (epoch, lr))
print()
print("compounding: 5%% a year for 40 years multiplies by %.2f" % (1.05 ** 40))
```

## Where that leaves you

Exponentials multiply rather than add. So they have a constant doubling time and eventually beat any polynomial. Bases above 1 explode, bases below 1 decay toward zero. And base e is the one whose slope equals its value &mdash; which is why it underlies softmax, sigmoid and each decay schedule you will set up.

## Overflow, and the softmax trick

Exponentials grow fast enough to break floating-point arithmetic. `e^1000` is infinity as far as a 64-bit float is concerned. And once an infinity enters a math the result is `NaN`.

This is a real problem in softmax, where the inputs are unbounded logits. The standard fix is to subtract the max before exponentiating:

```python
import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    z = z - np.max(z)          # shift: does not change the result
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

softmax(np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.]))   # works
np.exp(np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.]))    # inf, inf, inf
```

Subtracting a constant from each logit leaves the softmax unchanged, since the constant cancels between numerator and denominator &mdash; but it moves the largest exponent to `e^0 = 1`, safely inside range. Each framework does this internally. It is one reason you should pass logits to a loss function rather than probabilities you computed yourself.

The mirror problem is underflow: `e^-1000` turns just 0. And a later log gives &minus;infinity. Working in log space throughout, with `logsumexp`, avoids both.

## Reading exponential growth honestly

Three habits stop exponential quantities from misleading you.

<strong>Use a log scale.</strong> On a logarithmic y-axis, exponential growth is a straight line. And a change in the growth rate turns visible as a change in slope. On a linear axis all before the last few periods looks flat and identical.

<strong>Quote the doubling time.</strong> "Growing 7% a month" is abstract. "doubling each ten months" is not. The rule of 72 gives it quickly: divide 72 by the percentage growth rate to get the doubling time.

<strong>Remember nothing grows exponentially for long.</strong> Real processes hit a limit and flatten into an S-curve &mdash; a logistic function. It is the sigmoid again. Extrapolating an exponential past that bend is how forecasts turn absurd.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Why is e used instead of 2 or 10?</strong> Since e&#739. Is its own derivative, which makes each calculus expression cleaner. Other bases work and pick up a constant factor.

<strong>What is the gap between exponential and polynomial growth?</strong> A polynomial like x&sup2; grows by a shrinking proportion. An exponential grows by a constant proportion. And eventually overtakes any polynomial.

<strong>Why does softmax use exponentials?</strong> They are positive for any input. It is needed for probabilities. And they amplify gaps between scores in a smooth, differentiable way.

<strong>What causes `NaN` in my training?</strong> Very often an overflow in an exponential. Or a `log(0)`. Numerically stable implementations of softmax and cross-entropy exist precisely for this.

<strong>What is an exponential moving average?</strong> A running average weighting recent vals more heavily, with older vals fading geometrically. It is what momentum and Adam are built on.

<strong>How do I fit an exponential relationship?</strong> Take the log of the target and fit a straight line &mdash; log(y) = log(a) + x&thinsp;log(b) is linear in x.

## Recap in one screen

- Exponential growth multiplies by a constant factor each step. So the increments grow too.
- e is the base whose function is its own derivative. It is why calculus prefers it.
- Sigmoid, softmax, learning-rate decay, momentum and RBF kernels are all built on exponentials.
- Exponentials overflow easily &mdash; subtract the max before exponentiating. Or work in log space.
- Plot on a log scale and quote a doubling time. Nothing grows exponentially for ever.
