title: The Normal Distribution
intro: Two numbers describe the whole curve &mdash; and the central limit theorem explains why it is unavoidable.

## Quick Context

The <strong>normal</strong> (or Gaussian) spread is the familiar bell curve: symmetric, single-peaked, thin-tailed. It is fully named by just two counts. The mean &mu; sets where it sits. And the standard deviation &sigma; sets how wide it is.

The reason the normal spread appears all over is the central limit theorem. When you add many free random vars together, their sum converges to a normal spread no matter what the individual vars look like. Heights are the sum of many genetic and environmental factors. Measurement errors are the sum of many small disturbances. Stock returns over a period are the sum of many trades. In each case, the individual contributions can follow any spread. But their aggregate is about normal.

The standard normal has &mu; = 0 and &sigma; = 1. Any normal var can be standardised by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation: z = (x &minus; &mu;) / &sigma;. That z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. It makes vals from other scales comparable. A z-score of 2.0 means the same thing whether the first data was heights in centimetres or exam scores out of 100.

In machine learning, the normal spread appears in weight setup, in the assumption behind linear regression's residuals, in Gaussian naive Bayes. And in the reparameterisation trick used by variational autoencoders. Batch normalisation forces each layer's outputs toward a standard normal. And the Gaussian kernel in SVMs measures similarity as a function of distance.

The 68-95-99.7 rule gives a quick mental net: about 68% of vals fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two. And 99.7% within three. A value more than three standard deviations from the mean is rare enough to flag as an outlier in many applications.

## Two Knobs, Nothing Else

- <strong style="color: var(--c-b)">&mu;</strong> slides the curve left and right without changing its shape.
- <strong style="color: var(--accent-primary)">&sigma;</strong> stretches or squeezes it. Watch the peak <em>drop</em> as you widen it. The total area must always equal 1. So a wider curve has to be shorter.

That fixed unit area is what makes it a chance density: area under a stretch of the curve <em>is</em> the chance of landing in that range.

## The 68–95–99.7 Rule

These percentages hold for <em>each</em> normal spread, whatever &mu; and &sigma; are. Cycle the region selector and watch the shaded area match. The app integrates the curve numerically rather than quoting the constants.

The real use is spotting anomalies: beyond 3&sigma; you are in the outermost 0.3% of outcomes. It is why quality control and anomaly detection use it as a threshold.

## Z-scores Put All on One Scale

A z-score restates a value as "how many standard deviations from the mean". It makes incomparable quantities comparable. A height and an exam score both turn pure position. Setting &mu; = 0 and &sigma; = 1 gives the <strong>standard normal</strong>. It is what each stat-based table means. And what feature standardisation turns your data into.

## Why It Appears All over

The <strong>central limit theorem</strong>: when you add up many small free random effects, their total tends toward a normal spread <em>no matter how the individual effects were distributed</em>. Height is the sum of many genetic and environmental nudges. Measurement error is the sum of many tiny disturbances. Hence bells all over.

Press <strong>Sample 500</strong> a few times and watch the histogram converge onto the curve. In machine learning this shows up as weight setup (drawn from a normal), Gaussian noise in diffusion nets. And the assumption behind least-squares regression.

## When Not to Assume It

Normal tails are very thin &mdash; a 5&sigma; event should in effect never happen. Financial returns, city sizes and word frequencies have far <em>fatter</em> tails. So assuming normality there badly underestimates extreme events. The 2008 financial crisis is the textbook case of trusting a bell curve where none existed.

## The shape that keeps appearing

The normal spread is the symmetric bell curve, set fully by two counts: the mean &mu; (where the peak sits) and the standard deviation &sigma; (how wide it is).

Its most useful property is the empirical rule. It turns &sigma; into a statement about proportions:

|Range|Share of the data|
|---|---|
|&mu; &plusmn; 1&sigma;|68%|
|&mu; &plusmn; 2&sigma;|95%|
|&mu; &plusmn; 3&sigma;|99.7%|

So for adult heights with a mean of 170cm and &sigma; of 10cm: about two thirds of people are between 160 and 180, 95% between 150 and 190. And someone at 200cm is a genuine three-sigma rarity &mdash; about 1 in 700.

That is the real value of the curve. Two counts, and you can answer "how unusual is this?" for any value.

## The z-score

A z-score turns any value into "how many standard deviations from the mean":

<p class="mono-font">z = (x &minus; &mu;) / &sigma;</p>

A test score of 85 where the mean is 70 and &sigma; is 10 gives z = 1.5. One and a half standard deviations above average, better than about 93% of the group.

Standardising this way makes other scales comparable: a z of 1.5 means the same thing whether the underlying var is heights, salaries or reaction times. That is just what `StandardScaler` does to each column before a distance-based net sees it.

## Why it appears so often: the central limit theorem

The reason the bell curve shows up all over is not that nature prefers it. It is a math-based result: <strong>the sum or average of many free contributions tends towards a normal spread, whatever the shape of the individual contributions</strong>.

Roll one die and the outcomes are flat. Roll ten and add them. And the totals form a bell. Human height is the sum of many small genetic and environmental effects. Measurement error is the sum of many small disturbances. Both end up normal for the same reason.

Two consequences for real work:

- <strong>Sample means are often distributed</strong> still when the underlying data is not. It is why confidence intervals and t-tests work on skewed data given a reasonable sample size.
- <strong>Individual measurements are often not normal.</strong> Incomes, city sizes and response times are famously right-skewed. And treating them as normal gives bad intervals and worse thresholds.

The mistake to avoid is assuming normality rather than checking it. A histogram, or a Q-Q plot, settles it in seconds.

## Where it is used in machine learning

- <strong>Standardisation.</strong> Subtracting the mean and dividing by &sigma; is the standard preprocessing step for linear nets, SVMs, KNN and neural networks.
- <strong>Weight setup.</strong> Networks start with weights drawn from a normal spread, scaled by the layer size (He, Xavier).
- <strong>Assumed errors.</strong> Linear regression's test time. Confidence intervals, p-vals &mdash; assumes often distributed residuals. The predictions them do not.
- <strong>Gaussian mixtures</strong> net data as a few overlapping bells, giving soft cluster membership.
- <strong>Anomaly detection.</strong> Flag anything beyond three sigma, when the normality assumption holds.
- <strong>Noise.</strong> Gaussian noise is the default in augmentation, differential privacy and diffusion nets.

## Exploration guide

1. <strong>Slide &mu;.</strong> The curve translates. Its shape never changes.
2. <strong>Slide &sigma; wider</strong> and watch the peak fall to compensate. Fixed area in action.
3. <strong>Cycle &plusmn;1&sigma;, &plusmn;2&sigma;, &plusmn;3&sigma;</strong> and confirm 68 / 95 / 99.7 for any &mu; and &sigma; you choose.
4. <strong>Sample 20.</strong> The histogram looks nothing like a bell &mdash; small samples are deceptive. Now sample 500 repeatedly and watch it lock on.
5. <strong>Set a custom range beyond 3&sigma;</strong> and read how vanishingly small the chance turns.

## Where that leaves you

Two params describe the whole curve, area equals chance. And 68/95/99.7 holds universally. The central limit theorem is why the shape is so common. But its thin tails mean it is the wrong net whenever extreme events really matter.

## When the data is not normal

Assuming a bell curve for skewed data has predictable consequences: thresholds set at three sigma fire far too often, confidence intervals are wrong. And nets that assume symmetry step by step under-predict the tail.

Three replies, in order of preference:

<strong>Transform.</strong> A log transform straightens most right-skewed data &mdash; incomes, populations, counts. The Box-Cox and Yeo-Johnson transforms transfer this and can be fitted automatically.

<strong>Use a spread that fits.</strong> Poisson for counts, exponential for waiting times, log-normal for anything multiplicative, Student's t when the tails are heavier than normal.

<strong>Stop assuming.</strong> Rank-based ways, quantile regression and bootstrapping make no distributional assumption at all. The bootstrap in particular is a general-purpose way to get a confidence interval from data of any shape: resample with replacement a few thousand times and read the interval off the resulting spread.

How to check quickly: plot a histogram, then a Q-Q plot. If the points fall on the diagonal, normality is a reasonable approximation. Formal tests such as Shapiro-Wilk exist but reject almost any large real dataset, since no real data is just normal.

## Two relatives worth knowing

<strong>The log-normal spread</strong> describes a var whose <em>logarithm</em> is normal. It arises whenever effects multiply rather than add &mdash; incomes, file sizes, stock prices. It is right-skewed and strictly positive. It fits a great deal of real data that people mistakenly net as normal.

<strong>Student's t spread</strong> looks like a normal curve with heavier tails. It is what you use for confidence intervals from small samples. There the extra tail weight honestly reflects the uncertainty in estimating &sigma; from few readings. As the sample grows it converges to the normal.

## Measure the 68-95-99.7 rule

Half a million draws, then count how many land inside one, two and three standard deviations. The rule is not a rule of thumb.

```python-run
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(loc=100, scale=15, size=500_000)

print("normal(mean=100, sd=15), 500,000 draws")
print("  measured mean %.3f, sd %.3f" % (x.mean(), x.std()))
print()
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    inside = (np.abs(x - 100) < k * 15).mean()
    print("  within %d sd (%.0f to %.0f): %6.3f" % (k, 100 - k * 15, 100 + k * 15, inside))
print()
print("the 68-95-99.7 rule is not a rule of thumb, it is the area under the curve.")
print()
print("a crude histogram:")
edges = np.arange(55, 150, 10)
counts = np.histogram(x, bins=edges)[0]
for lo, c in zip(edges, counts):
    print("  %3d-%3d %s" % (lo, lo + 10, "#" * int(60 * c / counts.max())))
```

## Questions people ask

<strong>Does my data need to be normal for machine learning?</strong> Mostly no. Trees, forests and boosting do not care. Linear nets' <em>test time</em> assumes normal residuals, not normal features. Distance-based nets care about scale, which standardisation fixes no matter shape.

<strong>What is the gap between standardisation and normalisation?</strong> Standardising subtracts the mean and divides by &sigma;, giving mean 0 and &sigma; 1. Scaling often means rescaling to a fixed range such as [0, 1]. The words are used loosely. Say which you mean.

<strong>Is the mean always at the peak?</strong> For a normal spread the mean, median and mode coincide. For skewed data they split. And the gap tells you the way of the skew.

<strong>How many samples until the central limit theorem applies?</strong> The usual rule of thumb is 30. But heavily skewed data needs far more. Simulate it if the answer matters.

<strong>What is a three-sigma event?</strong> A value more than three standard deviations from the mean. About 0.3% of a normal spread. In markets and other heavy-tailed settings they happen far more often than the normal net predicts.

<strong>Why do neural network weights start often distributed?</strong> Since a symmetric, zero-centred spread breaks symmetry between neurons while keeping the initial outputs in a sensible range.

## Recap in one screen

- Two counts, the mean and the standard deviation, describe the whole curve.
- 68%, 95% and 99.7% fall within one, two and three standard deviations.
- A z-score expresses any value as a count of standard deviations, making scales comparable.
- The central limit theorem explains its ubiquity: sums and averages tend to normal.
- Individual measurements are often skewed &mdash; check before assuming. And transform or use a better-fitting spread.
