title: The Normal Distribution
intro: Two numbers describe the whole curve &mdash; and the central limit theorem explains why it is unavoidable.

## Quick Context

The <strong>normal</strong> (or Gaussian) distribution is the familiar bell curve: symmetric, single-peaked, thin-tailed. It is completely specified by just two numbers &mdash; the mean &mu; sets where it sits, and the standard deviation &sigma; sets how wide it is.

## Two Knobs, Nothing Else

- <strong style="color: var(--c-b)">&mu;</strong> slides the curve left and right without changing its shape.
- <strong style="color: var(--accent-primary)">&sigma;</strong> stretches or squeezes it. Watch the peak <em>drop</em> as you widen it &mdash; the total area must always equal 1, so a wider curve has to be shorter.

That fixed unit area is what makes it a probability density: area under a stretch of the curve <em>is</em> the probability of landing in that range.

## The 68–95–99.7 Rule

These percentages hold for <em>every</em> normal distribution, whatever &mu; and &sigma; are. Cycle the region selector and watch the shaded area match &mdash; the app integrates the curve numerically rather than quoting the constants.

The practical use is spotting anomalies: beyond 3&sigma; you are in the outermost 0.3% of outcomes, which is why quality control and anomaly detection use it as a threshold.

## Z-scores Put Everything on One Scale

A z-score restates a value as "how many standard deviations from the mean". It makes incomparable quantities comparable &mdash; a height and an exam score both become pure position. Setting &mu; = 0 and &sigma; = 1 gives the <strong>standard normal</strong>, which is what every statistical table refers to, and what feature standardisation converts your data into.

## Why It Appears Everywhere

The <strong>central limit theorem</strong>: when you add up many small independent random effects, their total tends toward a normal distribution <em>regardless of how the individual effects were distributed</em>. Height is the sum of many genetic and environmental nudges; measurement error is the sum of many tiny disturbances. Hence bells everywhere.

Press <strong>Sample 500</strong> a few times and watch the histogram converge onto the curve. In machine learning this shows up as weight initialisation (drawn from a normal), Gaussian noise in diffusion models, and the assumption behind least-squares regression.

## When Not to Assume It

Normal tails are extremely thin &mdash; a 5&sigma; event should essentially never happen. Financial returns, city sizes and word frequencies have far <em>fatter</em> tails, so assuming normality there badly underestimates extreme events. The 2008 financial crisis is the textbook example of trusting a bell curve where none existed.

## Exploration guide

1. <strong>Slide &mu;.</strong> The curve translates; its shape never changes.
2. <strong>Slide &sigma; wider</strong> and watch the peak fall to compensate &mdash; fixed area in action.
3. <strong>Cycle &plusmn;1&sigma;, &plusmn;2&sigma;, &plusmn;3&sigma;</strong> and confirm 68 / 95 / 99.7 for any &mu; and &sigma; you choose.
4. <strong>Sample 20.</strong> The histogram looks nothing like a bell &mdash; small samples are deceptive. Now sample 500 repeatedly and watch it lock on.
5. <strong>Set a custom range beyond 3&sigma;</strong> and read how vanishingly small the probability becomes.

## Where that leaves you

Two parameters describe the entire curve, area equals probability, and 68/95/99.7 holds universally. The central limit theorem is why the shape is so common &mdash; but its thin tails mean it is the wrong model whenever extreme events actually matter.
