title: The Normal Distribution
intro: Two numbers describe the whole curve &mdash; and the central limit theorem explains why it is unavoidable.

## Quick Context

The <strong>normal</strong> (or Gaussian) distribution is the familiar bell curve: symmetric, single-peaked, thin-tailed. It is completely specified by just two numbers &mdash; the mean &mu; sets where it sits, and the standard deviation &sigma; sets how wide it is.

## Two Knobs, Nothing Else

- <strong style="color: var(--c-b)">&mu;</strong> slides the curve left and right without changing its shape.
- <strong style="color: var(--accent-primary)">&sigma;</strong> stretches or squeezes it. Watch the peak <em>drop</em> as you widen it &mdash; the total area must always equal 1, so a wider curve has to be shorter.

That fixed unit area is what makes it a probability density: area under a stretch of the curve <em>is</em> the probability of landing in that range.

## The 68–95–99.7 Rule

These percentages hold for <em>every</em> normal distribution, whatever &mu; and &sigma; are. Cycle the region selector and watch the shaded area match &mdash; the app integrates the curve numerically rather than quoting the constants.

The practical use is spotting anomalies: beyond 3&sigma; you are in the outermost 0.3% of outcomes, which is why quality control and anomaly detection use it as a threshold.

## Z-scores Put Everything on One Scale

A z-score restates a value as "how many standard deviations from the mean". It makes incomparable quantities comparable &mdash; a height and an exam score both become pure position. Setting &mu; = 0 and &sigma; = 1 gives the <strong>standard normal</strong>, which is what every statistical table refers to, and what feature standardisation converts your data into.

## Why It Appears Everywhere

The <strong>central limit theorem</strong>: when you add up many small independent random effects, their total tends toward a normal distribution <em>regardless of how the individual effects were distributed</em>. Height is the sum of many genetic and environmental nudges; measurement error is the sum of many tiny disturbances. Hence bells everywhere.

Press <strong>Sample 500</strong> a few times and watch the histogram converge onto the curve. In machine learning this shows up as weight initialisation (drawn from a normal), Gaussian noise in diffusion models, and the assumption behind least-squares regression.

## When Not to Assume It

Normal tails are extremely thin &mdash; a 5&sigma; event should essentially never happen. Financial returns, city sizes and word frequencies have far <em>fatter</em> tails, so assuming normality there badly underestimates extreme events. The 2008 financial crisis is the textbook example of trusting a bell curve where none existed.

## The shape that keeps appearing

The normal distribution is the symmetric bell curve, defined entirely by two numbers: the mean &mu; (where the peak sits) and the standard deviation &sigma; (how wide it is).

Its most useful property is the empirical rule, which turns &sigma; into a statement about proportions:

|Range|Share of the data|
|---|---|
|&mu; &plusmn; 1&sigma;|68%|
|&mu; &plusmn; 2&sigma;|95%|
|&mu; &plusmn; 3&sigma;|99.7%|

So for adult heights with a mean of 170cm and &sigma; of 10cm: about two thirds of people are between 160 and 180, 95% between 150 and 190, and someone at 200cm is a genuine three-sigma rarity &mdash; about 1 in 700.

That is the practical value of the curve. Two numbers, and you can answer "how unusual is this?" for any value.

## The z-score

A z-score converts any value into "how many standard deviations from the mean":

<p class="mono-font">z = (x &minus; &mu;) / &sigma;</p>

A test score of 85 where the mean is 70 and &sigma; is 10 gives z = 1.5 &mdash; one and a half standard deviations above average, better than about 93% of the group.

Standardising this way makes different scales comparable: a z of 1.5 means the same thing whether the underlying variable is heights, salaries or reaction times. That is exactly what `StandardScaler` does to every column before a distance-based model sees it.

|z|Percentile|
|---|---|
|&minus;2|2.3%|
|&minus;1|15.9%|
|0|50%|
|1|84.1%|
|2|97.7%|
|3|99.9%|

## Why it appears so often: the central limit theorem

The reason the bell curve shows up everywhere is not that nature prefers it. It is a mathematical result: <strong>the sum or average of many independent contributions tends towards a normal distribution, whatever the shape of the individual contributions</strong>.

Roll one die and the outcomes are flat. Roll ten and add them, and the totals form a bell. Human height is the sum of many small genetic and environmental effects; measurement error is the sum of many small disturbances. Both end up normal for the same reason.

Two consequences for practical work:

- <strong>Sample means are normally distributed</strong> even when the underlying data is not, which is why confidence intervals and t-tests work on skewed data given a reasonable sample size.
- <strong>Individual measurements are often not normal.</strong> Incomes, city sizes and response times are famously right-skewed, and treating them as normal produces bad intervals and worse thresholds.

The mistake to avoid is assuming normality rather than checking it. A histogram, or a Q-Q plot, settles it in seconds.

## Where it is used in machine learning

- <strong>Standardisation.</strong> Subtracting the mean and dividing by &sigma; is the standard preprocessing step for linear models, SVMs, KNN and neural networks.
- <strong>Weight initialisation.</strong> Networks start with weights drawn from a normal distribution, scaled by the layer size (He, Xavier).
- <strong>Assumed errors.</strong> Linear regression's inference &mdash; confidence intervals, p-values &mdash; assumes normally distributed residuals. The predictions themselves do not.
- <strong>Gaussian mixtures</strong> model data as several overlapping bells, giving soft cluster membership.
- <strong>Anomaly detection.</strong> Flag anything beyond three sigma, when the normality assumption holds.
- <strong>Noise.</strong> Gaussian noise is the default in augmentation, differential privacy and diffusion models.

2. <strong>Slide &sigma; wider</strong> and watch the peak fall to compensate &mdash; fixed area in action.
3. <strong>Cycle &plusmn;1&sigma;, &plusmn;2&sigma;, &plusmn;3&sigma;</strong> and confirm 68 / 95 / 99.7 for any &mu; and &sigma; you choose.
4. <strong>Sample 20.</strong> The histogram looks nothing like a bell &mdash; small samples are deceptive. Now sample 500 repeatedly and watch it lock on.
5. <strong>Set a custom range beyond 3&sigma;</strong> and read how vanishingly small the probability becomes.

## Where that leaves you

Two parameters describe the entire curve, area equals probability, and 68/95/99.7 holds universally. The central limit theorem is why the shape is so common &mdash; but its thin tails mean it is the wrong model whenever extreme events actually matter.

## When the data is not normal

Assuming a bell curve for skewed data has predictable consequences: thresholds set at three sigma fire far too often, confidence intervals are wrong, and models that assume symmetry systematically under-predict the tail.

Three responses, in order of preference:

<strong>Transform.</strong> A log transform straightens most right-skewed data &mdash; incomes, populations, counts. The Box-Cox and Yeo-Johnson transforms generalise this and can be fitted automatically.

<strong>Use a distribution that fits.</strong> Poisson for counts, exponential for waiting times, log-normal for anything multiplicative, Student's t when the tails are heavier than normal.

<strong>Stop assuming.</strong> Rank-based methods, quantile regression and bootstrapping make no distributional assumption at all. The bootstrap in particular is a general-purpose way to get a confidence interval from data of any shape: resample with replacement a few thousand times and read the interval off the resulting spread.

How to check quickly: plot a histogram, then a Q-Q plot &mdash; if the points fall on the diagonal, normality is a reasonable approximation. Formal tests such as Shapiro-Wilk exist but reject almost any large real dataset, since no real data is exactly normal.

## Two relatives worth knowing

<strong>The log-normal distribution</strong> describes a variable whose <em>logarithm</em> is normal. It arises whenever effects multiply rather than add &mdash; incomes, file sizes, stock prices. It is right-skewed and strictly positive, which fits a great deal of real data that people mistakenly model as normal.

<strong>Student's t distribution</strong> looks like a normal curve with heavier tails. It is what you use for confidence intervals from small samples, where the extra tail weight honestly reflects the uncertainty in estimating &sigma; from few observations. As the sample grows it converges to the normal.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Does my data need to be normal for machine learning?</strong> Mostly no. Trees, forests and boosting do not care. Linear models' <em>inference</em> assumes normal residuals, not normal features. Distance-based models care about scale, which standardisation fixes regardless of shape.

<strong>What is the difference between standardisation and normalisation?</strong> Standardising subtracts the mean and divides by &sigma;, giving mean 0 and &sigma; 1. Normalising usually means rescaling to a fixed range such as [0, 1]. The words are used loosely; say which you mean.

<strong>Is the mean always at the peak?</strong> For a normal distribution the mean, median and mode coincide. For skewed data they separate, and the gap tells you the direction of the skew.

<strong>How many samples until the central limit theorem applies?</strong> The usual rule of thumb is 30, but heavily skewed data needs far more. Simulate it if the answer matters.

<strong>What is a three-sigma event?</strong> A value more than three standard deviations from the mean &mdash; about 0.3% of a normal distribution. In markets and other heavy-tailed settings they happen far more often than the normal model predicts.

<strong>Why do neural network weights start normally distributed?</strong> Because a symmetric, zero-centred spread breaks symmetry between neurons while keeping the initial activations in a sensible range.

## Recap in one screen

- Two numbers, the mean and the standard deviation, describe the whole curve.
- 68%, 95% and 99.7% fall within one, two and three standard deviations.
- A z-score expresses any value as a number of standard deviations, making scales comparable.
- The central limit theorem explains its ubiquity: sums and averages tend to normal.
- Individual measurements are often skewed &mdash; check before assuming, and transform or use a better-fitting distribution.
