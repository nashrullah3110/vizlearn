title: Identity, Inverse and Transpose: A Practical Guide
intro: Do nothing, undo, and flip &mdash; and why only two of those are related.

## Quick Context

A matrix is <a href="matrix_as_transformation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">a transformation</a>. Once you can do something to space, three questions follow at once: what does nothing, what puts it back. And what happens if you read the matrix sideways.

The first two are a pair. The third is a other animal that constantly gets mistaken for the second.

The identity matrix I is the matrix that does nothing: AI = IA = A for each matrix A of compatible size. It is the multiplicative identity, the same role that 1 plays for counts. Its entries are 1 on the diagonal and 0 all over else. In NumPy, `np.eye(n)` creates an n &times; n identity matrix.

The inverse of a matrix A, written A&sup1;, is the matrix that undoes what A does: AA&sup1; = A&sup1;A = I. Not each matrix has an inverse. A matrix that squashes two dimensions into one (a singular matrix, determinant zero) has no inverse, since the information about the collapsed dimension is lost and cannot be recovered. Testing whether a matrix is invertible amounts to checking whether its determinant is nonzero. Or equivalently whether all its eigenvalues are nonzero.

The transpose of A, written A&sup1; or A.T, swaps rows and columns: the item in row i, column j turns the item in row j, column i. Unlike the inverse, the transpose always exists and is trivial to compute. It is not the same step as the inverse — the inverse undoes a transformation, while the transpose reflects the matrix across its diagonal. For orthogonal matrices (rotations and reflections), the two happen to be equal: A&sup1; = A.T. It is a useful special case since it means you can invert a rotation by transposing instead of computing a full inverse.

## The identity

I = [[1, 0], [0, 1]]

Its columns are just where <span class="mono-font">i</span> and <span class="mono-font">j</span> now were. So it moves nothing. It is the 1 of matrix multiplication: <span class="mono-font">AI = IA = A</span> for each A. And the thing an inverse has to give.

## The inverse

<p>A&#8315;&sup1; = (1 / det A) &middot; [[d, &minus;b], [&minus;c, a]]</p><span class="mono-font">A&#8315;&sup1;</span> is the transformation that undoes A: apply one then the other and you are back where you started. It is what <span class="mono-font">A A&#8315;&sup1; = I</span> says. Look at the formula and the whole story of the <a href="determinant.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">determinant</a> falls out of it &mdash; each entry is divided by det A. So a determinant of zero leaves nothing to write.<p>That is not a notational accident. A matrix with zero determinant has flattened the plane onto a line. Two other starting points now sit on top of each other. And no transformation can pull them apart again. The information is gone. So the undo cannot exist.</p><p>A determinant that is merely <em>small</em> is its own kind of trouble: the inverse divides by it, so tiny errors in the input turn enormous errors in the output. That is what an ill-conditioned matrix is, and it is why numerical code solves <span class="mono-font">Ax = b</span> rather than computing <span class="mono-font">A&#8315;&sup1;b</span>.</p>

## The transpose

A&#7488; = [[a, c], [b, d]]

Reflect the matrix across its diagonal: rows turn columns. Geometrically it is not an undo and generally not tied to one &mdash; try it on the plot and the shape does not come back.

Its real job is bookkeeping in products. <span class="mono-font">(AB)&#7488; = B&#7488;A&#7488;</span>, the dot product of two vectors is <span class="mono-font">a&#7488;b</span>. And <span class="mono-font">X&#7488;X</span> is what turns a tall data matrix into the square, symmetric thing that <a href="../machine_learning/linear_regression_with_ols.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">least squares</a> and <a href="covariance_and_correlation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">covariance</a> both need. When you see a stray transpose in a formula, it is almost always there to make two shapes line up.

There is one key case where the two coincide. If A's columns are perpendicular unit vectors &mdash; an <strong>orthogonal</strong> matrix, a rotation or a reflection &mdash; then <span class="mono-font">A&#7488. = A&#8315;&sup1;</span> just. Undoing a rotation costs a transpose instead of a division. It is why numerical ways work so hard to keep things orthogonal.

## Three steps that keep appearing

<strong>The identity matrix `I`</strong> has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. Multiplying by it changes nothing:

<p class="mono-font">AI = IA = A</p>

It is the matrix equivalent of the count 1. And it is what "no transformation" looks like.

<strong>The inverse `A⁻¹`</strong> undoes what A did:

<p class="mono-font">A A&#8315;&sup1; = A&#8315;&sup1; A = I</p>

If A rotates by 30&deg;, its inverse rotates by &minus;30&deg;. If A doubles all, the inverse halves it.

<strong>The transpose `Aᵀ`</strong> flips the matrix across its diagonal, turning rows into columns:

<p class="mono-font">[[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]]&#7488. = [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]]</p>

A 2&times;3 turns a 3&times;2, which is most often just what you need to make a matrix product's shapes line up.

## Not each matrix has an inverse

A matrix is invertible just if its determinant is non-zero. Zero determinant means the transformation collapsed a dimension &mdash; flattened a plane onto a line &mdash; and no step can recover what was lost, since many other inputs now map to the same output.

Such matrices are called <strong>singular</strong>. And the geometric reading is exact: information was destroyed.

For a 2&times;2 matrix the inverse has a closed form:

<p class="mono-font">A = [[a, b], [c, d]]&nbsp;&nbsp;&rarr;&nbsp;&nbsp;A&#8315;&sup1; = (1 / (ad &minus; bc)) [[d, &minus;b], [&minus;c, a]]</p>

The `ad − bc` in the denominator is the determinant. And the division by it is why a zero determinant breaks all.

Beyond 2&times;2, do not compute inverses. Solving `Ax = b` right is faster and far more numerically stable than forming `A⁻¹` and multiplying:

```python
import numpy as np

x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)      # do this
x = np.linalg.inv(A) @ b       # not this - slower and less accurate
```

Still when a matrix is technically invertible, being <em>nearly</em> singular is a real problem: a tiny determinant makes the inverse enormous. And small errors in the input turn large errors in the output. That condition is just what multicollinearity does to a regression's normal equations. And it is why ridge regression &mdash; which adds a small multiple of the identity before inverting &mdash; is so effective.

## The transpose, and the rules that matter

Two identities do most of the work:

<p class="mono-font">(AB)&#7488; = B&#7488;A&#7488;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(A&#7488;)&#7488; = A</p>

The reversal in the first is not a quirk &mdash; it is what makes the shapes valid.

A matrix equal to its own transpose is <strong>symmetric</strong>. And symmetric matrices are the well-behaved ones: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors. And a clean decomposition. Covariance matrices, correlation matrices and Gram matrices are all symmetric. It is why the mathematics of PCA is so tidy.

A matrix whose transpose <em>is</em> its inverse (`QᵀQ = I`) is <strong>orthogonal</strong>. Those represent rotations and reflections &mdash; transformations that preserve lengths and angles &mdash; and they are numerically ideal, since inverting them costs nothing and they never amplify error.

## The three, checked

Each of the three has one setting property. And each is one line to confirm.

```python-run
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[4.0, 7.0], [2.0, 6.0]])
I = np.eye(2)

print("A @ I is A:", np.allclose(A @ I, A))
inv = np.linalg.inv(A)
print()
print("inverse:"); print(inv.round(4))
print("A @ inv:"); print((A @ inv).round(10))
print()
print("transpose swaps rows and columns:")
print(A.T)
print()
print("(A @ B).T == B.T @ A.T - the order reverses:")
B = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [3.0, 2.0]])
print(" ", np.allclose((A @ B).T, B.T @ A.T),
      " but (A@B).T == A.T @ B.T is", np.allclose((A @ B).T, A.T @ B.T))
```

## Try it yourself

1. <strong>Do and undo.</strong> Press <strong>Apply A</strong>, then <strong>Apply A&#8315;&sup1;</strong>. The shape leaves and comes back just. And the computed A A&#8315;&sup1; panel reads the identity.
2. <strong>Break it.</strong> Set c to 1.5 and d to 1.0, leaving a at 1.5 and b at 1.0. Now ad = bc and the determinant is just 0. The shape collapses onto a line, the inverse panel turns red. And the undo button is disabled &mdash; there is nothing to press.
3. <strong>Get close to broken.</strong> Nudge d up to 1.1 so the determinant is 0.15. The inverse exists, and its entries are enormous &mdash; that magnification is just the numerical instability people mean by "ill-conditioned".
4. <strong>Try the transpose as an undo.</strong> Tick <strong>Undo With A&#7488. Instead</strong> and run do-then-undo. The shape does not give, since the transpose was never the undo.
5. <strong>Unless it is.</strong> Set a = 0.6, b = &minus;0.8, c = 0.8, d = 0.6 &mdash; a rotation. Now A&#7488; = A&#8315;&sup1; reads yes. And undoing with the transpose works perfectly.
6. <strong>Find the identity.</strong> a = 1, b = 0, c = 0, d = 1. Apply it as often as you like. Nothing ever moves.

## Worth remembering

The identity is the matrix that changes nothing. And the inverse is the one that undoes A &mdash; set by <span class="mono-font">A A&#8315;&sup1; = I</span> and computed by dividing through by the determinant. It is precisely why a zero determinant means no inverse exists: the transformation destroyed information and no matrix can recover it. A small determinant is the same problem in slow motion. And the reason numerical code solves systems rather than inverting matrices. The transpose just looks like a relative: it flips rows and columns, it exists to make shapes agree in products such as <span class="mono-font">X&#7488;X</span>. And it equals the inverse in just one case &mdash; when the matrix is orthogonal.

## Where they show up

<strong>Solving linear systems.</strong> The normal equation for least squares, `β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy`, uses all three steps in one line: a transpose to form the square matrix, an inverse to solve it. And the identity implicitly as the target of the inversion. When features are collinear, `XᵀX` is near-singular and the coefficients turn unstable &mdash; which is precisely the problem ridge regression fixes by inverting `XᵀX + λI` instead.

<strong>Backpropagation.</strong> The gradient flowing backwards through a layer is multiplied by the transpose of that layer's weight matrix. The forward pass uses W. The backward pass uses Wᵀ. No inverse is ever needed, which is one reason training is feasible.

<strong>Data layout.</strong> Transposing is routine when reconciling "samples as rows" with "features as rows" conventions between libraries. And a surprising count of shape errors are a missing `.T`.

<strong>Orthogonal initialisation.</strong> Initialising a recurrent network's weights with an orthogonal matrix keeps the magnitude of signals stable through many time steps, right addressing exploding and vanishing gradients.

## The pseudo-inverse

When a matrix is not square. Or is singular, the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse `A⁺` gives the best on hand answer: the least-squares solution when the system is overdetermined. And the min-norm solution when it is underdetermined.

```python
x = np.linalg.pinv(A) @ b      # works even when inv(A) does not exist
```

It is computed from the SVD, and it is what `lstsq` uses internally. For a linear regression with more features than samples. Or with perfectly correlated columns, this is what returns a sensible answer where a direct inverse would fail outright.

## Questions people ask

<strong>How do I know if a matrix is invertible?</strong> Non-zero determinant. Or equivalently full rank, or equivalently no zero eigenvalues. `np.linalg.matrix_rank` is the real check.

<strong>Why avoid computing inverses?</strong> `solve` is faster and numerically better. Clearly inverting amplifies rounding error, especially when the matrix is ill-conditioned.

<strong>Is the transpose the same as the inverse?</strong> Just for orthogonal matrices &mdash; and that is just what makes them so convenient.

<strong>What is the condition count?</strong> The ratio of the largest to smallest singular value. A large value means the matrix is nearly singular and solutions will be sensitive to noise.

<strong>Does `A B = A C` imply `B = C`?</strong> Just if A is invertible. Otherwise, no &mdash; cancellation is not a valid move with singular matrices.

<strong>Why does ridge regression add λI?</strong> Since it makes `XᵀX + λI` invertible and better conditioned, still when the features are collinear.

## Recap in one screen

- The identity does nothing. The inverse undoes; the transpose flips rows and columns.
- A matrix is invertible just when its determinant is non-zero &mdash; a singular matrix destroyed information.
- Use `solve`, not `inv`: faster and numerically safer.
- `(AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ`, and symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors.
- Orthogonal matrices satisfy `Qᵀ = Q⁻¹`, preserve lengths. And are the numerically ideal case.
- The pseudo-inverse handles non-square and singular matrices. And is what least-squares solvers use.
