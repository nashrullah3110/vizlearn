title: Identity, Inverse and Transpose: A Practical Guide
intro: Do nothing, undo, and flip &mdash; and why only two of those are related.

## Quick Context

A matrix is <a href="matrix_as_transformation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">a transformation</a>. Once you can do something to space, three questions follow immediately: what does nothing, what puts it back, and what happens if you read the matrix sideways.

The first two are a pair. The third is a different animal that constantly gets mistaken for the second.

## The identity

I = [[1, 0], [0, 1]]

Its columns are exactly where <span class="mono-font">i</span> and <span class="mono-font">j</span> already were, so it moves nothing. It is the 1 of matrix multiplication: <span class="mono-font">AI = IA = A</span> for every A, and the thing an inverse has to produce.

## The inverse

<p>A&#8315;&sup1; = (1 / det A) &middot; [[d, &minus;b], [&minus;c, a]]</p><span class="mono-font">A&#8315;&sup1;</span> is the transformation that undoes A: apply one then the other and you are back where you started, which is what <span class="mono-font">A A&#8315;&sup1; = I</span> says. Look at the formula and the whole story of the <a href="determinant.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">determinant</a> falls out of it &mdash; every entry is divided by det A, so a determinant of zero leaves nothing to write.<p>That is not a notational accident. A matrix with zero determinant has flattened the plane onto a line; two different starting points now sit on top of each other, and no transformation can pull them apart again. The information is gone, so the undo cannot exist.</p><p>A determinant that is merely <em>small</em> is its own kind of trouble: the inverse divides by it, so tiny errors in the input become enormous errors in the output. That is what an ill-conditioned matrix is, and it is why numerical code solves <span class="mono-font">Ax = b</span> rather than computing <span class="mono-font">A&#8315;&sup1;b</span>.</p>

## The transpose

A&#7488; = [[a, c], [b, d]]

Reflect the matrix across its diagonal: rows become columns. Geometrically it is not an undo and generally not related to one &mdash; try it on the plot and the shape does not come back.

Its real job is bookkeeping in products. <span class="mono-font">(AB)&#7488; = B&#7488;A&#7488;</span>, the dot product of two vectors is <span class="mono-font">a&#7488;b</span>, and <span class="mono-font">X&#7488;X</span> is what turns a tall data matrix into the square, symmetric thing that <a href="../machine_learning/linear_regression_with_ols.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">least squares</a> and <a href="covariance_and_correlation.html" style="color: var(--accent-primary)">covariance</a> both need. When you see a stray transpose in a formula, it is almost always there to make two shapes line up.

There is one important case where the two coincide. If A's columns are perpendicular unit vectors &mdash; an <strong>orthogonal</strong> matrix, a rotation or a reflection &mdash; then <span class="mono-font">A&#7488; = A&#8315;&sup1;</span> exactly. Undoing a rotation costs a transpose instead of a division, which is why numerical methods work so hard to keep things orthogonal.

## Try it yourself

1. <strong>Do and undo.</strong> Press <strong>Apply A</strong>, then <strong>Apply A&#8315;&sup1;</strong>. The shape leaves and comes back exactly, and the computed A A&#8315;&sup1; panel reads the identity.
2. <strong>Break it.</strong> Set c to 1.5 and d to 1.0, leaving a at 1.5 and b at 1.0. Now ad = bc and the determinant is exactly 0. The shape collapses onto a line, the inverse panel turns red, and the undo button is disabled &mdash; there is nothing to press.
3. <strong>Get close to broken.</strong> Nudge d up to 1.1 so the determinant is 0.15. The inverse exists, and its entries are enormous &mdash; that magnification is exactly the numerical instability people mean by "ill-conditioned".
4. <strong>Try the transpose as an undo.</strong> Tick <strong>Undo With A&#7488; Instead</strong> and run do-then-undo. The shape does not return, because the transpose was never the undo.
5. <strong>Unless it is.</strong> Set a = 0.6, b = &minus;0.8, c = 0.8, d = 0.6 &mdash; a rotation. Now A&#7488; = A&#8315;&sup1; reads yes, and undoing with the transpose works perfectly.
6. <strong>Find the identity.</strong> a = 1, b = 0, c = 0, d = 1. Apply it as often as you like; nothing ever moves.

## Worth remembering

The identity is the matrix that changes nothing, and the inverse is the one that undoes A &mdash; defined by <span class="mono-font">A A&#8315;&sup1; = I</span> and computed by dividing through by the determinant, which is precisely why a zero determinant means no inverse exists: the transformation destroyed information and no matrix can recover it. A small determinant is the same problem in slow motion, and the reason numerical code solves systems rather than inverting matrices. The transpose only looks like a relative: it flips rows and columns, it exists to make shapes agree in products such as <span class="mono-font">X&#7488;X</span>, and it equals the inverse in exactly one case &mdash; when the matrix is orthogonal.
