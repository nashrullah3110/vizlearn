title: Taylor Series
intro: A Taylor polynomial approximates a smooth function near a chosen point using its derivatives there.

## Build the approximation

Around x = a, the degree-n Taylor polynomial is a sum of derivative terms:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="Taylor polynomial sum from k zero to n of f kth derivative at a times x minus a to k divided by k factorial">Tₙ(x) = Σₖ₌₀ⁿ f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)(x−a)ᵏ/k!</div>

For eˣ around a = 0, every derivative equals 1, so T₃(x) = 1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6. At x = 0.1 this gives 1.10517, close to e^0.1 ≈ 1.10517. Near the expansion point, higher-degree terms often improve the approximation.

## Local does not mean universal

The error depends on the function, expansion point, and distance from that point. A Taylor series may converge only within a limited interval; a finite polynomial can become inaccurate far away. Use the remainder bound or compare with the original function over the range where the approximation will be used.

Optimisers use first- and second-order Taylor approximations to estimate how a loss changes after a parameter step. Newton's method uses local curvature; see [Jacobians and Hessians](jacobian_and_hessian.html). The derivative definition is the first-order version; see [derivatives and slope](derivatives_and_slope.html).

## Why the expansion point matters

A Taylor polynomial matches the function's value and several derivatives at a chosen point a. For eˣ near zero, T₃(x)=1+x+x²/2+x³/6. At x=0.1 the cubic is close to e^0.1; at a point farther from zero, omitted higher-order terms matter more. Expanding around a different point can give a much better approximation near that location. A Taylor polynomial is local information expressed as a polynomial, not automatically an exact identity over the whole real line.

The remainder measures the approximation error. If the next derivative is bounded by M between a and x, the degree-n error is at most M|x−a|ⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)! under the usual Taylor theorem conditions. This bound depends on the interval, not just the degree. Some functions have a Taylor series that converges only within a radius; others have a convergent series that does not equal the function outside its convergence interval. Use the approximation only over a range where error is controlled. Optimisers use first- and second-order expansions as local models, then recompute as the parameters move.

## Try this above

Increase the polynomial degree and move x farther from the expansion point. Compare the approximation error near and far from that point.

For sin(x) around zero, the degree-three polynomial is x−x³/6. At x=0.2 it closely approximates sin(0.2), because the neglected terms are small near the expansion point. At x=2, the same low-degree polynomial is much less accurate. The polynomial degree is therefore not a stand-alone accuracy guarantee; the distance from a and the size of higher derivatives both matter. In numerical work, test the approximation over the actual operating range rather than only at the point where derivatives were matched.

A first-order Taylor polynomial is the tangent line; a second-order polynomial adds curvature. For a small parameter update Δ, a loss can be approximated as f(x+Δ)≈f(x)+∇f(x)ᵀΔ+½ΔᵀHΔ. Gradient methods use the linear term, while Newton-style methods use both. The approximation is only local, so an optimiser may need a line search or trust region to avoid taking a step where the polynomial no longer predicts the actual function.
