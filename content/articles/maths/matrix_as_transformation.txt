title: A Matrix as a Transformation
intro: The columns of a matrix show where the coordinate directions go, which explains matrix–vector multiplication.

## Read the columns

A linear map preserves sums and scaling. In two dimensions, knowing where the basis vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) go determines where every vector goes. Those two destination vectors are the columns of the matrix:

<div class="vz-math-block" role="math" aria-label="A times x y equals x times the first column plus y times the second column">A(x, y) = x·A(1,0) + y·A(0,1)</div>

For A = [[2, 1], [0, 1]], the basis directions map to (2, 0) and (1, 1). Thus A(3, 2) = 3(2, 0) + 2(1, 1) = (8, 2). The columns are not just a storage convention; they encode the transformation.

## What changes and what survives

Matrices can rotate, shear, stretch, reflect, or collapse space. A singular matrix loses a direction, so different inputs can map to the same output and no inverse can recover the original. For square matrices, determinant zero identifies singularity; rank counts the surviving independent directions. See [determinants](determinant.html), [rank](rank_and_linear_independence.html), and [eigenvectors](eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors.html).

Applying B and then A gives ABx: the rightmost matrix acts first on column vectors. Transformations generally do not commute, so reversing their order changes the result. A neural-network layer uses a linear map plus a bias; its activation function adds the nonlinearity.
