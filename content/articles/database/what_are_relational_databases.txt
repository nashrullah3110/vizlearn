title: Relational Databases: Small Tables, Strong Links
intro: Fifty years on, the relational model is still the default way to store business data. This is why.

## The problem it solves

A <strong>relational database</strong> stores data in <strong>tables</strong> of rows and columns, where every table describes one kind of thing &mdash; customers, orders, products &mdash; and tables are connected by shared key values rather than by nesting data inside each other.

## Keys Are the Whole Trick

- <strong class="pk">Primary key</strong> &mdash; a column whose value uniquely identifies a row. No duplicates, never NULL. It is the row's permanent name.
- <strong class="fk">Foreign key</strong> &mdash; a column holding the primary key of a row in <em>another</em> table. This is the link, and the database <em>enforces</em> it: you cannot create an order for customer 99 if no such customer exists.

That enforcement is called <strong>referential integrity</strong>, and it is a guarantee the database makes on your behalf &mdash; not something your application code has to remember.

## Why Split the Data at All?

<p>Switch this lab to <strong>one flat table</strong> and look at the red cells. The customer's name and city now repeat on every order they ever placed. That redundancy causes three classic problems:</p><ul><li><strong>Update anomaly</strong> &mdash; changing a customer's name means finding and editing every copy. Miss one and your data now contradicts itself. Press the rename button in both models and compare the counts.</li><li><strong>Insertion anomaly</strong> &mdash; you cannot record a new customer until they place an order, because there is nowhere to put them.</li><li><strong>Deletion anomaly</strong> &mdash; deleting a customer's only order erases the customer entirely.</li></ul><strong>Normalisation</strong> is the process of splitting tables until each fact is stored exactly once. The cost is that answering a question often requires a <span class="mono-font">JOIN</span> &mdash; and that trade, correctness for a little query effort, is the central bargain of the relational model.

## ACID: The Other Half of the Promise

Relational systems also guarantee that transactions are <strong>Atomic</strong> (all steps or none), <strong>Consistent</strong> (constraints always hold), <strong>Isolated</strong> (concurrent transactions do not corrupt each other) and <strong>Durable</strong> (committed data survives a crash). If you are moving money between accounts, this is not a nice-to-have.

## Exploration guide

1. <strong>Click a customer row.</strong> Their orders light up in the orders table &mdash; that is the foreign key doing its job.
2. <strong>Click an order.</strong> The single product it references is highlighted. One value, one link, no ambiguity.
3. <strong>Switch to the flat table</strong> and watch the cell count rise while the number of tables drops to one. Every red cell is a duplicated fact.
4. <strong>Run the rename in both models.</strong> Normalised: one cell changes. Flat: several &mdash; and every one is an opportunity to get it wrong.

## Summing up

Store each fact once, identify it with a primary key, and reference it with a foreign key. The database then enforces the relationships for you. Flattening everything looks simpler until the first time you have to change something.
