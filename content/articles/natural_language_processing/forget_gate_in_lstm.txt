title: The Forget Gate: Deciding What to Erase
intro: Before an LSTM writes anything new, it decides what of the past is still worth keeping. That single decision is what makes long-term memory possible.

## What the Gate Actually Is

The Forget Gate is a small neural layer with a <strong>sigmoid</strong> output. It reads the merged vector $[H_{t-1}, X_t]$ and produces one number between 0 and 1 <em>for every slot in the cell state</em>:

$$f_t = \sigma(W_f \cdot [H_{t-1}, X_t] + b_f)$$

Those numbers are then multiplied element-by-element into the old memory: $f_t \odot C_{t-1}$. A slot that receives <strong>1</strong> is copied forward perfectly. A slot that receives <strong>0</strong> is erased. Everything in between is partial fading.

## It Is a Dimmer, Not a Switch

The word "gate" suggests open or closed, but the sigmoid makes it a <strong>dimmer per dimension</strong>. In a real trained LSTM the forget vector might look like $[0.98, 0.03, 0.71, 0.99, \dots]$ &mdash; the cell holds on to some facts with near-perfect fidelity while dumping others in the same time step.

This is the crucial difference from a plain RNN. An RNN squashes its <em>entire</em> state through $\tanh$ at every step, so everything decays together. The LSTM decides <em>slot by slot</em>, so a subject noun can survive twenty words while a comma is discarded immediately.

## Forgetting Compounds — and That Is the Whole Story

Memory does not decay once, it decays <em>repeatedly</em>. If the gate emits roughly the same value $f$ each step, the fraction of the original signal still present after $n$ steps is:

$$\text{survival} = f^{\,n}$$

Because this is exponential, small differences in $f$ produce enormous differences in reach. Play with the slider in the <strong>Forget Gate Lab</strong> and watch the two survival bars:

- $f = 0.5$ &mdash; after only 5 steps just <strong>3%</strong> remains. This is effectively short-term memory.
- $f = 0.9$ &mdash; after 20 steps about <strong>12%</strong> survives. Usable long-range context.
- $f = 1.0$ &mdash; the memory is <strong>perfectly preserved</strong>, no matter how many steps pass.

That last case is the reason LSTMs defeat the vanishing gradient. When $f_t \approx 1$, the update becomes $C_t \approx C_{t-1} + \text{something}$ &mdash; pure addition. Gradients flowing backwards along this path are multiplied by $f_t \approx 1$ instead of being squashed, so they survive the trip.

## A Practitioner's Trick: Positive Forget Bias

At initialisation the weights are near zero, so $f_t \approx \sigma(0) = 0.5$ &mdash; the network starts out forgetting half its memory every single step and struggles to learn anything long-range. The standard fix is to initialise the forget-gate bias $b_f$ to <strong>+1 or +2</strong>, which pushes the gate to roughly 0.73&ndash;0.88 from the start. The cell begins life inclined to remember, and learns what to discard from there.

## In one line

The Forget Gate is the only component that can <em>remove</em> information from an LSTM's long-term memory, and it does so multiplicatively and permanently. Keeping it near 1 turns the cell state into a protected highway across time; letting it drift toward 0 collapses the LSTM back into a forgetful RNN.
