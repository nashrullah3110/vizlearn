title: Backpropagation Through Time, Step by Step
intro: How gradient descent trains a network that contains a loop.

## The Problem With a Loop

Backpropagation works on feed-forward graphs — signals flow one way, gradients flow back the same way. An RNN's feedback loop breaks that picture. The fix, <strong>Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)</strong>, is beautifully blunt: <em>unroll</em> the loop into T copies of the cell, one per timestep, and the loop becomes an ordinary (deep) feed-forward chain that standard backprop can handle.

## Forward, Then Backward

- <strong>Forward pass:</strong> tokens enter left to right; each cell computes h&#8348; from x&#8348; and h&#8348;&#8331;&#8321;; the loss L is measured at the end.
- <strong>Backward pass:</strong> the gradient &part;L/&part;h starts at the loss and travels right to left. Crossing each cell multiplies it by the local derivative &part;h&#8348;/&part;h&#8348;&#8331;&#8321; — the chain rule, applied once per timestep.
- <strong>Weight update:</strong> because every unrolled copy shares the same W and U, their gradient contributions from all timesteps are <em>summed</em> before the single weight update.

## Unrolling the loop

A recurrent network is a loop, and gradients cannot flow through a loop directly. So training unrolls it: a 50-step sequence becomes a 50-layer feed-forward network in which every layer shares the same weight matrix.

Ordinary backpropagation then applies to that unrolled graph. The only special feature is what happens to the shared weights.

<strong>Each weight's gradient is the sum of its gradients from every time step</strong>, because it was used at all of them:

<p class="mono-font">&part;L/&part;W = &Sigma;&#7511; &part;L&#7511;/&part;W</p>

That summation is the entire difference between BPTT and standard backpropagation. Everything else &mdash; the chain rule, the storage of activations, the update &mdash; is the same.

Two consequences follow directly. Memory grows with sequence length, because every step's activations must be kept for the backward pass. And the gradient for early steps is a long product of factors, which is where vanishing and exploding gradients come from.

## Truncated BPTT

Backpropagating through a 10,000-step sequence is impossible: the memory required is proportional to the length.

Truncated BPTT bounds it by propagating only k steps back, then detaching the hidden state and continuing forward:

```python
hidden = None
for chunk in chunks_of(sequence, size=50):        # k = 50
    out, hidden = model(chunk, hidden)
    loss = criterion(out, targets_for(chunk))
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    hidden = tuple(h.detach() for h in hidden)     # cut the gradient path
```

The `detach()` is the whole mechanism. The hidden state continues forward, carrying information indefinitely, while the <em>gradient</em> stops at the chunk boundary.

The trade is explicit: the model can use context from arbitrarily far back in the forward direction, and it cannot <em>learn</em> dependencies longer than k, because no gradient connects them. Typical k is 20&ndash;100 &mdash; long enough for useful structure, short enough to be affordable.

Forgetting to detach is a real bug: the graph grows without bound, memory climbs every step, and eventually the process is killed.

## Cost, and where it binds

|Aspect|Scaling|
|---|---|
|Forward compute|Linear in sequence length|
|Backward compute|Linear in sequence length|
|Activation memory|Linear in sequence length|
|Parallelism across steps|None &mdash; strictly sequential|

The last row is the one that mattered historically. Because step t needs `hₜ₋₁`, the steps cannot be computed simultaneously, so a GPU's thousands of cores sit mostly idle and training time scales with length.

That is a hardware-utilisation limitation rather than a quality one, and gating cannot fix it. It is the main reason transformers &mdash; where every position is computed in parallel &mdash; displaced recurrent networks for large-scale work, independent of any argument about long-range dependencies.

## Exploration guide

1. <strong>Run the forward pass.</strong> Cells light up green in sequence, ending at the loss node — this is the network "reading" the sentence.
2. <strong>Backpropagate.</strong> Amber arrows sweep right to left. Watch the chain-rule product in the sidebar: 1.0, then 0.7, then 0.49... each timestep multiplies again.
3. <strong>Set T = 7 and repeat.</strong> By the time the gradient reaches t = 1 it is 0.7&#8310; &asymp; 0.12 — barely a whisper. The bar chart makes the decay unmistakable. This shrinkage is the seed of the vanishing gradient problem, covered next.

## Worth remembering

BPTT = unroll the loop, backpropagate through the chain, sum the shared-weight gradients. It makes RNNs trainable — but it also chains together T multiplications, and a long product of numbers below 1 races toward zero. That arithmetic inevitability is the vanishing gradient problem.

## Practical notes

<strong>Clip gradients.</strong> The summation over time steps plus the product of factors makes large gradients common. `clip_grad_norm_` with a threshold of 1&ndash;5 is standard, and omitting it eventually produces a `NaN` run.

<strong>Pack variable-length sequences.</strong> `pack_padded_sequence` stops the recurrence at each sequence's real end. Without it, gradients flow through padding steps and the final hidden state summarises the padding rather than the sentence.

<strong>Watch memory with sequence length.</strong> Doubling the sequence doubles the stored activations. If training fits at 200 steps and fails at 400, this is why &mdash; truncate, or reduce the batch size.

<strong>Detach the carried state</strong> between chunks in stateful training, or the graph never gets freed.

<strong>Consider gradient checkpointing</strong> for long sequences: store activations only every few steps and recompute the rest during the backward pass, trading about 30% more compute for a large memory saving.

## How it differs from feed-forward backpropagation

|&nbsp;|Feed-forward|Through time|
|---|---|---|
|Weights|Distinct per layer|Shared across steps|
|Gradient for a weight|From one place|Summed over all steps|
|Depth|Fixed by architecture|Set by sequence length|
|Memory|Fixed|Grows with sequence length|
|Parallelism|Across the batch|Across the batch only|

The shared weights are what create both the summation and the long product. A network with distinct weights per step would have neither problem &mdash; and would need a separate parameter set for every possible sequence length, which is exactly what parameter sharing exists to avoid.

## Questions people ask

<strong>Why is it called "through time"?</strong> Because the unrolled layers correspond to time steps rather than to distinct network layers.

<strong>What value of k should I use?</strong> 20&ndash;100 typically. Long enough to cover the dependencies you care about, short enough to fit in memory.

<strong>Does truncation hurt?</strong> It prevents learning dependencies longer than k. The forward state still carries information further, so it is a limit on learning rather than on use.

<strong>Do transformers need BPTT?</strong> No &mdash; there is no recurrence, so ordinary backpropagation applies and all positions are computed in parallel.

<strong>Why does my memory grow every batch?</strong> A carried hidden state that was never detached, so the graph accumulates.

<strong>Is gradient clipping always needed?</strong> For recurrent models, effectively yes.

## Recap in one screen

- BPTT unrolls the recurrence into a deep network sharing one weight matrix, then backpropagates normally.
- A shared weight's gradient is the sum of its contributions from every time step.
- Memory and compute grow linearly with sequence length, and the steps cannot be parallelised.
- Truncated BPTT bounds cost by detaching the state every k steps &mdash; and caps what can be learned at k.
- Clip gradients, pack variable-length batches, and always detach carried state.
