intro: Learn how to break down text into meaningful units for NLP models.

## What is Tokenization?

Tokenization is the basic first step in any Natural Language Handling (NLP) pipeline. It's the process of breaking down a stream of raw text into smaller, meaningful units called <strong>tokens</strong>. These tokens can be words, characters, or sub-word units. It depends on the chosen plan. For a computer to understand human language, it must first dissect it into these discrete pieces, much like how we learn to read by first finding single words. Three plans dominate. Word-level tokenization splits on whitespace and punctuation, giving familiar units but a vocabulary that can run into the millions. And any word missing from it turns an unknown token. Character-level tokenization uses a tiny vocabulary (the alphabet) but gives very long sequences that the model must reassemble into meaning. Subword tokenization ([BPE](../gen_ai/byte_pair_encoding_tokenizer.html), WordPiece, SentencePiece) splits rare words into frequent pieces while keeping common words whole, balancing vocabulary size against sequence length. GPT uses BPE; BERT uses WordPiece. The choice of tokenizer decides the model's vocabulary, its max sequence length, and how it handles unseen words &mdash; making it one of the most consequential decisions in an NLP pipeline, even though it receives far less attention than the model design. This page tokenizes a sample sentence with all three plans, compares the coming token counts and vocabulary sizes, and shows why subword methods have turn the default.

## 1. Why is Tokenization So Key?

Imagine trying to understand a sentence by looking at it as one smooth string of letters. It would be nearly not doable. Tokenization gives the structure that machines need. By turning a sentence like "NLP is fascinating!" into tokens such as <code>["NLP", "is", "fascinating", "!"]</code>, we create a list of items that a model can count, study, and assign meaning to. This process is the gateway to almost each NLP task, including sentiment review, machine translation, and text summarization.

## 2. Exploring Other Tokenization Methods

There's no single "best" way to tokenize text; the right method depends on the task and the language. The interactive visualizer above lets you test with the most common plans. This page covers them:

- <strong>Whitespace Tokenization:</strong> This is the simplest method. It splits the text based on empty spaces. While fast and straightforward, it's often too basic. For case, it would treat "New York" as two separate tokens, <code>["New", "York"]</code>, and might fail to separate a word from punctuation, like in <code>"fun!"</code>.
- <strong>Word (Regex) Tokenization:</strong> A more complex approach that uses regular terms (regex) to define what constitutes a word. This method is much better at handling punctuation and special cases. For instance, it can correctly separate <code>"fun!"</code> into <code>["fun", "!"]</code> and can be configured to handle hyphenated words or contractions like "don't".
- <strong>Sentence Tokenization:</strong> Instead of words, this method splits the text into single sentences. It finds sentence edges using delimiters like periods (.), question marks (?), and exclamation marks (!). This is a key preprocessing step for tasks that need to understand the context of a full sentence, such as document summarization.
- <strong>Character Tokenization:</strong> This method breaks the text down to its most granular level: single characters. The sentence "Hello" turns <code>["H", "e", "l", "l", "o"]</code>. This approach is useful for languages without clear word edges or for tasks like spell-checking and text generation, as it creates a very small, manageable vocabulary.

## Cutting text into pieces a model can count

A model cannot consume a string. It consumes integers, each pointing at a row of an embedding table. Tokenisation is the step that decides what those integers stand for. And the choice shapes all of it downstream.

Three levels are doable:

|Level|"unhappiness" becomes|Vocabulary size|
|---|---|---|
|Character|u, n, h, a, p, ...|~100|
|Word|unhappiness|100,000+|
|Subword|un, happi, ness|30,000&ndash;100,000|

<strong>Character-level</strong> needs a tiny vocabulary and never meets an unknown symbol, but sequences turn very long and the model has to learn spelling before it can learn meaning.

<strong>Word-level</strong> gives short sequences and readable tokens, and it breaks on a thing unseen. A vocabulary built from training text will not hold "unhappiness" if only "happiness" appeared. So the word turns `<UNK>` and its meaning is lost fully. Morphologically rich languages make this far worse.

<strong>Subword</strong> is the compromise all of it modern uses. Common words stay whole; rare words split into meaningful fragments. Nothing is ever unknown, because the vocabulary includes the single characters as a fallback.

## How byte-pair encoding builds a vocabulary

BPE is the algorithm behind GPT's tokeniser, and it is simpler than its reputation.

1. Start with each character as a token.
2. Count all next pairs in the training corpus.
3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token.
4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size.

Run it on English text and the early merges are `t`+`h` → `th`. Then `th`+`e` → `the`. Frequent sequences turn single tokens; rare ones stay as fragments. The result is a vocabulary that spends its budget where the text really is.

The result worth knowing: <strong>token count is not word count</strong>. English averages roughly 1.3 tokens per word. Code, unusual names, other scripts and emoji use far more. A Japanese sentence may cost three or four times as many tokens as its English translation. This is a real cost and latency gap when calling an API.

WordPiece (BERT) and SentencePiece (T5, Llama) are variants of the same idea. SentencePiece is notable for treating the input as a raw byte stream with no pre-tokenisation step. This means it needs no language-set whitespace rules.

## What tokenisation decides for you

A few model behaviours trace right back to this step.

<strong>Why models are bad at spelling and character counting.</strong> "strawberry" may be three tokens, none of which is a letter. Asking how many r's it holds is asking the model to reason about a thing it cannot see.

<strong>Why numbers are handled inconsistently.</strong> "1234" might be one token or four. It depends on the tokeniser. So arithmetic is learned over an inconsistent form.

<strong>Why context limits are in tokens.</strong> A 128,000-token window is roughly 96,000 English words. And much fewer if the text is code or non-Latin script.

<strong>Why the tokeniser must ship with the model.</strong> Token id 1,547 means one thing to one tokeniser and a thing else to one more. A mismatch gives fluent nonsense, not an error.

## Advanced Tokenization: Sub-word Methods

Modern NLP models like BERT and GPT use more advanced methods called <strong>sub-word tokenization</strong> (e.g., Byte-Pair Encoding or WordPiece). These methods break down rare words into smaller, known sub-word units. For case, "tokenization" might turn <code>["token", "##ization"]</code>. This allows the model to handle any word it hits, even if it wasn't in the training data. This prevents the "unknown token" problem and maintaining a manageable vocabulary size.

## In code

```python
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")

tok.tokenize("Tokenisation isn't trivial.")
# ['token', '##isation', 'isn', "'", 't', 'trivial', '.']

ids = tok("Tokenisation isn't trivial.", return_tensors="pt")
ids["input_ids"]          # includes [CLS] and [SEP] automatically
tok.decode(ids["input_ids"][0])
```

The `##` prefix marks a follow-up of the last word, which is how WordPiece distinguishes "token" at the start of a word from the same letters inside one.

Special tokens matter and are easy to forget: `[CLS]` and `[SEP]` for BERT, `<s>` and `</s>` for others, plus `[PAD]` for batching and `[MASK]` for masked-language training. Using the tokeniser's `__call__` adds them correctly; calling `tokenize()` alone does not.

For classical NLP the simpler tools still apply:

```python
import re
re.findall(r"\b\w+\b", text.lower())     # crude word tokens
```

Splitting on whitespace looks adequate and is not: "don't", "state-of-the-art", "U.S.A." and "€19.99" all need decisions. NLTK's and spaCy's tokenisers encode those decisions; a regular term encodes your assumptions.

## Padding, truncation and attention masks

Models take fixed-size batches, so sequences of other lengths must be equalised.

<strong>Padding</strong> appends a filler token to short sequences. The <strong>attention mask</strong> is what tells the model to ignore those spots. Without it, the model attends to padding as if it were content. And the results degrade for no visible reason.

<strong>Truncation</strong> cuts sequences that exceed the limit. Which end you cut matters: for classification the start of a document is often most informative, for question answering the apt passage may be anywhere, and blind truncation silently discards the answer.

```python
batch = tok(texts, padding=True, truncation=True, max_length=512, return_tensors="pt")
model(**batch)          # attention_mask is included in batch
```

Padding to the longest sequence in the batch rather than to a fixed max &mdash; dynamic padding &mdash; is meaningfully faster when lengths vary.

Building on the same setup, the next block shows the left cases.

```python-run
import re
from collections import Counter

def pair_counts(splits, vocab):
    c = Counter()
    for w, freq in vocab.items():
        sym = splits[w]
        for i in range(len(sym) - 1):
            c[(sym[i], sym[i + 1])] += freq
    return c
print("done")
```

The below block covers the left steps.

```python-run
import re
from collections import Counter

merges = []
print("%6s %18s %8s %s" % ("step", "merged pair", "count", "example word after"))
for step in range(10):
    counts = pair_counts(splits, vocab)
    if not counts:
        break
    best, n = counts.most_common(1)[0]
    merges.append(best)
    for w in splits:
        sym, out, i = splits[w], [], 0
        while i < len(sym):
            if i < len(sym) - 1 and (sym[i], sym[i + 1]) == best:
                out.append(sym[i] + sym[i + 1]); i += 2
            else:
                out.append(sym[i]); i += 1
        splits[w] = out
    print("%6d %18s %8d %s"
          % (step + 1, "%s + %s" % best, n, " ".join(splits["newest"])))
print()
print("   the final splits:")
for w in ("low", "lower", "newest", "widest", "lowest"):
    print("      %-8s -> %s" % (w, " ".join(splits[w])))
print()
print("   'est</w>' became a single token because it appeared often. that is")
print("   the whole algorithm: repeatedly merge the commonest adjacent pair.")
print("   nothing about suffixes was programmed in -- it fell out of counting.")
print()
```

The next block goes on with the left demonstrations.

```python-run
import re
from collections import Counter

def bpe_encode(word, merges):
    sym = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for a, b in merges:
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(sym):
            if i < len(sym) - 1 and sym[i] == a and sym[i + 1] == b:
                out.append(a + b); i += 2
            else:
                out.append(sym[i]); i += 1
        sym = out
    return sym

print("   applied to words the tokeniser has never seen:")
for w in ("lowest", "newer", "wildest", "slowness"):
    print("      %-10s -> %s" % (w, bpe_encode(w, merges)))
print("   nothing became <UNK>. an unknown word decomposes into pieces the")
print("   model does know, which is the property that makes subword")
print("   tokenisation the default.")
print()
```

## Questions people ask

<strong>How many tokens is a word?</strong> About 1.3 for English prose, more for code, names and non-Latin scripts. Count with the actual tokeniser rather than estimating.

<strong>Should I lowercase before tokenising?</strong> Only if the tokeniser is uncased. Modern subword tokenisers often handle case themselves, and lowercasing first destroys data they were trained to use.

<strong>Can I use a other tokeniser than the model was trained with?</strong> No. The ids would mean the wrong things.

<strong>Why does my model see `<UNK>` all over?</strong> A word-level vocabulary built from too little text. Use subword tokenisation.

<strong>Do I still need stemming and stop-word cutting?</strong> For classical bag-of-words models, sometimes. For transformers, no &mdash; they use the full form and the function words carry syntax.

<strong>What is the vocabulary size trade-off?</strong> Larger means shorter sequences and a bigger embedding table; smaller means longer sequences and more fragmenting. 30,000&ndash;100,000 is the usual range.

## Recap in one screen

- Tokenisation turns text into integer ids; the choice of unit shapes all of it after it.
- Character level is reliable and long; word level is short and breaks on unseen words; subword is the standard compromise.
- BPE repeatedly merges the most frequent next pair, so common words stay whole and rare ones fragment.
- Token count is not word count, and it varies by language and content type.
- Pad with an attention mask, truncate on purpose, and always ship the tokeniser with the model.
